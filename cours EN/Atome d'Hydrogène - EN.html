The hydrogen atom

Energy levels, orbitals and spectral lines

1 The Coulomb model

A hydrogen atom contains one electron and one proton. Their Coulomb attraction produces bound states. We will derive their energies, describe their spatial wave functions and connect the energy differences to spectral lines.

We consider a point nucleus of charge \(+Ze\) and mass \(m_N\), with one electron of charge \(-e\) and mass \(m_e\). The model is non-relativistic and has no external field. We first leave out spin and nuclear structure. Hydrogen has \(Z=1\); the same calculation applies to one-electron ions such as \(\mathrm{He}^+\) and \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\).

1.1 Separating the center of mass

Let \(\mathbf r_e\) and \(\mathbf r_N\) be the positions of the electron and the nucleus. The two-body Hamiltonian is

\[ \widehat H =-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla_e^2 -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_N}\nabla_N^2 -\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0|\mathbf r_e-\mathbf r_N|}. \tag{1}\]

Both particles move. To separate the motion of the whole atom from its internal motion, introduce the center-of-mass position

\[ \mathbf R =\frac{m_e\mathbf r_e+m_N\mathbf r_N}{m_e+m_N}, \]

and the relative coordinate

\[ \mathbf r=\mathbf r_e-\mathbf r_N. \]

With the total mass \(M=m_e+m_N\) and the reduced mass

\[ \mu=\frac{m_em_N}{m_e+m_N}, \tag{2}\]

the chain rule gives

\[ \nabla_e=\frac{m_e}{M}\nabla_R+\nabla_r, \qquad \nabla_N=\frac{m_N}{M}\nabla_R-\nabla_r. \]

On squaring and adding the two kinetic terms, the mixed derivatives cancel:

\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla_e^2 -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_N}\nabla_N^2 =-\frac{\hbar^2}{2M}\nabla_R^2 -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla_r^2. \]

The Hamiltonian therefore becomes

\[ \widehat H =\underbrace{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2M}\nabla_R^2}_{\widehat H_{\mathrm{CM}}} +\underbrace{\left( -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla_r^2 -\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r} \right)}_{\widehat H_{\mathrm{rel}}}. \tag{3}\]

The center of mass behaves like a free particle. The internal motion is equivalent to that of a particle of mass \(\mu\) in the potential

\[ V(r)=-\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}. \tag{4}\]

Since \(m_N\gg m_e\), the fixed-nucleus approximation \(\mu\simeq m_e\) is often sufficient. Keeping \(\mu\) accounts for the leading effect of nuclear motion on the spectrum.

1.2 Natural scales and atomic units

Confining the electron to a region of size \(a\) gives a momentum of order \(\hbar/a\). A simple estimate of its energy is

\[ E(a)\sim\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu a^2} -\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a}. \]

The positive term grows faster as \(a\to0\) than the attraction. Minimizing this estimate gives \(a\sim a_\mu/Z\), where

\[ a_\mu=\frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2}{\mu e^2}. \tag{5}\]

The length \(a_\mu\) is the Bohr radius corrected for nuclear motion. With \(\mu=m_e\), it becomes

\[ a_0=5.29\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm m. \]

The associated energy scale is the reduced Hartree

\[ E_{\mathrm h}^{(\mu)} =\frac{\mu e^4}{(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2\hbar^2}. \]

In atomic units, we set \(\hbar=e=4\pi\varepsilon_0=m_e=1\). For an infinitely heavy nucleus, the Hamiltonian becomes

\[ \widehat H=-\frac12\nabla^2-\frac{Z}{r}. \tag{6}\]

The radial plots use \(a_0\) for lengths and Hartree for energies, with a fixed nucleus. Energy diagrams also show electronvolts. The spectral-line plot includes the hydrogen reduced mass.

NoteFrom spectral lines to wave functions

Balmer found a formula for hydrogen’s visible lines in 1885. Bohr reproduced the spectrum with quantized orbits in 1913. Schrödinger’s equation later gave the same leading energies from wave functions and their boundary conditions.

1.3 Bound states and the continuum

The potential tends to \(-\infty\) as \(r\to0\) and to zero as \(r\to\infty\). Bound states have \(E<0\); the threshold \(E=0\) separates the discrete spectrum from the ionization continuum.

def laguerre_associe(p, alpha, x):
    """Associated Laguerre polynomial L_p^alpha(x), with p a non-negative integer."""
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    if p == 0:
        return np.ones_like(x)
    if p == 1:
        return 1 + alpha - x
    L_avant = np.ones_like(x)
    L_courant = 1 + alpha - x
    for k in range(2, p + 1):
        L_suivant = ((2*k - 1 + alpha - x) * L_courant
                     - (k - 1 + alpha) * L_avant) / k
        L_avant, L_courant = L_courant, L_suivant
    return L_courant

def R_nl(n, l, r, Z=1.0):
    """Hydrogen-like radial function in atomic units (fixed nucleus)."""
    if not (n >= 1 and 0 <= l < n):
        raise ValueError("We must have n >= 1 and 0 <= l < n.")
    r = np.asarray(r, dtype=float)
    rho = 2 * Z * r / n
    prefacteur = (2*Z/n)**1.5 * np.sqrt(
        math.factorial(n-l-1) / (2*n*math.factorial(n+l))
    )
    return (prefacteur * np.exp(-rho/2) * rho**l
            * laguerre_associe(n-l-1, 2*l+1, rho))

def probabilite_radiale(n, l, r, Z=1.0):
    return r**2 * np.abs(R_nl(n, l, r, Z))**2

def harmonique_reelle(type_orbitale, theta, phi):
    """Normalized real spherical harmonics for s, p and d."""
    if type_orbitale == "s":
        return np.ones_like(theta) / np.sqrt(4*np.pi)
    if type_orbitale == "pz":
        return np.sqrt(3/(4*np.pi)) * np.cos(theta)
    if type_orbitale == "px":
        return np.sqrt(3/(4*np.pi)) * np.sin(theta) * np.cos(phi)
    if type_orbitale == "py":
        return np.sqrt(3/(4*np.pi)) * np.sin(theta) * np.sin(phi)
    if type_orbitale == "dz2":
        return np.sqrt(5/(16*np.pi)) * (3*np.cos(theta)**2 - 1)
    if type_orbitale == "dxz":
        return np.sqrt(15/(4*np.pi)) * np.sin(theta)*np.cos(theta)*np.cos(phi)
    if type_orbitale == "dxy":
        return np.sqrt(15/(4*np.pi)) * np.sin(theta)**2*np.sin(phi)*np.cos(phi)
    if type_orbitale == "dx2y2":
        return np.sqrt(15/(16*np.pi)) * np.sin(theta)**2*np.cos(2*phi)
    raise ValueError("Unknown orbital type.")

2 Separating variables in spherical coordinates

The potential Equation 4 depends only on the distance \(r\). We use spherical coordinates \((r,\theta,\varphi)\) to separate the radial and angular dependence. The time-independent equation for the relative motion is

\[ \left[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla^2 -\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r} \right]\psi(\mathbf r)=E\psi(\mathbf r). \tag{7}\]

2.1 Laplacian and angular momentum

In spherical coordinates,

\[ \nabla^2 =\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r} \left(r^2\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right) +\frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} \left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right) +\frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\varphi^2}. \tag{8}\]

The angular part is related to the square of the orbital angular momentum:

\[ \widehat L^2 =-\hbar^2\left[ \frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} \left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right) +\frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\varphi^2} \right]. \tag{9}\]

The Laplacian can therefore be written as

\[ \nabla^2 =\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r} \left(r^2\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right) -\frac{\widehat L^2}{\hbar^2r^2}. \]

Since \([\widehat H,\widehat L^2]=[\widehat H,\widehat L_z]=0\), we can look for common eigenstates of the form

\[ \psi_{n\ell m}(r,\theta,\varphi) =R_{n\ell}(r)Y_\ell^m(\theta,\varphi). \tag{10}\]

2.2 Angular equation and the numbers \(\ell\) and \(m\)

The spherical harmonics satisfy

\[ \widehat L^2Y_\ell^m =\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)Y_\ell^m, \tag{11}\]

\[ \widehat L_zY_\ell^m =\hbar mY_\ell^m. \tag{12}\]

Periodicity in the azimuthal angle requires

\[ Y(\theta,\varphi+2\pi)=Y(\theta,\varphi), \]

which gives a factor \(e^{im\varphi}\) with integer \(m\). Regularity at the poles \(\theta=0\) and \(\theta=\pi\) then requires

\[ \ell=0,1,2,\ldots, \qquad m=-\ell,-\ell+1,\ldots,\ell. \tag{13}\]

To fix the phase convention, define

\[ P_\ell^m(x)=(-1)^m(1-x^2)^{m/2}\frac{d^mP_\ell(x)}{dx^m}, \]

where \(P_\ell\) is the Legendre polynomial. For \(m\ge0\), the normalized spherical harmonics are

\[ Y_\ell^m(\theta,\varphi) =\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi} \frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}} P_\ell^m(\cos\theta)e^{im\varphi}. \tag{14}\]

The phase convention is specified explicitly because it varies between references; see NIST DLMF, spherical harmonics. For negative \(m\),

\[ Y_\ell^{-m}=(-1)^m\left(Y_\ell^m\right)^*. \]

The first ones are

\[ Y_0^0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}, \]

\[ Y_1^0=\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}\cos\theta, \qquad Y_1^{\pm1}=\mp\sqrt{\frac{3}{8\pi}}\sin\theta\,e^{\pm i\varphi}, \]

\[ Y_2^0=\sqrt{\frac{5}{16\pi}}(3\cos^2\theta-1). \]

The index \(\ell\) sets the magnitude of the angular momentum,

\[ |\mathbf L|=\hbar\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}, \]

while \(m\) sets its measurable projection along the chosen axis,

\[ L_z=m\hbar. \]

Since the components of \(\mathbf L\) do not commute, they cannot in general all have definite values. The basis \(|\ell,m\rangle\) fixes \(L^2\) and \(L_z\).

For \(m\ne0\), \(Y_\ell^m\) is generally complex. The familiar real orbitals \(p_x\), \(p_y\), \(d_{xy}\) and \(d_{x^2-y^2}\) combine the states \(m\) and \(-m\). They remain energy eigenstates in the Coulomb potential, but they do not have a definite \(L_z\).

2.3 Radial equation

By inserting Equation 10 into Equation 7 and then using Equation 11, we obtain

\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu} \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dr} \left(r^2\frac{\mathrm dR}{\mathrm dr}\right) +\frac{\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu r^2}R -\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}R =ER. \tag{15}\]

To remove the first derivative, set

\[ u(r)=rR(r). \tag{16}\]

Indeed,

\[ R=\frac{u}{r}, \qquad r^2\frac{\mathrm dR}{\mathrm dr}=ru'-u, \qquad \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dr}(ru'-u)=ru''. \]

After multiplying by \(r\), the equation becomes a one-dimensional Schrödinger equation on \(r>0\):

\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\frac{\mathrm d^2u}{\mathrm dr^2} +V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)u(r)=Eu(r), \tag{17}\]

with the effective potential

\[ V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r) =-\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r} +\frac{\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu r^2}. \tag{18}\]

The second term is the centrifugal barrier. It is absent for \(s\) states (\(\ell=0\)) and pushes states with large \(\ell\) away from the nucleus. The physical conditions are

\[ u(0)=0, \qquad u(r)\longrightarrow0\quad\text{as }r\to\infty \]

for a normalizable bound state.

3 Radial solutions and energy levels

We now look for bound states, so \(E<0\). Let us introduce

\[ \kappa=\frac{\sqrt{-2\mu E}}{\hbar}>0, \qquad \rho=2\kappa r, \tag{19}\]

as well as the dimensionless parameter

\[ \gamma =\frac{\mu Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2\kappa} =\frac{Z}{a_\mu\kappa}. \tag{20}\]

Equation Equation 17 becomes

\[ \frac{\mathrm d^2u}{\mathrm d\rho^2} +\left[ -\frac14+\frac{\gamma}{\rho} -\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{\rho^2} \right]u=0. \tag{21}\]

3.1 Behavior at small and large distances

As \(\rho\to\infty\), the terms in \(1/\rho\) disappear and

\[ u''-\frac14u\simeq0. \]

The leading exponential factors are \(e^{\pm\rho/2}\). A bound state must have the decaying factor \(e^{-\rho/2}\), possibly multiplied by powers of \(\rho\).

Near the origin, inserting \(u\sim\rho^s\) and keeping the lowest powers gives

\[ s(s-1)-\ell(\ell+1)=0, \]

which gives \(s=\ell+1\) or \(s=-\ell\). The second branch diverges for \(\ell>0\) and tends to a nonzero constant for \(\ell=0\). In both cases it fails \(u(0)=0\), so

\[ u(\rho)\sim\rho^{\ell+1}. \]

These two behaviors suggest the factorization

\[ u(\rho)=\rho^{\ell+1}e^{-\rho/2}v(\rho). \tag{22}\]

3.2 Power series and termination condition

By substituting Equation 22 into Equation 21 and simplifying, we find

\[ \rho v''+(2\ell+2-\rho)v' +(\gamma-\ell-1)v=0. \tag{23}\]

Let us expand

\[ v(\rho)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_j\rho^j. \]

After shifting the indices, the coefficient of \(\rho^j\) gives

\[ (j+1)(j+2\ell+2)a_{j+1} +(\gamma-\ell-1-j)a_j=0. \]

The recurrence relation is therefore

\[ a_{j+1} =\frac{j+\ell+1-\gamma} {(j+1)(j+2\ell+2)}a_j. \tag{24}\]

At large \(j\), the recurrence has \(a_{j+1}/a_j\sim1/j\). A non-terminating regular solution contains an \(e^\rho\) growth, up to powers of \(\rho\). Multiplication by \(e^{-\rho/2}\) then leaves a growing \(u\), which is not normalizable. The series must stop: its numerator must vanish at an integer \(j=n_r\ge0\). Thus

\[ n_r+\ell+1-\gamma=0. \]

We define the principal quantum number

\[ n=n_r+\ell+1=1,2,3,\ldots \tag{25}\]

and the quantization condition becomes

\[ \gamma=n. \]

Since \(\gamma=Z/(a_\mu\kappa)\),

\[ \kappa=\frac{Z}{na_\mu}. \]

Finally, the definition of \(\kappa\) in Equation 19 gives

\[ \boxed{ E_n =-\frac{\mu Z^2e^4} {2(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2\hbar^2n^2} =-\frac{\mu c^2(Z\alpha)^2}{2n^2}. } \tag{26}\]

Here \(\alpha=e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c)\) is the fine-structure constant. For an infinitely heavy nucleus and \(Z=1\),

\[ E_n=-\frac{13.6057\ \mathrm{eV}}{n^2}. \]

Including the proton mass gives a ground-state ionization energy of about \(13.598\ \mathrm{eV}\) in this model.

The allowed energies are the values for which the radial solution is regular at the origin and normalizable at infinity. This series method is also developed in the MIT notes on the hydrogen atom.

3.3 Normalized radial functions

The terminated series is an associated Laguerre polynomial. Now setting

\[ \rho=\frac{2Zr}{na_\mu}, \]

the normalized solution is

\[ R_{n\ell}(r) =\left(\frac{2Z}{na_\mu}\right)^{3/2} \sqrt{\frac{(n-\ell-1)!}{2n(n+\ell)!}} e^{-\rho/2}\rho^\ell L_{n-\ell-1}^{2\ell+1}(\rho). \tag{27}\]

It satisfies

\[ \int_0^\infty |R_{n\ell}(r)|^2r^2\,\mathrm dr=1. \]

For \(Z=1\), the first examples are

\[ R_{10}(r)=2a_\mu^{-3/2}e^{-r/a_\mu}, \]

\[ R_{20}(r)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\,a_\mu^{-3/2} \left(2-\frac{r}{a_\mu}\right)e^{-r/(2a_\mu)}, \]

\[ R_{21}(r)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{6}}\,a_\mu^{-3/2} \frac{r}{a_\mu}e^{-r/(2a_\mu)}. \]

For a hydrogen-like ion, we only need to replace \(a_\mu\) with \(a_\mu/Z\) in these expressions.

3.4 Radial distribution and nodes

The quantity \(|R_{n\ell}(r)|^2\) is not directly the probability density for the distance from the nucleus. The volume of a spherical shell of radius \(r\) and thickness \(\mathrm dr\) contains the factor \(r^2\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\Omega\). Since the spherical harmonics are normalized,

\[ \int |Y_\ell^m|^2\,\mathrm d\Omega=1, \]

the probability of finding the electron between \(r\) and \(r+\mathrm dr\) is

\[ \mathrm dP=P_{n\ell}(r)\,\mathrm dr, \qquad P_{n\ell}(r)=r^2|R_{n\ell}(r)|^2. \tag{28}\]

In particular, \(R_{10}(r)\) is largest at the nucleus, but \(P_{10}(0)=0\) because a shell of zero radius has zero volume. For the \(1s\) state,

\[ P_{10}(r)=\frac{4r^2}{a_\mu^3}e^{-2r/a_\mu}. \]

Its derivative vanishes when

\[ \frac{\mathrm dP_{10}}{\mathrm dr} \propto 2r\left(1-\frac{r}{a_\mu}\right)e^{-2r/a_\mu}=0. \]

The maximum of the radial distribution is therefore at

\[ r_{\mathrm{mp}}=a_\mu. \tag{29}\]

The polynomial \(L_{n-\ell-1}^{2\ell+1}\) has \(n-\ell-1\) positive zeros. Therefore,

\[ N_{\mathrm{radial}}=n-\ell-1. \]

For the usual real orbitals built from a single \(|m|\), there are also \(\ell\) angular nodes (planes or cones). Counting these with the radial nodes gives

\[ N_{\mathrm{total}}=(n-\ell-1)+\ell=n-1. \tag{30}\]

This surface count refers to that real basis. A complex harmonic such as \(Y_1^1\propto\sin\theta\,e^{i\varphi}\) has no nodal plane: its angular zeros lie on the \(z\) axis.

3.5 Shells, subshells and orbitals

A shell is specified by \(n\). Within it, a subshell has fixed \((n,\ell)\), with \(0\le\ell<n\). In the \(L_z\) basis, an orbital has a further label \(m\), from \(-\ell\) to \(\ell\).

The letters \(s,p,d,f\) stand for \(\ell=0,1,2,3\). For example, the \(3d\) subshell has \(n=3\) and \(\ell=2\); its five values of \(m\) give five spatial orbitals. Adding the subshells of a shell gives

\[ g_n=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}(2\ell+1)=n^2. \tag{31}\]

With two spin states per orbital, a shell contains \(2n^2\) one-electron states. Hydrogen occupies only one of them.

In the Coulomb model, all these spatial states have the same energy \(E_n\). Rotational symmetry explains the degeneracy in \(m\). The additional degeneracy in \(\ell\) is a special property of the \(1/r\) potential.

4 The shape and size of orbitals

A hydrogen-like orbital is the function

\[ \psi_{n\ell m}(r,\theta,\varphi) =R_{n\ell}(r)Y_\ell^m(\theta,\varphi). \]

Its probability density is

\[ \rho_{n\ell m}(\mathbf r) =|R_{n\ell}(r)|^2|Y_\ell^m(\theta,\varphi)|^2. \tag{32}\]

An orbital drawing describes this spatial distribution. A surface may mark a chosen density value; a cloud of sampled points shows where position measurements are more likely. Neither gives the electron a definite path.

4.1 Nodes and the sign of the wave function

A radial node is a sphere on which \(R_{n\ell}(r)=0\). An angular node is a plane, cone or other surface on which \(Y_\ell^m=0\).

For a real orbital, two colors indicate opposite signs of \(\psi\). Both regions have positive probability density \(|\psi|^2\). The relative sign matters when amplitudes interfere.

The \(1s\) state is spherical and has no node. The \(2s\) state has a nodal sphere at \(r=2a_\mu\) for \(Z=1\). A real \(2p_z\) orbital instead has the nodal plane \(z=0\), since its angular factor is \(\cos\theta\). The real \(3p_z\) orbital has both a radial node and this plane.

The usual real orbitals can be written, for example, as

\[ p_z\propto\cos\theta, \qquad p_x\propto\sin\theta\cos\varphi, \qquad p_y\propto\sin\theta\sin\varphi, \]

\[ d_{z^2}\propto3\cos^2\theta-1, \qquad d_{xy}\propto\sin^2\theta\sin(2\varphi), \]

\[ d_{x^2-y^2}\propto\sin^2\theta\cos(2\varphi). \]

def coupe_orbitale(n, l, type_orbitale, etendue, points=420):
    x = np.linspace(-etendue, etendue, points)
    z = np.linspace(-etendue, etendue, points)
    X, Zm = np.meshgrid(x, z)
    Ycoord = np.zeros_like(X)
    rayon = np.sqrt(X**2 + Ycoord**2 + Zm**2)
    theta = np.arccos(np.divide(
        Zm, rayon, out=np.ones_like(rayon), where=rayon > 1e-14
    ))

    psi = R_nl(n, l, rayon) * harmonique_reelle(type_orbitale, theta, phi)
    psi /= np.max(np.abs(psi))
    return x, z, psi

4.2 Mean values and the size of the atom

A mean distance averages over the radial distribution:

\[ \langle r^p\rangle=\int_0^\infty r^p P_{n\ell}(r)\,dr. \]

For the \(1s\) state of hydrogen, for example,

\[ \langle r\rangle_{100} =\frac4{a_\mu^3}\int_0^\infty r^3e^{-2r/a_\mu}\,dr =\frac4{a_\mu^3}\frac{3!}{(2/a_\mu)^4} =\frac32a_\mu. \]

Integrating the general radial functions gives

\[ \left\langle\frac1r\right\rangle_{n\ell} =\frac{Z}{a_\mu n^2}, \tag{33}\]

\[ \langle r\rangle_{n\ell} =\frac{a_\mu}{2Z} \left[3n^2-\ell(\ell+1)\right], \tag{34}\]

\[ \langle r^2\rangle_{n\ell} =\frac{a_\mu^2n^2}{2Z^2} \left[5n^2+1-3\ell(\ell+1)\right]. \tag{35}\]

For the \(Z=1\) ground state,

\[ r_{\mathrm{mp}}=a_\mu, \qquad \langle r\rangle_{100}=\frac32a_\mu, \qquad \sqrt{\langle r^2\rangle_{100}}=\sqrt{3}\,a_\mu. \]

The most probable distance, the mean and the root-mean-square distance differ because the electron has a distribution of distances.

The virial theorem for a stationary bound state is \(2\langle T\rangle=\langle rV'(r)\rangle\). Since \(V\propto-1/r\), it gives

\[ 2\langle T\rangle=-\langle V\rangle. \]

Since \(E=\langle T\rangle+\langle V\rangle\), it follows that

\[ \langle T\rangle=-E_n, \qquad \langle V\rangle=2E_n. \tag{36}\]

The total energy is negative even though the mean kinetic energy is positive. The energy \(|E_n|\) must be supplied to ionize the atom from level \(n\).

5 Hydrogen spectrum and quantum transitions

For an energy eigenstate, \(\Psi(\mathbf r,t)=\psi(\mathbf r)e^{-iEt/\hbar}\), so \(|\Psi|^2\) is time independent. The Coulomb Hamiltonian alone does not describe emission. Coupling the atom to the electromagnetic field allows transitions; spontaneous emission requires a quantum description of that field.

A spectral line appears when an interaction, usually with the electromagnetic field, takes the atom from an initial level \(n_i\) to a final level \(n_f\). For emission,

\[ n_i>n_f, \qquad h\nu=E_{n_i}-E_{n_f}>0. \tag{37}\]

Using Equation 26,

\[ h\nu =\frac{\mu Z^2e^4}{2(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2\hbar^2} \left(\frac{1}{n_f^2}-\frac{1}{n_i^2}\right). \]

Since \(\nu=c/\lambda\), we obtain the Rydberg formula:

\[ \frac{1}{\lambda} =R_\mu Z^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_f^2}-\frac{1}{n_i^2}\right), \tag{38}\]

where

\[ R_\mu=\frac{\mu e^4}{8\varepsilon_0^2h^3c}. \]

For hydrogen, \(R_\mathrm H\simeq1.0968\times10^7\ \mathrm{m}^{-1}\). The dependence on reduced mass explains why hydrogen and deuterium have slightly shifted lines.

5.1 Spectral series

Transitions ending at \(n_f=1\) form the Lyman series in the ultraviolet. Those ending at \(n_f=2\) form the Balmer series; the first line, \(3\to2\), has a wavelength of about \(656\ \mathrm{nm}\). The Paschen and Brackett series end at \(n_f=3\) and \(4\) and lie in the infrared.

As \(n_i\to\infty\), the lines approach the series limit. From Equation 38,

\[ \lambda_{\mathrm{limit}}=\frac{n_f^2}{R_\mu Z^2}. \]

The corresponding photon energy is the energy required to ionize level \(n_f\). Wavelengths below are measured in vacuum.

5.2 Dipole selection rules

Energy conservation tells us where a line is located, but not whether it is strong or even allowed. When the wavelength is much larger than the atom, the leading interaction is the electric dipole term \(H_I=-\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf E\). In the fixed-nucleus approximation, \(\mathbf d=-e\mathbf r\), so the transition amplitude contains

\[ \mathbf d_{fi} =-e\langle n_f\ell_fm_f|\widehat{\mathbf r}| n_i\ell_im_i\rangle. \tag{39}\]

Each Cartesian component of \(\mathbf r\) has angular momentum one. Adding angular momenta therefore permits \(\ell_f=\ell_i-1,\ell_i,\ell_i+1\). The matrix element must also have even overall parity. Since the states have parity \((-1)^\ell\) and \(\mathbf r\) is odd, \(\ell_i+\ell_f\) must be odd, excluding \(\ell_f=\ell_i\). The angular integral thus requires

\[ \Delta\ell=\pm1, \qquad \Delta m=0,\pm1. \tag{40}\]

The photon’s polarization determines which value of \(\Delta m\) occurs. In the absence of an interaction acting on spin,

\[ \Delta s=0. \]

A transition that does not follow these rules is forbidden at the electric dipole level, but it can still occur much more slowly through a higher-order mechanism. Thus, the \(2s\) state cannot decay to \(1s\) by emitting a single dipole photon; it decays mainly through two-photon emission.

A transition changes the quantum state. The energy difference fixes the photon frequency; it does not describe a trajectory between two orbital surfaces.

5.3 Numerical check of the radial equation

We can recover the levels without using their analytical expression. In atomic units, let us discretize Equation 17 on a grid \(r_j=j\Delta r\). The centered second derivative is

\[ u''(r_j)\simeq \frac{u_{j+1}-2u_j+u_{j-1}}{\Delta r^2}. \]

On \(0<r<r_{\max}\) we set \(u(0)=u(r_{\max})=0\). The matrix entries are

\[ H_{jj}=\frac1{\Delta r^2}+\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2r_j^2}-\frac1{r_j}, \qquad H_{j,j\pm1}=-\frac1{2\Delta r^2}. \]

Its negative eigenvalues approximate bound-state energies. A smaller \(\Delta r\) improves the derivative approximation; a larger \(r_{\max}\) reduces the effect of the outer boundary, especially for excited states.

N_grille = 800
dr = r_max / (N_grille + 1)

def spectre_radial_numerique(l):
    diagonale = 1/dr**2 - 1/r + l*(l+1)/(2*r**2)
    hors_diagonale = -np.ones(N_grille - 1) / (2*dr**2)
    H = (np.diag(diagonale)
         + np.diag(hors_diagonale, 1)
         + np.diag(hors_diagonale, -1))
    energies, vecteurs = np.linalg.eigh(H)
    masque = energies < 0
    return energies[masque], vecteurs[:, masque]

5.4 Corrections to the Coulomb spectrum

Relativistic motion and spin modify the energies. For a fixed point nucleus, the leading fine-structure correction is

\[ \Delta E_{nj}^{\mathrm{fine}} =-\frac{m_ec^2(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^4} \left(\frac{n}{j+1/2}-\frac34\right), \tag{41}\]

where \(j=\ell\pm\tfrac12\) is the total electronic angular momentum (only \(j=\tfrac12\) for \(\ell=0\)). The energy now depends on \(j\) as well as \(n\). Nuclear recoil gives further corrections when the nucleus can move.

States with the same \(n\) and \(j\), such as \(2S_{1/2}\) and \(2P_{1/2}\), still coincide in this approximation. Their Lamb shift comes from radiative corrections. Coupling the nuclear and electronic spins gives hyperfine structure, including hydrogen’s \(21\ \mathrm{cm}\) line. External magnetic and electric fields give the Zeeman and Stark effects.
