Special relativity and particle kinematics

Four-vectors, invariant masses, collisions and decays

Author

Mohammed Reguig

1 Minkowski space

Energy, momentum, time and position depend on the inertial frame. A particle can be at rest for one observer and highly energetic for another. However, whether a reaction is possible and the mass of a particle cannot depend on the observer. Special relativity separates frame-dependent components from invariant physical quantities.

We use natural units, \(c=\hbar=1\), unless a conversion to metres or seconds is needed. Mass, energy and momentum then have the same unit, usually MeV or GeV. We choose the metric

\[ g_{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1). \]

A contravariant four-vector is written

\[ a^\mu=(a^0,\mathbf a). \]

Lowering its index changes the signs of the spatial components:

\[ a_\mu=g_{\mu\nu}a^\nu=(a^0,-\mathbf a). \]

The Minkowski scalar product is

\[ a\cdot b=a_\mu b^\mu=a^0b^0-\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b. \tag{1}\]

It is invariant under Lorentz transformations. For the separation \(\Delta x^\mu\) between two events,

\[ \Delta x^2=\Delta t^2-|\Delta\mathbf x|^2. \]

Its sign gives three cases. A timelike interval has \(\Delta x^2>0\) and admits a frame in which both events occur at the same position. A lightlike interval has \(\Delta x^2=0\). A spacelike interval has \(\Delta x^2<0\) and admits a frame in which the events are simultaneous. This classification is the same for every inertial observer.

For a timelike trajectory, the proper time is defined by

\[ d\tau^2=dt^2-d\mathbf x^2=dt^2(1-v^2), \]

so

\[ d\tau=\frac{dt}{\gamma}, \qquad \gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2}}. \tag{2}\]

NoteFrom Lorentz transformations to space-time

Lorentz first obtained the transformations while studying electrodynamics. Einstein interpreted them in 1905 as relations between inertial observers, without a preferred medium. Minkowski then joined space and time into a four-dimensional geometry. This geometric form is why relativistic calculations can be organised with four-vectors and invariant scalar products.

2 Lorentz transformations

Consider a frame \(\mathcal R'\) moving at velocity \(\beta\) along \(+z\) relative to \(\mathcal R\). The coordinates of the same event satisfy

\[ \begin{aligned} t'&=\gamma(t-\beta z),\\ z'&=\gamma(z-\beta t),\\ x'&=x,\qquad y'=y. \end{aligned} \tag{3}\]

In matrix form, \(x'^\mu=\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}x^\nu\), with

\[ \Lambda(\beta)= \begin{pmatrix} \gamma&0&0&-\gamma\beta\\ 0&1&0&0\\ 0&0&1&0\\ -\gamma\beta&0&0&\gamma \end{pmatrix}. \]

Substituting Equation 3 gives

\[ \begin{aligned} t'^2-z'^2 &=\gamma^2\big[(t-\beta z)^2-(z-\beta t)^2\big]\\ &=\gamma^2(1-\beta^2)(t^2-z^2)\\ &=t^2-z^2. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore \(x'^2=x^2\). In matrix notation, this condition is

\[ \Lambda^{\mathrm T}g\Lambda=g. \tag{4}\]

The inverse boost is obtained by changing the sign of the velocity:

\[ \Lambda^{-1}(\beta)=\Lambda(-\beta). \]

It is often useful to introduce the rapidity \(\eta\) through

\[ \beta=\tanh\eta, \qquad \gamma=\cosh\eta, \qquad \gamma\beta=\sinh\eta. \]

Successive boosts along the same axis add their rapidities. If the first boost has velocity \(\beta_1\) and the second \(\beta_2\), the combined velocity is

\[ \beta=\frac{\beta_1+\beta_2}{1+\beta_1\beta_2}. \tag{5}\]

This expression never exceeds the speed of light when \(|\beta_1|<1\) and \(|\beta_2|<1\).

2.1 Four-velocity and four-momentum

The four-velocity is

\[ u^\mu=\frac{dx^\mu}{d\tau}=\gamma(1,\mathbf v), \]

and satisfies \(u^2=1\). Multiplying by the invariant mass gives the four-momentum

\[ p^\mu=mu^\mu=(E,\mathbf p), \]

where

\[ E=\gamma m, \qquad \mathbf p=\gamma m\mathbf v. \]

The invariant norm is the mass-shell relation

\[ \boxed{p^2=E^2-\mathbf p^2=m^2.} \tag{6}\]

For \(|\mathbf p|\ll m\),

\[ E=\sqrt{m^2+\mathbf p^2} =m+\frac{\mathbf p^2}{2m}+O\!\left(\frac{p^4}{m^3}\right). \]

The rest energy and the classical kinetic energy appear as the first two terms. For a massless particle, \(E=|\mathbf p|\).

Under a boost along \(z\),

\[ \begin{aligned} E'&=\gamma(E-\beta p_z),\\ p_z'&=\gamma(p_z-\beta E),\\ p_x'&=p_x,\qquad p_y'=p_y. \end{aligned} \tag{7}\]

The transverse momentum \(p_T=\sqrt{p_x^2+p_y^2}\) is unchanged by a longitudinal boost.

3 Invariant mass and scattering variables

For a reaction

\[ a+b\longrightarrow 1+2+\cdots, \]

energy and momentum conservation form one four-vector equation:

\[ p_a+p_b=\sum_f p_f. \tag{8}\]

If a set of particles has total momentum \(P=\sum_i p_i\), its invariant mass is

\[ M_{\mathrm{inv}}^2=P^2 =\left(\sum_iE_i\right)^2 -\left|\sum_i\mathbf p_i\right|^2. \tag{9}\]

In the centre-of-momentum frame, \(P=(M_{\mathrm{inv}},\mathbf0)\), so the invariant mass is the total energy in that frame. It is not generally the sum of the individual masses: kinetic energy and relative directions also contribute.

For two particles,

\[ M_{12}^2=m_1^2+m_2^2 +2(E_1E_2-\mathbf p_1\cdot\mathbf p_2). \]

For two photons separated by an angle \(\theta\),

\[ M_{\gamma\gamma}^2=2E_1E_2(1-\cos\theta). \tag{10}\]

This is how a massive parent can be reconstructed from two massless decay products.

If some particles are not detected, define

\[ p_{\mathrm{miss}}=P_{\mathrm{initial}}-\sum_{\mathrm{visible}}p_i, \qquad M_{\mathrm{miss}}^2=p_{\mathrm{miss}}^2. \]

The method requires the initial four-momentum to be known well enough.

3.1 Mandelstam variables

For the process \(a(p_a)+b(p_b)\to1(p_1)+2(p_2)\), define

\[ \begin{aligned} s&=(p_a+p_b)^2,\\ t&=(p_a-p_1)^2,\\ u&=(p_a-p_2)^2. \end{aligned} \tag{11}\]

The variable \(s\) is the square of the total centre-of-momentum energy. The variable \(t\) measures the four-momentum transfer between \(a\) and particle 1; it is often negative with our metric. Conservation gives

\[ s+t+u=m_a^2+m_b^2+m_1^2+m_2^2. \tag{12}\]

To see this, expand the three definitions and use \(p_b-p_1-p_2=-p_a\). Only two of the three invariants are independent once the external masses are fixed.

4 Two-body kinematics

4.1 Centre-of-momentum frame

In this frame, marked with a star,

\[ P^*=p_a^*+p_b^*=(\sqrt{s},\mathbf0). \]

The scalar product \(P\cdot p_a\) is invariant. From \(P=p_a+p_b\),

\[ P\cdot p_a=\frac{s+m_a^2-m_b^2}{2}. \]

In the centre-of-momentum frame it is also \(\sqrt{s}\,E_a^*\). Therefore

\[ E_a^*=\frac{s+m_a^2-m_b^2}{2\sqrt{s}}, \qquad E_b^*=\frac{s+m_b^2-m_a^2}{2\sqrt{s}}. \tag{13}\]

For the final state,

\[ E_1^*=\frac{s+m_1^2-m_2^2}{2\sqrt{s}}, \qquad E_2^*=\frac{s+m_2^2-m_1^2}{2\sqrt{s}}. \]

Introduce the Källén function

\[ \lambda(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2-2xy-2xz-2yz. \]

Using \(|\mathbf p^*|^2=E^{*2}-m^2\) gives

\[ |\mathbf p_i^*| =\frac{\sqrt{\lambda(s,m_a^2,m_b^2)}}{2\sqrt{s}}, \qquad |\mathbf p_f^*| =\frac{\sqrt{\lambda(s,m_1^2,m_2^2)}}{2\sqrt{s}}. \tag{14}\]

The condition \(\lambda(s,m_1^2,m_2^2)\ge0\) gives the physical threshold \(\sqrt{s}\ge m_1+m_2\).

If \(\theta^*\) is the angle between \(\mathbf p_a^*\) and \(\mathbf p_1^*\),

\[ t=m_a^2+m_1^2-2E_a^*E_1^* +2|\mathbf p_i^*||\mathbf p_f^*|\cos\theta^*. \tag{15}\]

At fixed \(s\), the scattering angle therefore determines \(t\).

4.2 Fixed target and collider

Let particle \(b\) be at rest in the laboratory frame. Then

\[ s=(p_a+p_b)^2=m_a^2+m_b^2+2m_bE_a. \tag{16}\]

At high energy, \(\sqrt{s}\simeq\sqrt{2m_bE_a}\). Much of the projectile energy becomes kinetic energy of the centre of momentum rather than energy available to create new particles.

If the final masses are \(m_1,\ldots,m_n\), the threshold value is

\[ s_{\mathrm{th}}=\left(\sum_jm_j\right)^2. \]

Thus the minimum total projectile energy is

\[ E_a^{\mathrm{th}} =\frac{(\sum_jm_j)^2-m_a^2-m_b^2}{2m_b}, \tag{17}\]

and its minimum kinetic energy is \(T_a^{\mathrm{th}}=E_a^{\mathrm{th}}-m_a\). At threshold, the final particles are at rest relative to one another in their centre-of-momentum frame, not in the laboratory frame.

For two opposite ultrarelativistic beams,

\[ \sqrt{s}\simeq2\sqrt{E_aE_b}. \]

With equal beam energies \(E\), this becomes \(\sqrt{s}\simeq2E\). A collider therefore uses beam energy much more efficiently than a fixed-target experiment.

4.3 Rapidity

The longitudinal rapidity of a particle is

\[ y=\frac12\ln\left(\frac{E+p_z}{E-p_z}\right). \]

With \(m_T=\sqrt{m^2+p_T^2}\),

\[ E=m_T\cosh y, \qquad p_z=m_T\sinh y. \]

A longitudinal boost of rapidity \(\eta\) gives \(y'=y-\eta\). Rapidity differences and \(p_T\) are therefore invariant under a common longitudinal boost. For a highly relativistic particle, \(y\) is close to the pseudorapidity

\[ \eta_{\mathrm{pseudo}}=-\ln\tan\left(\frac\theta2\right), \]

which depends only on the polar angle measured by the detector.

5 Particle decays

Consider the decay \(A\to1+2\), with parent mass \(M\). In the parent rest frame,

\[ P^*=(M,\mathbf0), \qquad P^*=p_1^*+p_2^*. \]

Momentum conservation gives \(\mathbf p_2^*=-\mathbf p_1^*\). Squaring \(p_2^*=P^*-p_1^*\) gives

\[ m_2^2=M^2+m_1^2-2ME_1^*, \]

so

\[ E_1^*=\frac{M^2+m_1^2-m_2^2}{2M}, \qquad E_2^*=\frac{M^2+m_2^2-m_1^2}{2M}. \tag{18}\]

The common momentum magnitude is

\[ p^*=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda(M^2,m_1^2,m_2^2)}}{2M}. \tag{19}\]

The decay is possible only when \(M\ge m_1+m_2\). Kinematics fixes the energies and momentum magnitude, while the interaction and the spin state determine the angular distribution.

If the parent moves along \(+z\) with velocity \(\beta\), a daughter emitted at angle \(\theta^*\) has

\[ \begin{aligned} E_1^{\mathrm{lab}}&=\gamma(E_1^*+\beta p^*\cos\theta^*),\\ p_{1z}^{\mathrm{lab}}&=\gamma(p^*\cos\theta^*+\beta E_1^*),\\ p_{1T}^{\mathrm{lab}}&=p^*\sin\theta^*. \end{aligned} \tag{20}\]

For an isotropic decay, \(\cos\theta^*\) is uniform. Since \(E_1^{\mathrm{lab}}\) is linear in \(\cos\theta^*\), the laboratory energy distribution is flat between

\[ E_{1,\min}^{\mathrm{lab}}=\gamma(E_1^*-\beta p^*), \qquad E_{1,\max}^{\mathrm{lab}}=\gamma(E_1^*+\beta p^*). \]

5.1 Phase space, width and lifetime

For a final state with \(n\) particles, the Lorentz-invariant phase-space measure is

\[ d\Phi_n(P;p_1,\ldots,p_n) =(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}\!\left(P-\sum_{i=1}^np_i\right) \prod_{i=1}^n\frac{d^3\mathbf p_i}{(2\pi)^3,2E_i}. \tag{21}\]

The decay rate is

\[ d\Gamma=\frac{1}{2M}\overline{|\mathcal M|^2},d\Phi_n. \tag{22}\]

For a two-body decay,

\[ \frac{d\Gamma}{d\Omega} =\frac{p^*}{32\pi^2M^2}\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}. \]

If the squared matrix element is angle-independent,

\[ \Gamma=\frac{p^*}{8\pi M^2}\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}. \tag{23}\]

The factor \(p^*\) shows the phase-space suppression near threshold. If the final state contains identical particles, the phase-space integral must be divided by the appropriate factorial to avoid counting the same state several times.

An unstable state evolves as \(e^{-iMt-\Gamma t/2}\) in its rest frame. Its survival probability is \(e^{-\Gamma t}\), hence

\[ \tau_0=\frac{1}{\Gamma} \]

in natural units, or \(\tau_0=\hbar/\Gamma\) in SI units. In the laboratory, the mean decay length is

\[ L=\beta\gamma c\tau_0=\frac{pc}{M}\tau_0. \tag{24}\]

6 Cross sections and observed events

For a reaction with two incoming particles,

\[ d\sigma=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{(p_a\cdot p_b)^2-m_a^2m_b^2}} \overline{|\mathcal M|^2},d\Phi_n. \tag{25}\]

For \(2\to2\) scattering in the centre-of-momentum frame,

\[ \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} =\frac{1}{64\pi^2s} \frac{|\mathbf p_f^*|}{|\mathbf p_i^*|} \overline{|\mathcal M|^2}. \tag{26}\]

If there is no azimuthal dependence, Equation 15 gives

\[ \frac{dt}{d\cos\theta^*} =2|\mathbf p_i^*||\mathbf p_f^*|. \]

Using \(d\Omega=2\pi,d\cos\theta^*\),

\[ \frac{d\sigma}{dt} =\frac{1}{64\pi s|\mathbf p_i^*|^2} \overline{|\mathcal M|^2}. \]

For massless incoming particles, \(|\mathbf p_i^*|=\sqrt{s}/2\) and

\[ \frac{d\sigma}{dt} =\frac{1}{16\pi s^2}\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}. \]

The number of observed events in a selected decay channel is

\[ N_{\mathrm{obs}} =\sigma\,\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}\,\mathcal B\,\varepsilon, \tag{27}\]

where \(\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}\) is the integrated luminosity, \(\mathcal B\) the product of branching fractions and \(\varepsilon\) the total acceptance and efficiency.

Useful conversions are

\[ 1\ \mathrm{GeV}^{-2}\simeq0.3894\ \mathrm{mb}, \qquad 1\ \mathrm{pb}=10^{-12}\ \mathrm b. \]

7 Deep-inelastic scattering

Consider

\[ e^-(k)+p(P)\longrightarrow e^-(k')+X. \]

The momentum transferred to the proton is \(q=k-k'\). Neglecting the electron mass,

\[ q^2=-4EE'\sin^2\left(\frac\theta2\right), \]

so one defines the positive quantity

\[ Q^2=-q^2=4EE'\sin^2\left(\frac\theta2\right). \tag{28}\]

A large \(Q^2\) corresponds to a short distance scale. The invariant mass of the hadronic final state is

\[ W^2=(P+q)^2=M_p^2+2P\cdot q-Q^2. \tag{29}\]

The Bjorken variable and the inelasticity are

\[ x=\frac{Q^2}{2P\cdot q}, \qquad y=\frac{P\cdot q}{P\cdot k}. \tag{30}\]

In the proton rest frame, \(y=(E-E')/E\). Eliminating \(P\cdot q\) gives

\[ W^2=M_p^2+Q^2\left(\frac1x-1\right). \tag{31}\]

If the electron mass is neglected,

\[ s=(k+P)^2=M_p^2+2P\cdot k, \]

and therefore

\[ Q^2=xy(s-M_p^2)\simeq xys \tag{32}\]

at high energy. Elastic scattering has \(W=M_p\) and \(x=1\). In deep-inelastic scattering, \(W>M_p\) and \(0<x<1\). In the parton model and at large \(Q^2\), \(x\) is interpreted as the longitudinal momentum fraction of the struck parton. That interpretation uses dynamics in addition to kinematics.

8 Exercises

Unless stated otherwise, use \(c=\hbar=1\) and \(g=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)\).

8.1 Exercise 1 — Interval and proper time

Two events have separation \(\Delta x^\mu=(5,3,0,0)\ \mathrm{m}\), with the time component written as \(c\Delta t\).

  1. Classify the interval.
  2. Find the proper time between the events.
  3. Find the velocity of the frame in which the events occur at the same place.
NoteSolution

The invariant interval is

\[ \Delta x^2=5^2-3^2=16\ \mathrm{m}^2>0, \]

so it is timelike. In the frame where the spatial separation vanishes, \(c\Delta\tau=\sqrt{\Delta x^2}=4\ \mathrm m\), hence

\[ \Delta\tau=\frac4c\simeq1.33\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm s. \]

From \(\Delta x'=\gamma(\Delta x-\beta c\Delta t)=0\),

\[ \beta=\frac{\Delta x}{c\Delta t}=\frac35. \]

8.2 Exercise 2 — A boosted four-momentum

A particle has \(p^\mu=(10,0,0,8)\ \mathrm{GeV}\).

  1. Find its invariant mass.
  2. Find the velocity of its rest frame relative to the given frame.
  3. Verify that a boost to this frame gives \(p'^\mu=(m,\mathbf0)\).
NoteSolution

The mass is

\[ m=\sqrt{E^2-p_z^2}=\sqrt{100-64}=6\ \mathrm{GeV}. \]

To set \(p_z'=\gamma(p_z-\beta E)\) to zero, choose \(\beta=p_z/E=0.8\). Then \(\gamma=1/\sqrt{1-0.8^2}=5/3\), and

\[ E'=\frac53(10-0.8\times8)=6\ \mathrm{GeV}, \qquad p_z'=0. \]

8.3 Exercise 3 — Muon decay length

A muon has energy \(E=20\ \mathrm{GeV}\), mass \(m_\mu=105.7\ \mathrm{MeV}\) and proper lifetime \(\tau_0=2.20\ \mu\mathrm s\).

  1. Calculate \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\).
  2. Find its mean decay length.
NoteSolution

Using GeV throughout,

\[ \gamma=\frac{E}{m_\mu}=\frac{20}{0.1057}\simeq189.2. \]

Also \(\beta=\sqrt{1-1/\gamma^2}\simeq0.999986\). Therefore

\[ L=\beta\gamma c\tau_0 \simeq189.2(2.998\times10^8)(2.20\times10^{-6})\ \mathrm m \simeq1.25\times10^5\ \mathrm m. \]

8.4 Exercise 4 — Diphoton invariant mass

Two photons have energies \(E_1=40\ \mathrm{GeV}\) and \(E_2=60\ \mathrm{GeV}\), with an angle of \(120^\circ\) between them. Find their invariant mass. What would happen if they were collinear?

NoteSolution

From Equation 10,

\[ M_{\gamma\gamma}^2 =2(40)(60)(1-\cos120^\circ) =7200\ \mathrm{GeV}^2. \]

Thus \(M_{\gamma\gamma}\simeq84.9\ \mathrm{GeV}\). If the photons were collinear in the same direction, \(\theta=0\) and their invariant mass would vanish.

8.5 Exercise 5 — Mandelstam variables

For massless elastic scattering \(a+b\to a+b\) in the centre-of-momentum frame, show that

\[ t=-\frac s2(1-\cos\theta^*), \qquad u=-\frac s2(1+\cos\theta^*), \]

and verify \(s+t+u=0\).

NoteSolution

Every particle has \(E^*=|\mathbf p^*|=\sqrt{s}/2\). Equation Equation 15 gives

\[ t=-2p^{*2}(1-\cos\theta^*) =-\frac s2(1-\cos\theta^*). \]

Particle 2 is emitted in the opposite direction, so replacing \(\cos\theta^*\) by \(-\cos\theta^*\) gives

\[ u=-\frac s2(1+\cos\theta^*). \]

Adding the three expressions gives zero, in agreement with Equation 12 for four massless particles.

8.6 Exercise 6 — Pion production on a fixed target

For \(p+p\to p+p+\pi^0\), use \(m_p=938.27\ \mathrm{MeV}\) and \(m_{\pi^0}=134.98\ \mathrm{MeV}\).

  1. Find the threshold kinetic energy of the projectile proton.
  2. Explain why it is larger than \(m_{\pi^0}\).
NoteSolution

The minimum final invariant mass is \(2m_p+m_{\pi^0}\). Equation Equation 17 gives

\[ E_p^{\mathrm{th}} =\frac{(2m_p+m_{\pi^0})^2-2m_p^2}{2m_p}. \]

Subtracting the projectile rest mass,

\[ T_p^{\mathrm{th}} =2m_{\pi^0}+\frac{m_{\pi^0}^2}{2m_p} \simeq279.7\ \mathrm{MeV}. \]

The final state must keep the incident momentum. At threshold its particles have no relative motion in their centre-of-momentum frame, but that frame still moves in the laboratory. Some energy is therefore required for collective kinetic motion.

8.7 Exercise 7 — A two-body decay

A particle of mass \(M=5.0\ \mathrm{GeV}\) decays to particles of masses \(m_1=1.0\ \mathrm{GeV}\) and \(m_2=2.0\ \mathrm{GeV}\).

  1. Find \(E_1^*\) and \(E_2^*\).
  2. Find the common momentum magnitude.
  3. Check energy conservation.
NoteSolution

Using Equation 18,

\[ E_1^*=\frac{25+1-4}{10}=2.2\ \mathrm{GeV}, \qquad E_2^*=\frac{25+4-1}{10}=2.8\ \mathrm{GeV}. \]

Then

\[ p^*=\sqrt{E_1^{*2}-m_1^2} =\sqrt{2.2^2-1^2} \simeq1.96\ \mathrm{GeV}. \]

The second daughter gives the same value, and \(E_1^*+E_2^*=5.0\ \mathrm{GeV}=M\).

8.8 Exercise 8 — Energy endpoints after a boost

The parent in Exercise 7 has laboratory energy \(E_A=10\ \mathrm{GeV}\) and moves along \(+z\). Assume an isotropic decay.

  1. Find \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\).
  2. Find the minimum and maximum laboratory energies of particle 1.
  3. Describe the energy distribution between them.
NoteSolution

Here \(\gamma=E_A/M=2\) and \(\beta=\sqrt{1-1/\gamma^2}=\sqrt3/2\). With \(E_1^*=2.2\ \mathrm{GeV}\) and \(p^*\simeq1.96\ \mathrm{GeV}\),

\[ E_{1,\min}^{\mathrm{lab}} =2(2.2-0.866\times1.96)\simeq1.00\ \mathrm{GeV}, \]

\[ E_{1,\max}^{\mathrm{lab}} =2(2.2+0.866\times1.96)\simeq7.80\ \mathrm{GeV}. \]

For an isotropic decay, \(\cos\theta^*\) is uniform. Since the energy is linear in \(\cos\theta^*\), the ideal energy distribution is uniform between these endpoints.

8.9 Exercise 9 — Width and lifetime

A particle has total width \(\Gamma=4.0\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{GeV}\).

  1. Find its proper lifetime using \(\hbar=6.582\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{GeV\,s}\).
  2. If \(\beta\gamma=3\), find its mean decay length.
NoteSolution

The proper lifetime is

\[ \tau_0=\frac{\hbar}{\Gamma} =\frac{6.582\times10^{-25}}{4.0\times10^{-13}} \simeq1.65\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm s. \]

Thus

\[ L=\beta\gamma c\tau_0 =3(2.998\times10^8)(1.65\times10^{-12})\ \mathrm m \simeq1.48\ \mathrm{mm}. \]

8.10 Exercise 10 — Event yield

A process has \(\sigma=2.5\ \mathrm{pb}\) and is studied with \(\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}=140\ \mathrm{fb}^{-1}\). The selected decay has branching fraction \(0.12\) and efficiency \(0.35\).

  1. Calculate the number produced before decays and selection.
  2. Calculate the expected observed yield.
NoteSolution

Since \(1\ \mathrm{fb}^{-1}=10^3\ \mathrm{pb}^{-1}\),

\[ N_{\mathrm{prod}}=(2.5)(140\times10^3)=3.50\times10^5. \]

After branching fraction and efficiency,

\[ N_{\mathrm{obs}} =(3.50\times10^5)(0.12)(0.35) =1.47\times10^4. \]

8.11 Exercise 11 — Deep-inelastic variables

An electron of energy \(E=20\ \mathrm{GeV}\) scatters from a proton at rest. The final electron has \(E'=12\ \mathrm{GeV}\) and scattering angle \(\theta=20^\circ\). Use \(M_p=0.938\ \mathrm{GeV}\).

  1. Calculate \(Q^2\) and \(y\).
  2. Calculate \(x\).
  3. Calculate \(W\) and check that the event is inelastic.
NoteSolution

From Equation 28,

\[ Q^2=4(20)(12)\sin^2(10^\circ) \simeq28.95\ \mathrm{GeV}^2. \]

In the proton rest frame,

\[ y=1-\frac{E'}E=0.40. \]

Since \(2P\cdot q=2M_p(E-E')\),

\[ x=\frac{Q^2}{2M_p(E-E')} \simeq\frac{28.95}{2(0.938)(8)} \simeq1.93. \]

This is outside the physical range \(x\le1\). The stated values cannot describe scattering from a free proton at rest. Equivalently,

\[ W^2=M_p^2+2M_p(E-E')-Q^2<0. \]

The contradiction is useful: measured energies and angles must satisfy the invariant kinematic constraints. For example, at the same energies the angle must be smaller than about \(14.4^\circ\) to keep \(x\le1\).

8.12 Exercise 12 — Physical DIS event

Repeat Exercise 11 with \(\theta=10^\circ\).

  1. Find \(Q^2\), \(y\), \(x\) and \(W\).
  2. Explain separately what \(Q^2\), \(x\) and \(W\) describe.
NoteSolution

The inelasticity is unchanged: \(y=0.40\). Now

\[ Q^2=4(20)(12)\sin^2(5^\circ) \simeq7.29\ \mathrm{GeV}^2. \]

Therefore

\[ x=\frac{7.29}{2(0.938)(8)}\simeq0.486. \]

Finally,

\[ W^2=M_p^2+2M_p(8)-7.29\simeq8.60\ \mathrm{GeV}^2, \]

so \(W\simeq2.93\ \mathrm{GeV}>M_p\). The event is inelastic. The value \(Q^2\) sets the resolution scale, \(x\) labels the longitudinal momentum fraction in the parton-model interpretation, and \(W\) is the invariant mass produced in the hadronic system.

9 Problems

9.1 Problem 1 — Formation and decay of a resonance

An electron and positron collide head-on with equal beam energies \(E\). They form a resonance \(R\) of mass \(M_R\), which decays as \(R\to\mu^+\mu^-\). Neglect the electron mass.

  1. Find \(s\) and the beam energy required to produce \(R\) at rest.
  2. Find the muon energy and momentum in the resonance rest frame.
  3. Show that the reconstructed dimuon invariant mass is \(M_R\).
  4. If the beams instead have unequal energies \(E_+\) and \(E_-\), find the velocity of the produced resonance in the laboratory.
NoteSolution

For opposite massless beams,

\[ p_-=(E,0,0,E), \qquad p_+=(E,0,0,-E). \]

Their sum is \((2E,\mathbf0)\), so \(s=4E^2\). Resonant production at rest requires

\[ E=\frac{M_R}{2}. \]

The two muons have equal masses and are back-to-back. Hence

\[ E_\mu^*=\frac{M_R}{2}, \qquad p_\mu^*=\frac12\sqrt{M_R^2-4m_\mu^2}. \]

Their total four-momentum is the resonance momentum, so

\[ M_{\mu\mu}^2=(p_{\mu^+}+p_{\mu^-})^2=P_R^2=M_R^2. \]

For unequal beams,

\[ P_R=(E_-+E_+,0,0,E_--E_+), \]

therefore

\[ \beta_R=\frac{E_--E_+}{E_-+E_+}. \]

9.2 Problem 2 — Missing-mass reconstruction

A particle \(A\) of known four-momentum decays as \(A\to B+X\). Only \(B\) is detected.

  1. Express the missing mass \(M_X\) in terms of \(P_A\) and \(p_B\).
  2. In the rest frame of \(A\), express \(M_X^2\) using \(M_A\), \(m_B\) and \(E_B^*\).
  3. Suppose \(M_A=5.0\ \mathrm{GeV}\), \(m_B=1.0\ \mathrm{GeV}\) and \(E_B^*=2.2\ \mathrm{GeV}\). Find \(M_X\).
  4. Explain why the result does not depend on the frame used for the measurement.
NoteSolution

Four-momentum conservation gives \(p_X=P_A-p_B\). Thus

\[ M_X^2=(P_A-p_B)^2. \]

In the rest frame of \(A\), \(P_A=(M_A,\mathbf0)\), so

\[ M_X^2=M_A^2+m_B^2-2M_AE_B^*. \]

With the given values,

\[ M_X^2=25+1-2(5)(2.2)=4\ \mathrm{GeV}^2, \]

and \(M_X=2.0\ \mathrm{GeV}\). The result is frame-independent because it is the Minkowski square of a four-vector.

9.3 Problem 3 — From a cross section to a displaced signal

A particle \(X\) is produced with cross section \(0.80\ \mathrm{pb}\) in a dataset of \(300\ \mathrm{fb}^{-1}\). It decays to the selected channel with branching fraction \(0.05\). The selection efficiency, excluding the decay-position requirement, is \(0.40\). Each accepted particle has approximately \(\beta\gamma=2.5\) and proper lifetime \(\tau_0=0.50\ \mathrm{ns}\).

  1. Find the mean laboratory decay length.
  2. Assuming an exponential decay law, find the probability that \(X\) decays between \(1\ \mathrm{cm}\) and \(20\ \mathrm{cm}\) from the production point.
  3. Find the expected number of selected events in this interval.
NoteSolution

The mean decay length is

\[ L=\beta\gamma c\tau_0 =2.5(2.998\times10^8)(0.50\times10^{-9})\ \mathrm m \simeq0.375\ \mathrm m. \]

For an exponential distribution in distance,

\[ P(\ell_1<\ell<\ell_2) =e^{-\ell_1/L}-e^{-\ell_2/L}. \]

With \(\ell_1=0.01\ \mathrm m\) and \(\ell_2=0.20\ \mathrm m\),

\[ P\simeq e^{-0.01/0.375}-e^{-0.20/0.375} \simeq0.387. \]

The number produced is

\[ N_{\mathrm{prod}}=(0.80)(300\times10^3)=2.40\times10^5. \]

The expected selected yield is

\[ N_{\mathrm{obs}} =(2.40\times10^5)(0.05)(0.40)(0.387) \simeq1.86\times10^3. \]
