1 Dynamics in quantum mechanics

A particle confined in a well has only certain allowed energies. A particle approaching a barrier can be reflected or transmitted, even when classical mechanics predicts a different result. These effects follow from the wave function and the conditions imposed at each boundary.

We study a nonrelativistic, spinless particle of mass \(m\) in one dimension. The potential \(V(x)\) is real and independent of time. We solve the Schrödinger equation in each region, then match the solutions at the interfaces.

The formulas keep \(m\) and \(\hbar\). For the figures and simulations, we use reduced units with \(\hbar=1\) and \(\hbar^2/(2m)=1\). The wave function is normalized for bound states; scattering states are described using incident and outgoing currents.

1.1 The time-dependent Schrödinger equation

For a classical particle in a potential \(V(x)\), the energy is

\[ H(x,p)=\frac{p^2}{2m}+V(x). \]

Canonical quantization replaces position and momentum with the operators

\[ \widehat x=x, \qquad \widehat p=-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}. \]

The Hamiltonian operator therefore becomes

\[ \widehat H =-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}+V(x), \tag{1}\]

and the evolution of the state is described by

\[ i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi(x,t)}{\partial t} =\widehat H\Psi(x,t) =-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2} +V(x)\Psi(x,t). \tag{2}\]

The time-evolution equation is a postulate of quantum mechanics. For a given Hamiltonian and boundary conditions, specifying \(\Psi(x,0)\) determines the later state. The stationary equation below is second order in space, so each constant-potential region has two independent spatial solutions.

The Born interpretation associates the state with the probability density

\[ \rho(x,t)=|\Psi(x,t)|^2. \tag{3}\]

For a localized state, normalization requires

\[ \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|\Psi(x,t)|^2\,\mathrm dx=1. \tag{4}\]

1.2 Conservation of probability

Probability must neither appear nor disappear. This conservation law follows directly from the Schrödinger equation. Multiply Equation 2 by \(\Psi^*\), then subtract the complex conjugate equation multiplied by \(\Psi\). The terms containing the real potential \(V\) cancel:

\[ i\hbar\left( \Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} +\Psi\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t} \right) =-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \left( \Psi^*\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2} -\Psi\frac{\partial^2\Psi^*}{\partial x^2} \right). \]

The left-hand side is \(i\hbar\,\partial_t|\Psi|^2\). The term in parentheses on the right-hand side is a total derivative:

\[ \Psi^*\partial_x^2\Psi-\Psi\partial_x^2\Psi^* =\partial_x\left( \Psi^*\partial_x\Psi-\Psi\partial_x\Psi^* \right). \]

We thus obtain the continuity equation

\[ \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} +\frac{\partial j}{\partial x}=0, \tag{5}\]

with the probability current

\[ j(x,t)=\frac{\hbar}{m} \operatorname{Im}\!\left( \Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x} \right). \tag{6}\]

For a plane wave \(Ae^{i(kx-\omega t)}\), the current is

\[ j_+=\frac{\hbar k}{m}|A|^2. \]

For a wave \(Be^{-i(kx+\omega t)}\) traveling toward decreasing \(x\),

\[ j_-=-\frac{\hbar k}{m}|B|^2. \]

The sign of the current therefore gives the direction of propagation, information that the density \(|\Psi|^2\) alone does not contain.

1.3 Stationary states

When \(V\) does not depend on time, let us look for a separated solution

\[ \Psi(x,t)=\psi(x)T(t). \]

Substituting into Equation 2 and dividing by \(\psi T\) gives

\[ i\hbar\frac{1}{T}\frac{\mathrm dT}{\mathrm dt} =-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{1}{\psi} \frac{\mathrm d^2\psi}{\mathrm dx^2}+V(x). \]

The left-hand side depends only on \(t\), and the right-hand side only on \(x\). They must therefore equal a constant, denoted by \(E\). The time-dependent part satisfies

\[ i\hbar\frac{\mathrm dT}{\mathrm dt}=ET, \qquad T(t)=e^{-iEt/\hbar}, \]

while the spatial part satisfies the time-independent Schrödinger equation

\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\mathrm d^2\psi}{\mathrm dx^2} +V(x)\psi(x)=E\psi(x). \tag{7}\]

An energy eigenstate can therefore be written as

\[ \Psi_E(x,t)=\psi_E(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}. \]

Its time factor is a phase with modulus one, so \(|\Psi_E(x,t)|^2=|\psi_E(x)|^2\) does not depend on time. The word “stationary” describes the probability density, not the complex phase.

A superposition of several energies,

\[ \Psi(x,t)=\sum_n c_n\psi_n(x)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}, \tag{8}\]

is generally not stationary: the interference terms oscillate at the Bohr angular frequencies \((E_n-E_m)/\hbar\).

NoteSchrödinger and Born

In 1926, Erwin Schrödinger published a series of papers in which he developed wave mechanics and, among other things, solved the hydrogen atom. That same year, Max Born proposed the probabilistic interpretation of the wave function. The equation therefore describes a probability amplitude, not a classical material wave that directly spreads the particle’s charge through space.

1.4 Solution in a region of constant potential

Suppose that \(V(x)=V_j\) in a given region. Equation Equation 7 becomes

\[ \frac{\mathrm d^2\psi}{\mathrm dx^2} +\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E-V_j)\psi=0. \tag{9}\]

The form of the solution depends on the sign of \(E-V_j\).

If \(E>V_j\), define

\[ k_j=\frac{\sqrt{2m(E-V_j)}}{\hbar}. \tag{10}\]

The solution is oscillatory:

\[ \psi_j(x)=A_je^{ik_jx}+B_je^{-ik_jx}. \tag{11}\]

The first term carries a current to the right and the second to the left.

If \(E<V_j\), define

\[ \kappa_j=\frac{\sqrt{2m(V_j-E)}}{\hbar}. \tag{12}\]

The solution becomes exponential:

\[ \psi_j(x)=C_je^{\kappa_jx}+D_je^{-\kappa_jx}. \tag{13}\]

These are evanescent solutions. In a semi-infinite forbidden region, discard the term that grows at infinity. In a finite region, both terms may be needed to satisfy the boundary conditions.

At the threshold \(E=V_j\), the equation becomes \(\psi''=0\), so

\[ \psi_j(x)=A_j+B_jx. \]

1.5 Matching conditions

Consider a finite interface at \(x=x_0\). Integrate Equation 7 between \(x_0-\varepsilon\) and \(x_0+\varepsilon\):

\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \left[\psi'(x_0+\varepsilon)-\psi'(x_0-\varepsilon)\right] +\int_{x_0-\varepsilon}^{x_0+\varepsilon}(V-E)\psi\,\mathrm dx=0. \]

If \(V\) remains finite, the integral tends to zero as \(\varepsilon\to0\). Therefore,

\[ \psi'(x_0^+)=\psi'(x_0^-). \]

The function \(\psi\) must also be continuous: a jump in \(\psi\) would produce a distribution in \(\psi''\) more singular than any finite term in the equation could balance. For a finite step,

\[ \psi(x_0^-)=\psi(x_0^+), \qquad \psi'(x_0^-)=\psi'(x_0^+). \tag{14}\]

These two relations at each interface give exactly the equations needed to determine the unknown amplitudes.

An infinite wall imposes \(\psi=0\) at the boundary. A delta potential is different: \(\psi\) remains continuous, but its derivative has a jump. We derive this jump below.

def integrale_trapezes(y, x):
    """Integrate sampled values with the trapezoidal rule."""
    y = np.asarray(y)
    x = np.asarray(x)
    return np.sum(0.5 * (y[1:] + y[:-1]) * np.diff(x))

def bisection(f, a, b, iterations=80):
    """Find a root between endpoints with opposite signs."""
    fa, fb = f(a), f(b)
    if not np.isfinite(fa) or not np.isfinite(fb) or fa * fb > 0:
        raise ValueError("The root is not correctly bracketed.")
    for _ in range(iterations):
        c = 0.5 * (a + b)
        fc = f(c)
        if fa * fc <= 0:
            b, fb = c, fc
        else:
            a, fa = c, fc
    return 0.5 * (a + b)

2 Bound states: confinement and quantization

A bound state describes a particle that remains localized near the well. Its wave function is normalizable, and its energy generally belongs to a discrete spectrum. The infinite and finite square wells show how boundary conditions turn a continuous differential equation into a sequence of quantized energies.

2.1 The infinite square well

Consider the potential

\[ V(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & 0<x<L,\\ +\infty, & x\leq0\ \text{or}\ x\geq L. \end{cases} \tag{15}\]

The particle cannot be detected outside the well. The wave function must therefore satisfy

\[ \psi(0)=\psi(L)=0. \tag{16}\]

Inside the well, \(V=0\) and Equation 7 becomes

\[ \psi''+k^2\psi=0, \qquad k=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}. \]

The general solution is

\[ \psi(x)=A\sin(kx)+B\cos(kx). \]

The condition \(\psi(0)=0\) requires \(B=0\). The second condition gives

\[ A\sin(kL)=0. \]

For a nonzero wave function, we therefore need

\[ kL=n\pi, \qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots \]

and the allowed energies are

\[ E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}. \tag{17}\]

The value \(n=0\) would give \(\psi=0\) everywhere, which does not represent any physical state. After normalization,

\[ \psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}} \sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right). \tag{18}\]

The ground state has nonzero energy,

\[ E_1=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}. \]

This zero-point energy is a consequence of confinement. At zero energy, the wave function would be linear. Requiring it to vanish at both walls would make it zero everywhere. Localizing it therefore requires a nonzero spread in momentum.

Two properties can be seen immediately in the diagram: state \(n\) has \(n-1\) internal nodes, and its wavelength decreases as the energy increases. For this one-dimensional well, each energy corresponds to one independent eigenfunction.

For two real normalized eigenfunctions with distinct energies, write

\[ \Psi=\sqrt p\,\psi_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar} +\sqrt{1-p}\,e^{i\phi}\psi_m e^{-iE_mt/\hbar}. \]

Multiplying by the complex conjugate gives

\[ |\Psi|^2=p\psi_n^2+(1-p)\psi_m^2 +2\sqrt{p(1-p)}\,\psi_n\psi_m \cos\!\left(\phi-\frac{E_m-E_n}{\hbar}t\right). \]

The last term changes with time. The density can therefore move inside a fixed well, even though each eigenstate has a stationary density. The second simulation shows this interference.

2.2 The finite square well

The infinite well is an idealization. In a real confinement system, the walls have a finite height and the wave function enters the classically forbidden regions. Place the origin at the center of the well and choose

\[ V(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & |x|<L/2,\\ V_0, & |x|\geq L/2, \end{cases} \qquad V_0>0. \tag{19}\]

A bound state satisfies \(0<E<V_0\). Inside the well, the solution oscillates with

\[ k=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}, \]

while outside it decays with

\[ \kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}{\hbar}. \]

The potential is even, \(V(-x)=V(x)\). The Hamiltonian therefore commutes with the parity operator, and the eigenstates can be chosen to be even or odd. We can therefore solve on \(x\geq0\) and recover the other half by reflection.

For an even state, take, for \(x\geq0\),

\[ \psi_{\mathrm p}(x)= \begin{cases} A\cos(kx), & 0\leq x<L/2,\\ Ce^{-\kappa(x-L/2)}, & x\geq L/2. \end{cases} \]

At the boundary \(x=L/2\), the continuity of \(\psi\) and \(\psi'\) gives

\[ A\cos\!\left(\frac{kL}{2}\right)=C, \]

\[ -Ak\sin\!\left(\frac{kL}{2}\right)=-\kappa C. \]

Dividing the second relation by the first removes the constants:

\[ k\tan\!\left(\frac{kL}{2}\right)=\kappa \qquad\text{(even states).} \tag{20}\]

For an odd state, take \(\psi=A\sin(kx)\) inside the well and \(\psi=Ce^{-\kappa(x-L/2)}\) for \(x\geq L/2\). Matching gives \(A\sin(kL/2)=C\) and \(Ak\cos(kL/2)=-\kappa C\). Dividing gives

\[ -k\cot\!\left(\frac{kL}{2}\right)=\kappa \qquad\text{(odd states).} \tag{21}\]

Since the unknown energy appears both in the square roots and inside the trigonometric functions, we find the roots numerically.

Introduce the dimensionless variables

\[ z=\frac{kL}{2}, \qquad z_0=\frac{L}{2}\frac{\sqrt{2mV_0}}{\hbar}. \]

Since

\[ \left(\frac{\kappa L}{2}\right)^2=z_0^2-z^2, \]

the quantization conditions become

\[ z\tan z=\sqrt{z_0^2-z^2} \qquad\text{(even)}, \tag{22}\]

\[ -z\cot z=\sqrt{z_0^2-z^2} \qquad\text{(odd)}. \tag{23}\]

The single parameter \(z_0\) measures the effective depth of the well. The larger it is, the more bound states the well contains. Unlike the infinite well, their number is finite.

Show Python code 
# Units hbar²/(2m)=1
L = 2.0
V0 = 25.0
z0 = 0.5 * L * np.sqrt(V0)
eps = 1e-8
racines = []

# One even root in each interval (n*pi, n*pi + pi/2)
# and one odd root in ((n+1/2)*pi, (n+1)*pi).
for n in range(int(np.ceil(z0 / np.pi)) + 1):
    a_pair = n * np.pi + eps
    b_pair = min(n * np.pi + np.pi / 2 - eps, z0 - eps)
    if a_pair < b_pair:
        f_pair = lambda z: z * np.tan(z) - np.sqrt(z0**2 - z**2)
        try:
            racines.append((bisection(f_pair, a_pair, b_pair), "even"))
        except ValueError:
            pass

    a_impair = n * np.pi + np.pi / 2 + eps
    b_impair = min((n + 1) * np.pi - eps, z0 - eps)
    if a_impair < b_impair:
        f_impair = lambda z: -z / np.tan(z) - np.sqrt(z0**2 - z**2)
        try:
            racines.append((bisection(f_impair, a_impair, b_impair), "odd"))
        except ValueError:
            pass

The wave function does not suddenly vanish at the walls: it has exponential tails with characteristic length \(1/\kappa\). The energy of a level is therefore lower than that of the corresponding level in an infinite well of the same width, because the particle explores a larger effective region.

As \(V_0\to\infty\), the tails disappear and each fixed energy level approaches the corresponding infinite-well value. Near \(E=V_0\), \(\kappa\) becomes small: the tail extends far outside the well. In one dimension, this square well has at least one bound state for every \(V_0>0\) and \(L>0\).

2.3 The attractive delta potential

The delta potential is the ideal limit of an extremely narrow and deep well whose area remains finite:

\[ V(x)=-g\,\delta(x), \qquad g>0. \tag{24}\]

The potential is zero everywhere except at \(x=0\). For a bound state with \(E<0\), let

\[ \kappa=\frac{\sqrt{-2mE}}{\hbar}. \]

Normalizability requires decay on both sides of the origin:

\[ \psi(x)=A e^{-\kappa|x|}. \tag{25}\]

The function is continuous, but its derivative changes suddenly. Integrate the Schrödinger equation around the origin:

\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \left[\psi'(0^+)-\psi'(0^-)\right] -g\psi(0)=0. \]

The jump is therefore

\[ \psi'(0^+)-\psi'(0^-) =-\frac{2mg}{\hbar^2}\psi(0). \tag{26}\]

For \(Ae^{-\kappa|x|}\), we have \(\psi'(0^+)=-\kappa A\) and \(\psi'(0^-)=+\kappa A\). The previous condition gives

\[ -2\kappa A=-\frac{2mg}{\hbar^2}A, \qquad \kappa=\frac{mg}{\hbar^2}. \]

There is therefore a single bound state, with energy

\[ E=-\frac{mg^2}{2\hbar^2}, \tag{27}\]

and normalization gives

\[ \psi(x)=\sqrt{\kappa}\,e^{-\kappa|x|}. \tag{28}\]

3 Quantum scattering: step, barrier, and tunneling

A localized incident particle is described by a wave packet. To study scattering at a fixed energy, we first use stationary plane waves and calculate their reflected and transmitted parts. These stationary states cannot be normalized over the whole real line, so we use current ratios.

If \(j_{\mathrm i}\), \(j_{\mathrm r}\), and \(j_{\mathrm t}\) denote the incident, reflected, and transmitted currents, respectively, we define

\[ R=\frac{|j_{\mathrm r}|}{j_{\mathrm i}}, \qquad T=\frac{j_{\mathrm t}}{j_{\mathrm i}}. \tag{29}\]

For a real, time-independent potential that does not absorb the particle, current conservation requires

\[ R+T=1. \tag{30}\]

3.1 The potential step with \(E>V_0\)

Consider

\[ V(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\ V_0, & x\geq0. \end{cases} \tag{31}\]

A particle comes from the left with energy \(E>V_0\). The wave numbers are

\[ k_1=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}, \qquad k_2=\frac{\sqrt{2m(E-V_0)}}{\hbar}. \]

There is an incident wave and a reflected wave in region I. In region II, no wave returns from \(+\infty\). Setting the incident amplitude to one, write

\[ \psi_{\mathrm I}(x)=e^{ik_1x}+r e^{-ik_1x}, \qquad \psi_{\mathrm{II}}(x)=t e^{ik_2x}. \tag{32}\]

At \(x=0\), continuity of \(\psi\) gives

\[ 1+r=t, \]

and continuity of \(\psi'\) gives

\[ k_1(1-r)=k_2t. \]

Replacing \(t\) by \(1+r\) in the second relation gives

\[ k_1-k_1r=k_2+k_2r, \]

and therefore

\[ r=\frac{k_1-k_2}{k_1+k_2}, \qquad t=1+r=\frac{2k_1}{k_1+k_2}. \tag{33}\]

The transmitted and incident waves have different velocities, \(\hbar k_2/m\) and \(\hbar k_1/m\). The transmission coefficient must include their ratio:

\[ R=|r|^2 =\left(\frac{k_1-k_2}{k_1+k_2}\right)^2, \]

\[ T=\frac{k_2}{k_1}|t|^2 =\frac{4k_1k_2}{(k_1+k_2)^2}. \tag{34}\]

Adding the numerators gives \((k_1-k_2)^2+4k_1k_2=(k_1+k_2)^2\), hence \(R+T=1\).

Classically, a particle with \(E>V_0\) crosses the step. Here the change in wavelength produces partial reflection. The reflection becomes small when \(E\) is much larger than \(V_0\).

3.2 The potential step with \(E<V_0\)

If \(0<E<V_0\), region II is classically forbidden. Let

\[ k=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}, \qquad \kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}{\hbar}. \]

The solution that does not diverge as \(x\to+\infty\) is

\[ \psi_{\mathrm I}(x)=e^{ikx}+r e^{-ikx}, \qquad \psi_{\mathrm{II}}(x)=C e^{-\kappa x}. \tag{35}\]

The matching conditions at \(x=0\) are

\[ 1+r=C, \qquad ik(1-r)=-\kappa C. \]

Eliminating \(C\) gives

\[ r=\frac{k-i\kappa}{k+i\kappa}, \qquad C=\frac{2k}{k+i\kappa}. \tag{36}\]

The numerator and denominator of \(r\) are complex conjugates. Therefore,

\[ |r|=1, \qquad R=1, \qquad T=0. \tag{37}\]

However, there is a nonzero density in the forbidden region:

\[ |\psi_{\mathrm{II}}(x)|^2 =|C|^2e^{-2\kappa x}. \]

The wave amplitude falls by a factor \(e\) over the length

\[ \ell_{\mathrm p}=\frac{1}{\kappa}. \tag{38}\]

The density falls by a factor \(e\) over \(1/(2\kappa)\). Since \(\psi^*\psi'=-\kappa|C|^2e^{-2\kappa x}\) is real, its imaginary part vanishes and the current is zero. A nonzero density near the step is therefore compatible with total reflection.

3.3 The rectangular barrier

Now place a forbidden region of finite width between two free regions:

\[ V(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\ V_0, & 0\leq x\leq a,\\ 0, & x>a. \end{cases} \tag{39}\]

The difference from the step is important. The evanescent wave can reach the second interface and create a propagating wave there. A particle can therefore cross the barrier even when \(E<V_0\).

3.3.1 Case \(E>V_0\): propagation and resonances

Define

\[ k=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}, \qquad q=\frac{\sqrt{2m(E-V_0)}}{\hbar}. \]

The three solutions are chosen in the form

\[ \begin{aligned} \psi_{\mathrm I}(x)&=e^{ikx}+r e^{-ikx},\\ \psi_{\mathrm{II}}(x)&=Ae^{iqx}+Be^{-iqx},\\ \psi_{\mathrm{III}}(x)&=t e^{ik(x-a)}. \end{aligned} \tag{40}\]

The phase of the last wave was chosen so that \(\psi_{\mathrm{III}}(a)=t\). The matching conditions at \(0\) give

\[ 1+r=A+B, \qquad k(1-r)=q(A-B), \]

and those at \(a\) give

\[ Ae^{iqa}+Be^{-iqa}=t, \]

\[ q\left(Ae^{iqa}-Be^{-iqa}\right)=kt. \]

First solve the two equations at \(a\):

\[ Ae^{iqa}=\frac t2\left(1+\frac kq\right), \qquad Be^{-iqa}=\frac t2\left(1-\frac kq\right). \]

Substituting into the conditions at \(0\) gives

\[ 1+r=t\left[\cos(qa)-i\frac kq\sin(qa)\right], \]

\[ 1-r=t\left[\cos(qa)-i\frac qk\sin(qa)\right]. \]

Adding eliminates \(r\), so

\[ t=\left[ \cos(qa)-i\frac{k^2+q^2}{2kq}\sin(qa) \right]^{-1}. \tag{41}\]

Because the wave numbers are the same on the left and right, \(T=|t|^2\). Using \(k^2-q^2=2mV_0/\hbar^2\), we obtain

\[ T(E>V_0)= \left[ 1+\frac{V_0^2}{4E(E-V_0)}\sin^2(qa) \right]^{-1}. \tag{42}\]

When \(qa=n\pi\) with \(n=1,2,\ldots\), the wave completes an integer number of half-wavelengths inside the barrier, and

\[ T=1. \]

The barrier becomes perfectly transparent through constructive interference, even though each interface is reflective on its own.

3.3.2 Case \(E<V_0\): tunneling

Now let

\[ \kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}{\hbar}. \]

Inside the barrier, keep both exponential terms,

\[ \psi_{\mathrm{II}}(x)=Ae^{\kappa x}+Be^{-\kappa x}, \]

because the forbidden region has a finite width. The growing term does not diverge on the interval \([0,a]\) and contributes to matching at the second interface.

The previous result extends by replacing \(q\) with \(i\kappa\), with

\[ \cos(i\kappa a)=\cosh(\kappa a), \qquad \sin(i\kappa a)=i\sinh(\kappa a). \]

We find

\[ T(E<V_0)= \left[ 1+\frac{V_0^2}{4E(V_0-E)}\sinh^2(\kappa a) \right]^{-1}. \tag{43}\]

For an opaque barrier, \(\kappa a\gg1\), use \(\sinh(\kappa a)\simeq e^{\kappa a}/2\):

\[ T\simeq 16\frac{E(V_0-E)}{V_0^2} e^{-2\kappa a}. \tag{44}\]

For a thick barrier, the exponential factor dominates: increasing its width strongly reduces transmission.

At \(E=V_0\), use \(\sin(qa)/q\to a\) in the transmission amplitude. With \(k=\sqrt{2mV_0}/\hbar\), this gives

\[ t=\frac1{1-ika/2}, \qquad T(E=V_0)=\frac1{1+mV_0a^2/(2\hbar^2)}. \]

The formulas from above and below the barrier approach this same value.

def transmission_barriere(epsilon, alpha):
    epsilon = np.asarray(epsilon, dtype=float)
    T = np.empty_like(epsilon)
    dessous = epsilon < 1 - 1e-7
    dessus = epsilon > 1 + 1e-7
    seuil = ~(dessous | dessus)

    e = epsilon[dessous]
    T[dessous] = 1 / (1 + np.sinh(alpha * np.sqrt(1 - e))**2 /
                      (4 * e * (1 - e)))

    e = epsilon[dessus]
    T[dessus] = 1 / (1 + np.sin(alpha * np.sqrt(e - 1))**2 /
                     (4 * e * (e - 1)))

    T[seuil] = 1 / (1 + alpha**2 / 4)
    return T

In 1928, George Gamow, as well as Ronald Gurney and Edward Condon, explained alpha decay through tunneling. An alpha particle whose energy is below the Coulomb barrier of the nucleus still has a probability of escaping. The exponential dependence of \(T\) explains why small changes in energy lead to nuclear lifetimes ranging from fractions of a second to billions of years.

3.4 Scattering by a delta potential

Consider the delta potential again in its general form

\[ V(x)=\lambda\delta(x), \]

where \(\lambda\) can be positive or negative. For a particle incident from the left,

\[ \psi(x)= \begin{cases} e^{ikx}+r e^{-ikx}, & x<0,\\ t e^{ikx}, & x>0. \end{cases} \]

Continuity gives \(1+r=t\). This time, the derivative jump is

\[ \psi'(0^+)-\psi'(0^-) =\frac{2m\lambda}{\hbar^2}\psi(0). \tag{45}\]

Substituting the plane waves gives

\[ ikt-ik(1-r)=\frac{2m\lambda}{\hbar^2}t. \]

With \(t=1+r\) and

\[ \eta=\frac{m\lambda}{\hbar^2k}, \]

we obtain

\[ t=\frac{1}{1+i\eta}, \qquad r=\frac{-i\eta}{1+i\eta}. \tag{46}\]

Therefore,

\[ T=\frac{1}{1+\eta^2}, \qquad R=\frac{\eta^2}{1+\eta^2}. \tag{47}\]

Transmission depends only on \(\lambda^2\), but only the attractive case \(\lambda<0\) has the bound state found earlier.

3.5 Multiple interfaces and propagation matrices

Repeating the matching calculation by hand becomes tedious for a series of wells and barriers. A matrix formulation brings the calculation together. In a region of width \(d\) where \(V\) is constant and \(E>V\), knowing \(\psi\) and \(\psi'\) at the left boundary determines their values at the right boundary:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} \psi(x+d)\\[2mm] \psi'(x+d) \end{pmatrix} = \underbrace{ \begin{pmatrix} \cos(kd) & \dfrac{\sin(kd)}{k}\\[3mm] -k\sin(kd) & \cos(kd) \end{pmatrix}}_{\mathbf P(k,d)} \begin{pmatrix} \psi(x)\\[2mm] \psi'(x) \end{pmatrix}. \tag{48}\]

Indeed, \(\psi(x+d)=\psi(x)\cos(kd)+\psi'(x)\sin(kd)/k\). Differentiating with respect to \(d\) gives the second row. For \(E<V\), take \(k=i\kappa\). At \(E=V\), the limit is \(\mathbf P=\begin{pmatrix}1&d\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\).

For \(N\) successive layers, the total matrix is the ordered product

\[ \mathbf P_{\mathrm{tot}} =\mathbf P_N\mathbf P_{N-1}\cdots\mathbf P_1. \tag{49}\]

The continuity of \(\psi\) and \(\psi'\) at the interfaces is already included, because the same state vector is passed from one layer to the next.

A two-barrier system shows the value of this method. Between the two barriers, multiple reflections can build a nearly bound wave. At certain energies, this interference makes the whole system almost perfectly transparent: this is resonant tunneling.

Show Python code 
def matrice_propagation(E, V, largeur):
    q = np.sqrt(complex(E - V))
    if abs(q) < 1e-11:
        return np.array([[1, largeur], [0, 1]], dtype=complex)
    c = np.cos(q * largeur)
    s = np.sin(q * largeur)
    return np.array([[c, s / q], [-q * s, c]], dtype=complex)

def transmission_couches(E, couches):
    """couches is a list of pairs (V, width), in units hbar²/(2m)=1."""
    k = np.sqrt(E)
    matrice = np.eye(2, dtype=complex)
    for V, largeur in couches:
        matrice = matrice_propagation(E, V, largeur) @ matrice

    incident = np.array([1, 1j * k], dtype=complex)
    reflechi = np.array([1, -1j * k], dtype=complex)
    sortant = np.array([1, 1j * k], dtype=complex)

    # M(incident + r*reflected) = t*outgoing
    systeme = np.column_stack((matrice @ reflechi, -sortant))
    r, t = np.linalg.solve(systeme, -(matrice @ incident))
    return float(np.clip(abs(t)**2, 0, 1 + 1e-10))

4 Numerical bound states

The previous problems use analytical solutions in each region. For a more complicated geometry, the Hamiltonian can be discretized directly.

4.1 Discretizing the Hamiltonian

On a uniform grid \(x_i=x_{\min}+i\Delta x\), the second derivative is approximated by

\[ \psi''(x_i) \simeq \frac{\psi_{i+1}-2\psi_i+\psi_{i-1}}{\Delta x^2}. \tag{50}\]

The stationary equation becomes a matrix problem

\[ \mathbf H\boldsymbol\psi=E\boldsymbol\psi, \tag{51}\]

with

\[ H_{ii}=\frac{\hbar^2}{m\Delta x^2}+V(x_i), \]

\[ H_{i,i+1}=H_{i+1,i} =-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m\Delta x^2}. \tag{52}\]

The matrix is real and symmetric. Its eigenvalues approximate the energies, and its eigenvectors give the wave functions on the grid. Setting \(\psi=0\) at the ends imposes infinite walls.

Here the physical model has infinite walls at \(x=-4\) and \(x=4\), with a square barrier in the middle. A high central barrier makes the first two energies close: the ground state is even and the next state is odd.

Show Python code

When the two wells are weakly coupled, a suitable choice of relative sign in \((\psi_1\pm\psi_2)/\sqrt2\) concentrates the state mainly in one well. The relative phase changes at angular frequency \((E_2-E_1)/\hbar\). The state returns after the period

\[ T_{\mathrm{tun}}=\frac{2\pi\hbar}{E_2-E_1}. \tag{53}\]

The transfer to the other well takes half this period, \(\pi\hbar/(E_2-E_1)\).

To assess the grid error, repeat the calculation with a smaller spacing and compare the low energy levels. For a bound state on an unbounded domain, the box must also be enlarged until moving its artificial boundaries no longer changes the result.
