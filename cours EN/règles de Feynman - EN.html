Interactions, the S-matrix and Feynman diagrams

From the Dyson series to scattering amplitudes

Author

Mohammed Reguig

1 From free fields to interactions

A free quantum field describes particles which propagate without scattering or decaying. Its Hamiltonian is diagonal in the occupation-number basis,

\[ H_0=\sum_a\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3} E_{a,\mathbf p}\,a_a^\dagger(\mathbf p)a_a(\mathbf p), \]

where \(a\) labels the species, spin or polarization. The time evolution changes only the phase of a multiparticle state. Its momenta and particle content remain unchanged.

An interacting theory is written as

\[ \mathcal L=\mathcal L_0+\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}, \qquad H=H_0+H_{\mathrm{int}}. \tag{1}\]

The interaction contains products of fields and therefore products of creation and annihilation operators. A term proportional to \(\phi^4\), for example, contains terms such as \(a^\dagger a^\dagger aa\), which can change the momenta of two particles, as well as terms which change the number of particles when energy and momentum conservation allow it.

In four space-time dimensions,

\[ [\phi]=1, \qquad [\psi]=\frac32, \qquad [A_\mu]=1, \qquad [\mathcal L]=4. \]

Two simple examples are

\[ \mathcal L_{\phi^4} =\frac12(\partial\phi)^2-\frac12m^2\phi^2 -\frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4, \tag{2}\]

and the Yukawa theory

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm Y} =\frac12(\partial\phi)^2-\frac12\mu^2\phi^2 +\bar\psi(i\not\!\partial-m)\psi -g\bar\psi\psi\phi. \tag{3}\]

Both \(\lambda\) and \(g\) are dimensionless. The factor \(1/4!\) in Equation 2 cancels the \(4!\) equivalent ways of attaching four identical scalar fields to a vertex.

We use natural units, \(\hbar=c=1\), and

\[ \eta_{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1). \]

One-particle states have the relativistic normalization

\[ \langle\mathbf q|\mathbf p\rangle =2E_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q). \tag{4}\]

2 The S-matrix

2.1 Asymptotic states

In a scattering experiment, particles are far apart before the collision and again far apart after it. Stable particles can then be described by free states with their physical masses. We denote them by

\[ |i,\mathrm{in}\rangle \quad\text{at}\quad t\to-\infty, \]

and

\[ |f,\mathrm{out}\rangle \quad\text{at}\quad t\to+\infty. \]

The scattering matrix connects the two descriptions:

\[ |i,\mathrm{out}\rangle=S|i,\mathrm{in}\rangle. \]

The transition amplitude is

\[ S_{fi}=\langle f,\mathrm{out}|i,\mathrm{in}\rangle. \]

Writing

\[ S=\mathbf1+iT \]

separates the trivial case in which nothing happens from the interaction. Translation invariance gives an overall momentum-conserving delta function. The invariant amplitude \(\mathcal M\) is defined by

\[ \boxed{ \langle f|iT|i\rangle =i(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}(P_f-P_i)\mathcal M_{i\to f}.} \tag{5}\]

The delta function is not part of \(\mathcal M\). It expresses a symmetry of space-time, while \(\mathcal M\) contains the dynamics.

Unitarity,

\[ S^\dagger S=1, \]

implies

\[ -i(T-T^\dagger)=T^\dagger T. \tag{6}\]

Taking a forward matrix element produces the optical theorem. It relates the imaginary part of a forward amplitude to a sum over all possible intermediate final states. This relation is an important test of perturbative calculations.

2.2 The interaction picture

The Schrödinger picture puts time dependence in the states. The Heisenberg picture puts it in the operators. Perturbation theory is easier in the interaction picture, where the free Hamiltonian controls the operators and the interaction Hamiltonian controls the states.

For a Schrödinger operator \(O_S\),

\[ O_I(t)=e^{iH_0t}O_Se^{-iH_0t}. \]

The interaction-picture state is

\[ |\Psi(t)\rangle_I=e^{iH_0t}|\Psi(t)\rangle_S. \]

Differentiating and using the Schrödinger equation gives

\[ i\frac{d}{dt}|\Psi(t)\rangle_I =H_I(t)|\Psi(t)\rangle_I, \tag{7}\]

where

\[ H_I(t)=e^{iH_0t}H_{\mathrm{int}}e^{-iH_0t}. \]

The fields inside \(H_I\) are therefore free fields. This is why free propagators appear in perturbation theory even though the full theory is interacting.

Let \(U_I(t,t_0)\) be the evolution operator,

\[ |\Psi(t)\rangle_I=U_I(t,t_0)|\Psi(t_0)\rangle_I. \]

It obeys

\[ i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}U_I(t,t_0) =H_I(t)U_I(t,t_0), \qquad U_I(t_0,t_0)=1. \tag{8}\]

Integrating once gives

\[ U_I(t,t_0) =1-i\int_{t_0}^{t}dt_1\,H_I(t_1)U_I(t_1,t_0). \]

Repeated substitution produces the perturbative expansion.

3 Dyson series and time ordering

3.1 Derivation of the series

The first terms of the iterative solution are

\[ \begin{aligned} U_I(t,t_0) ={}&1-i\int_{t_0}^{t}dt_1\,H_I(t_1)\\ &+(-i)^2\int_{t_0}^{t}dt_1 \int_{t_0}^{t_1}dt_2\,H_I(t_1)H_I(t_2)+\cdots. \end{aligned} \]

The second integral covers only the region \(t_1>t_2\). We may extend it to the full square if we introduce the time-ordering operator,

\[ T\{H_I(t_1)H_I(t_2)\} =\theta(t_1-t_2)H_I(t_1)H_I(t_2) +\theta(t_2-t_1)H_I(t_2)H_I(t_1). \]

Then

\[ \int_{t_0}^{t}dt_1\int_{t_0}^{t_1}dt_2 \,H_I(t_1)H_I(t_2) =\frac1{2!}\int_{t_0}^{t}dt_1dt_2 \,T\{H_I(t_1)H_I(t_2)\}. \]

The general result is the Dyson series

\[ \boxed{ U_I(t,t_0) =T\exp\left[-i\int_{t_0}^{t}dt'\,H_I(t')\right].} \tag{9}\]

Taking the asymptotic limits gives

\[ \boxed{ S=T\exp\left[-i\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dt\,H_I(t)\right].} \tag{10}\]

For interactions without derivative subtleties, \(H_I(t)=-\int d^3\mathbf x\,\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}\), so

\[ S=T\exp\left[i\int d^4x\,\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}(x)\right]. \tag{11}\]

This last form must be used with care for derivative interactions, constrained systems and gauge theories, where the relation between the interaction Hamiltonian and Lagrangian is more delicate.

NoteDyson’s organisation of perturbation theory

In 1949, Freeman Dyson showed how the operator and diagrammatic approaches to quantum electrodynamics fit into a single perturbative expansion. Time ordering is the step that turns the evolution equation into the diagrams now associated with Feynman’s name.

3.2 Correlation functions

Scattering amplitudes are obtained from time-ordered correlation functions. For an interacting scalar field,

\[ G_n(x_1,\ldots,x_n) =\langle\Omega|T\{\phi_H(x_1)\cdots\phi_H(x_n)\}|\Omega\rangle, \]

where \(|\Omega\rangle\) is the interacting vacuum. In terms of interaction-picture fields,

\[ \boxed{ G_n =\frac{ \langle0|T\left\{ \phi_I(x_1)\cdots\phi_I(x_n) \exp\left[i\int d^4x\,\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}(x)\right] \right\}|0\rangle }{ \langle0|T\exp\left[i\int d^4x\,\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}(x)\right]|0\rangle }.} \tag{12}\]

The denominator cancels vacuum bubbles: diagrams that contain no external point and are disconnected from the process.

4 Wick’s theorem

4.1 Normal ordering and contractions

Normal ordering, denoted by colons, places all creation operators to the left of annihilation operators. Its vacuum expectation value vanishes unless the expression contains no operators:

\[ \langle0|:\!\phi(x_1)\cdots\phi(x_n)\!:|0\rangle=0. \]

For two free scalar fields, define the contraction by

\[ \big[\phi(x)\phi(y)\big]_{\mathrm c} \equiv T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\}-:\!\phi(x)\phi(y)\!:. \]

Its vacuum expectation value is the Feynman propagator,

\[ \big[\phi(x)\phi(y)\big]_{\mathrm c} =\langle0|T\phi(x)\phi(y)|0\rangle =\Delta_F(x-y), \]

with

\[ \Delta_F(x-y) =\int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4} \frac{i\,e^{-ip\cdot(x-y)}}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}. \tag{13}\]

Wick’s theorem states that a time-ordered product of free fields equals the normal-ordered product plus all possible single contractions, all possible double contractions, and so on. For four scalar fields,

\[ \begin{aligned} T\{\phi_1\phi_2\phi_3\phi_4\} ={}&:\!\phi_1\phi_2\phi_3\phi_4\!: +\text{all single contractions}\\ &+\Delta_{12}\Delta_{34} +\Delta_{13}\Delta_{24} +\Delta_{14}\Delta_{23}. \end{aligned} \]

Only complete contractions survive between vacuum states. This is the origin of diagrammatic perturbation theory: every contraction becomes a propagator line, and every factor from \(\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}\) becomes a vertex.

For fermions, exchanging two fields produces a minus sign. A closed fermion loop therefore contributes an extra factor \(-1\). The sign follows from the operator ordering, not from the shape of the drawing.

4.2 Connected diagrams and loop number

A diagram may be connected or disconnected. Only connected diagrams contribute to the connected part of a correlation function. Vacuum bubbles cancel through the denominator of Equation 12. Disconnected pieces describing several independent scatterings must also be separated when one defines a single connected amplitude.

For a connected diagram with \(I\) internal lines and \(V\) vertices, the number of independent loop momenta is

\[ \boxed{L=I-V+1.} \tag{14}\]

Tree diagrams have \(L=0\). Each loop introduces an unconstrained four-momentum integration. The word “loop” refers to the topology and the momentum integral; it does not mean that a particle follows a classical closed path.

5 Momentum-space Feynman rules

The rules are a compact form of the Dyson expansion and Wick’s theorem. They depend on the sign conventions in the Lagrangian and propagators, so one consistent convention must be used from beginning to end.

At every vertex, space-time integration gives

\[ \int d^4x\,e^{-i(p_1+\cdots+p_n)\cdot x} =(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}(p_1+\cdots+p_n). \]

It is convenient to take every momentum as incoming at a vertex. Momentum conservation is then

\[ p_1+\cdots+p_n=0. \]

After using the vertex delta functions, one overall delta function remains. It is removed according to Equation 5, leaving \(i\mathcal M\).

5.1 Scalar \(\phi^4\) theory

For

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}=-\frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4, \]

the momentum-space rules are:

Each internal scalar line of momentum \(p\) contributes

\[ \frac{i}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}. \]

Each four-scalar vertex contributes

\[ -i\lambda. \]

Each independent loop momentum is integrated with

\[ \int\frac{d^4\ell}{(2\pi)^4}. \]

The diagram is divided by its symmetry factor when identical contractions produce the same graph.

For the tree-level process

\[ \phi(p_1)+\phi(p_2)\to\phi(p_3)+\phi(p_4), \]

there is one contact vertex. Thus

\[ i\mathcal M=-i\lambda, \qquad \boxed{\mathcal M=-\lambda.} \tag{15}\]

At the next order, the \(s\)-channel bubble contains two vertices and two internal lines:

\[ i\mathcal M_s^{(1)} =\frac12(-i\lambda)^2 \int\frac{d^4\ell}{(2\pi)^4} \frac{i}{\ell^2-m^2+i\epsilon} \frac{i}{(\ell+p_1+p_2)^2-m^2+i\epsilon}. \tag{16}\]

The factor \(1/2\) is its symmetry factor. The \(t\)- and \(u\)-channel diagrams have the same form with different external momentum combinations. The integral is ultraviolet divergent; its treatment belongs to renormalization.

5.2 Yukawa theory

For

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}=-g\bar\psi\psi\phi, \]

the scalar propagator is

\[ \frac{i}{p^2-\mu^2+i\epsilon}, \]

the fermion propagator is

\[ \frac{i(\not\!p+m)}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}, \tag{17}\]

and each Yukawa vertex contributes

\[ -ig. \]

An incoming fermion contributes \(u_s(p)\), an outgoing fermion contributes \(\bar u_s(p)\), an incoming antifermion contributes \(\bar v_s(p)\), and an outgoing antifermion contributes \(v_s(p)\). The order of spinors and gamma matrices must follow the fermion line.

For elastic fermion-fermion scattering through scalar exchange,

\[ \psi(p_1)+\psi(p_2)\to\psi(p_3)+\psi(p_4), \]

one contribution is

\[ i\mathcal M_t =(-ig)^2 \left[\bar u(p_3)u(p_1)\right] \frac{i}{(p_1-p_3)^2-\mu^2+i\epsilon} \left[\bar u(p_4)u(p_2)\right]. \tag{18}\]

Because the external fermions are identical, the exchanged diagram must also be included with the relative minus sign required by Fermi statistics.

The scalar decay \(\phi\to\psi\bar\psi\) has

\[ i\mathcal M=(-ig)\bar u(p)v(q), \]

so

\[ \mathcal M=-g\bar u(p)v(q). \]

6 From correlation functions to amplitudes

6.1 LSZ reduction

A correlation function includes external propagators between the interaction region and the points \(x_i\). A scattering amplitude should describe the interaction of on-shell particles, without these external propagators. The Lehmann–Symanzik–Zimmermann reduction formula performs this removal.

For a scalar field with wave-function factor \(Z\), the schematic form is

\[ \begin{aligned} \langle p_1',\ldots,p_m',\mathrm{out} |p_1,\ldots,p_n,\mathrm{in}\rangle_{\mathrm{conn}} ={}& \left(\frac{i}{\sqrt Z}\right)^{m+n} \prod_{a=1}^{m}\int d^4x_a\,e^{ip_a'\cdot x_a} (\Box_{x_a}+m^2)\\ &\times \prod_{b=1}^{n}\int d^4y_b\,e^{-ip_b\cdot y_b} (\Box_{y_b}+m^2) G_{m+n}^{\mathrm{conn}}. \end{aligned} \tag{19}\]

The operators \((\Box+m^2)\) cancel the poles of the external propagators. The external momenta are then placed on shell. In diagram language, one calculates connected amputated diagrams and attaches the appropriate external wave functions.

For fermions, the amputation operators are first order, and external spinors remain. This explains why a fermionic amplitude contains factors such as \(\bar u\Gamma u\).

A field is not a particle. A field is a local operator which can create or destroy particle states. The residue \(Z\) measures the overlap between the chosen field and the one-particle state.

6.2 Tree level and loops

The order in the coupling can often be read from the number of vertices. In \(\phi^4\) theory a graph with \(V\) vertices is proportional to \(\lambda^V\). In Yukawa theory it is proportional to \(g^V\).

Tree level means the lowest loop order, not necessarily low energy. A tree amplitude can be used at high energy when perturbation theory remains valid. Conversely, loops can matter at low energy when a tree contribution is forbidden or strongly suppressed.

Internal lines are off shell in general. They are integration variables inside a correlation function and do not satisfy the classical mass-shell relation at every point. Only external asymptotic particles are required to be on shell.

7 From amplitudes to observables

7.1 Lorentz-invariant phase space

For a final state containing \(n\) particles, define

\[ d\Phi_n(P;p_1,\ldots,p_n) =(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}\left(P-\sum_{f=1}^{n}p_f\right) \prod_{f=1}^{n} \frac{d^3\mathbf p_f}{(2\pi)^3,2E_f}. \tag{20}\]

This measure is Lorentz invariant. It combines momentum conservation with the on-shell measure of every final particle.

For a decay of a particle of mass \(M\),

\[ \boxed{d\Gamma=\frac1{2M}|\mathcal M|^2d\Phi_n.} \tag{21}\]

For a two-body decay in the rest frame,

\[ d\Gamma =\frac{|\mathbf p^*|}{32\pi^2M^2} |\mathcal M|^2d\Omega, \]

and after angular integration, when the amplitude is angle-independent,

\[ \Gamma =\frac{|\mathbf p^*|}{8\pi M^2}|\mathcal M|^2. \tag{22}\]

For a scattering process \(1+2\to f\),

\[ d\sigma =\frac{|mathcal M|^2}{4\sqrt{(p_1\cdot p_2)^2-m_1^2m_2^2}} d\Phi_n. \tag{23}\]

For \(2\to2\) scattering in the centre-of-mass frame,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} =\frac1{64\pi^2s} \frac{|\mathbf p_f^*|}{|\mathbf p_i^*|} \overline{|\mathcal M|^2}.} \tag{24}\]

The bar means that initial spins are averaged and final spins are summed. If identical particles occur in the final state, phase space must be divided by the appropriate factorial to avoid counting the same physical configuration more than once.

7.2 Mandelstam variables

For \(1+2\to3+4\),

\[ s=(p_1+p_2)^2, \qquad t=(p_1-p_3)^2, \qquad u=(p_1-p_4)^2. \tag{25}\]

On shell,

\[ s+t+u=m_1^2+m_2^2+m_3^2+m_4^2. \]

\(s\) is the squared centre-of-mass energy. The variables \(t\) and \(u\) measure momentum transfer in the two exchange arrangements. They allow amplitudes to be written in a Lorentz-invariant form.

Crossing symmetry relates processes obtained by moving an external particle from the final state to the initial state and replacing it by the corresponding antiparticle with reversed four-momentum. It is a relation between analytic amplitudes, not simply a rotation of a diagram.

7.3 Spin sums and traces

Unpolarized observables require sums over final spins and averages over initial spins. The completeness relations are

\[ \sum_su_s(p)\bar u_s(p)=\not\!p+m, \]

\[ \sum_sv_s(p)\bar v_s(p)=\not\!p-m. \tag{26}\]

They turn products of spinor bilinears into gamma-matrix traces. Useful identities include

\[ \operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu)=4\eta^{\mu\nu}, \]

\[ \operatorname{Tr} (\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma) =4\left( \eta^{\mu\nu}\eta^{\rho\sigma} -\eta^{\mu\rho}\eta^{\nu\sigma} +\eta^{\mu\sigma}\eta^{\nu\rho} \right), \]

and the trace of an odd number of ordinary gamma matrices vanishes.

For example, if

\[ \mathcal M=g\bar u(p)v(q), \]

then

\[ \sum_{r,s}|\mathcal M|^2 =g^2\operatorname{Tr} \left[(\not\!p+m)(\not\!q-m)\right]. \]

The trace can then be reduced to scalar products of four-momenta.

8 Path-integral formulation

8.1 Generating functional for a scalar field

The same perturbative expansion can be obtained from a functional integral. For a real scalar field,

\[ Z[J] =\int\mathcal D\phi\, \exp\!\left\{i\int d^4x \left[\mathcal L(\phi)+J(x)\phi(x)\right]\right\}. \tag{27}\]

Correlation functions are generated by functional differentiation:

\[ \langle\Omega|T\phi(x_1)\cdots\phi(x_n)|\Omega\rangle =\left. \frac{1}{i^nZ[0]} \frac{\delta^nZ[J]} {\delta J(x_1)\cdots\delta J(x_n)} \right|_{J=0}. \tag{28}\]

For the free theory,

\[ Z_0[J] =Z_0[0]\exp\left[ -\frac{1}{2}\int d^4x\,d^4y\, J(x)\Delta_F(x-y)J(y) \right], \tag{29}\]

where \(\Delta_F\) includes the factor \(i\) as in Equation 13. Differentiating twice with respect to \(J\) produces the free two-point function. The inverse of the quadratic kinetic operator is therefore the propagator.

For \(\phi^4\) theory,

\[ Z[J] =\exp\left[ -i\frac{\lambda}{4!} \int d^4x \left(\frac1i\frac{\delta}{\delta J(x)}\right)^4 \right]Z_0[J]. \tag{30}\]

Expanding the exponential in powers of \(\lambda\) and differentiating reproduces the same vertices, propagators and combinatorial factors as the operator method.

8.2 Fermionic fields

Fermionic path integrals use anticommuting Grassmann variables. With sources \(\eta\) and \(\bar\eta\),

\[ Z_0[\eta,\bar\eta] =\int\mathcal D\psi\mathcal D\bar\psi\, \exp\!\left\{i\int d^4x \left(\bar\psi(i\not\!\partial-m)\psi +\bar\eta\psi+\bar\psi\eta\right)\right\}. \]

The Gaussian integral gives

\[ Z_0[\eta,\bar\eta] =Z_0[0]\exp\left[ -\int d^4x\,d^4y\, \bar\eta(x)S_F(x-y)\eta(y) \right]. \]

The order of Grassmann derivatives matters and produces the fermionic signs. Integrating out a fermion also gives a determinant rather than the inverse square root found for an ordinary bosonic Gaussian integral. This is another way to understand the minus sign associated with a closed fermion loop.

8.3 Wick rotation

The Minkowski weight \(e^{iS}\) is oscillatory. Many formal and numerical calculations become easier after the change

\[ t=-i\tau, \qquad p^0=ip_E^0. \]

For a scalar field,

\[ \frac{i}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon} \longrightarrow \frac{1}{p_E^2+m^2}. \]

The functional weight becomes approximately \(e^{-S_E}\), similar to a statistical Boltzmann factor. The \(i\epsilon\) prescription tells us how the integration contour is rotated without crossing the poles.

9 A practical calculation

For a tree-level process, begin by writing the interaction Lagrangian and fixing the sign conventions. Draw all connected diagrams at the required order. Label the external and internal momenta, with momentum conserved at each vertex. Translate each diagram into factors for vertices, propagators and external states, while keeping the order of fermion matrices and spinors. Add diagrams coherently before taking the modulus squared.

The result of this step is \(i\mathcal M\) after the overall delta function has been removed. Then calculate \(\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}\), including spin sums, spin averages and interference terms. Finally insert the invariant phase space and flux factor, and include any factorial required for identical final particles.

Several distinctions prevent common mistakes. External particles are on shell, while internal momenta are generally off shell. A virtual line is a factor in a perturbative correlation function, not a directly observable particle. A symmetry factor belongs to the combinatorics of a diagram, while the factorial for identical final particles belongs to phase space. Diagrams must be added before squaring because their interference is physical.

The full chain is therefore

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}} \longrightarrow S \longrightarrow\text{Dyson expansion} \longrightarrow\text{Wick contractions} \longrightarrow i\mathcal M \longrightarrow\overline{|\mathcal M|^2} \longrightarrow d\sigma\ \text{or}\ d\Gamma. \]

Feynman diagrams are useful because they compress this chain without replacing it. Their rules come from the action, time ordering and field contractions; their physical predictions come only after the amplitude is combined with the correct external states and phase-space factors.
