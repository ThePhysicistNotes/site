Theoretical electro­magnetism

Exterior algebra and Maxwell’s equations

Magnetic flux is measured through a surface, while electric charge is measured in a volume. A circulation is measured along a curve. Differential forms describe these integrals and keep track of their orientations.

We will use this language to combine the electric and magnetic fields into one field on spacetime. We first need the wedge product and the exterior derivative. The metric will then enter through the Hodge operator.

We use Heaviside–Lorentz units with \(c=\varepsilon_0=\mu_0=1\), coordinates \((t,x,y,z)\), and the conventions

\[ \eta=\operatorname{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1), \qquad \operatorname{vol}=dt\wedge dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. \]

Greek indices run from \(0\) to \(3\) and spatial indices from \(1\) to \(3\). An index repeated once above and once below is summed. The fields are assumed smooth in the region under study.

1 Exterior algebra

1.1 Vectors and covectors

Let \(V\) be an \(n\)-dimensional real vector space. A covector is a linear map \(\alpha:V\to\mathbb R\): it takes a vector as input and returns a number. Covectors form the dual space \(V^\ast\).

If \((e_1,\ldots,e_n)\) is a basis of \(V\), the dual basis satisfies

\[ e^i(e_j)=\delta^i{}_j. \]

For \(v=v^ie_i\) and \(\alpha=\alpha_ie^i\), this gives

\[ \alpha(v)=\alpha_iv^i. \]

Vectors and covectors have different roles. A displacement is a vector; the differential of a function takes a displacement and gives its first-order change. For example, \(df(v)\) is the directional derivative of \(f\) along \(v\).

A non-degenerate metric \(g\) gives a map between the two spaces:

\[ v^\flat=g(v,\cdot), \qquad (v^\flat)_i=g_{ij}v^j, \qquad (\alpha^\sharp)^i=g^{ij}\alpha_j. \]

Here \(\flat\) lowers an index and \(\sharp\) raises it. These maps depend on the metric; the pairing \(\alpha(v)\) does not.

1.2 Alternating forms and the wedge product

A \(p\)-form is a multilinear map from \(p\) vectors to a number. It is alternating, which means that exchanging two arguments changes its sign:

\[ \omega(\ldots,u,\ldots,v,\ldots) =-\omega(\ldots,v,\ldots,u,\ldots). \]

It therefore vanishes when two arguments are equal. The space of these forms is \(\Lambda^p(V^\ast)\). A 0-form is a scalar and a 1-form is a covector.

The wedge product combines a \(p\)-form and a \(q\)-form into a \((p+q)\)-form. For two covectors,

\[ (\alpha\wedge\beta)(u,v) =\alpha(u)\beta(v)-\alpha(v)\beta(u). \]

In coordinates,

\[ \alpha\wedge\beta =\sum_{i<j}(\alpha_i\beta_j-\alpha_j\beta_i)e^i\wedge e^j. \]

For example,

\[ (dx\wedge dy)(u,v)=u_xv_y-u_yv_x. \]

This determinant is the signed area of the two vectors projected onto the coordinate plane. Exchanging \(u\) and \(v\) reverses the orientation and hence the sign.

For general degrees, the product is defined by antisymmetrizing the tensor product:

\[ (\alpha\wedge\beta)_{i_1\ldots i_{p+q}} =\frac{(p+q)!}{p!\,q!} \alpha_{[i_1\ldots i_p}\beta_{i_{p+1}\ldots i_{p+q}]}. \]

Square brackets mean the signed average over permutations; for example, \(T_{[ij]}=(T_{ij}-T_{ji})/2\). The wedge product is bilinear and associative. It obeys

\[ \alpha\wedge\beta=(-1)^{pq}\beta\wedge\alpha. \]

To exchange the two groups, each of the \(p\) factors must pass the \(q\) factors in the other group, giving \(pq\) sign changes. This rule is called graded commutativity.

If \(\omega\) has odd degree, then \(\omega\wedge\omega=-\omega\wedge\omega\), so its square is zero. This need not hold for even degree. In four dimensions, for example,

\[ \omega=e^1\wedge e^2+e^3\wedge e^4 \]

gives

\[ \omega\wedge\omega=2e^1\wedge e^2\wedge e^3\wedge e^4. \]

The individual squares vanish because of repeated factors. The two cross terms are equal because 2-forms commute.

1.3 The Grassmann algebra

A basis of \(\Lambda^p(V^\ast)\) consists of

\[ e^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{i_p}, \qquad 1\leq i_1<\cdots<i_p\leq n. \]

Choosing \(p\) distinct indices gives

\[ \dim\Lambda^p(V^\ast)=\binom np. \]

For \(p>n\), every \(p\)-form vanishes. The direct sum of all degrees, with the wedge product, is the exterior algebra, also called the Grassmann algebra:

\[ \Lambda(V^\ast)=\bigoplus_{p=0}^n\Lambda^p(V^\ast), \qquad \dim\Lambda(V^\ast)=\sum_{p=0}^n\binom np=2^n. \]

Writing the degree-one generators as \(\theta^1,\ldots,\theta^n\) and omitting the wedge symbol gives

\[ \theta^i\theta^j=-\theta^j\theta^i, \qquad (\theta^i)^2=0. \]

Thus every element is a finite sum,

\[ f=f_0+f_i\theta^i+\frac1{2!}f_{ij}\theta^i\theta^j +\cdots+f_{12\ldots n}\theta^1\cdots\theta^n, \]

with antisymmetric coefficients. For differential forms, the coordinate differentials \(dx^i\) play the role of these generators.

NoteGrassmann and Cartan

Grassmann developed exterior algebra in the nineteenth century. Cartan later used differential forms to study geometry. Their algebra is useful here because an oriented surface or volume changes sign when its orientation is reversed.

2 Differential forms

2.1 Forms that vary with position

On a smooth manifold \(M\), a differential \(p\)-form assigns a \(p\)-form to the tangent space at each point. Its coefficients vary smoothly. In coordinates,

\[ \omega=\frac1{p!}\omega_{i_1\ldots i_p}(x) \,dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_p}. \]

The coefficients are antisymmetric. The factor \(1/p!\) removes repeated copies when all index orders are summed. If we sum only over \(i_1<\cdots<i_p\), this factor is omitted.

A magnetic field at a fixed time provides a useful example:

\[ \beta=B_x\,dy\wedge dz+B_y\,dz\wedge dx+B_z\,dx\wedge dy. \]

Its integral over an oriented surface is the magnetic flux. The coefficients describe the field, while the differentials describe which surface directions are being integrated.

2.2 Exterior derivative

The exterior derivative raises the degree by one. For a function,

\[ df=\partial_i f\,dx^i. \]

For a general form,

\[ d\omega=\frac1{p!}\partial_j\omega_{i_1\ldots i_p} \,dx^j\wedge dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_p}, \]

or, in components,

\[ (d\omega)_{i_0i_1\ldots i_p} =(p+1)\partial_{[i_0}\omega_{i_1\ldots i_p]}. \]

In particular, if \(\alpha=\alpha_i dx^i\),

\[ d\alpha=\frac12(\partial_i\alpha_j-\partial_j\alpha_i) \,dx^i\wedge dx^j. \]

The product rule keeps track of the degree:

\[ d(\alpha\wedge\beta) =d\alpha\wedge\beta+(-1)^p\alpha\wedge d\beta, \qquad p=\deg\alpha. \]

A second exterior derivative always vanishes. For a function, this follows from

\[ d(df)=\frac12(\partial_j\partial_i f-\partial_i\partial_j f) \,dx^j\wedge dx^i=0. \]

The partial derivatives commute, while the differentials anticommute. The same cancellation applies to each coefficient of a form, so

\[ d^2=0. \]

A form is closed if \(d\omega=0\) and exact if \(\omega=d\alpha\). Every exact form is closed. For positive degree, the converse holds locally on a sufficiently small contractible region: this is the Poincaré lemma. It may fail on the whole space when the space has holes.

2.3 Pullback, integration and Stokes’ theorem

To integrate a form over a curve or a surface, we first express it in the parameters of that curve or surface. This operation is the pullback.

For a smooth map \(\Phi:M\to N\),

\[ (\Phi^\ast\omega)_x(v_1,\ldots,v_p) =\omega_{\Phi(x)}(d\Phi_xv_1,\ldots,d\Phi_xv_p). \]

In practice, substitute the parametrization and differentiate the coordinate functions. On a circle \(x=R\cos\theta\), \(y=R\sin\theta\), for example,

\[ \Phi^\ast dx=-R\sin\theta\,d\theta, \qquad \Phi^\ast dy=R\cos\theta\,d\theta. \]

The pullback respects products and derivatives:

\[ \Phi^\ast(\alpha\wedge\beta)=\Phi^\ast\alpha\wedge\Phi^\ast\beta, \qquad \Phi^\ast(d\omega)=d(\Phi^\ast\omega). \]

If \(\Phi:U\subset\mathbb R^p\to\Sigma\) is an orientation-preserving parametrization, then

\[ \int_\Sigma\omega=\int_U\Phi^\ast\omega. \]

For a compact oriented \((p+1)\)-dimensional region \(\Sigma\) with boundary, Stokes’ theorem states that

\[ \int_\Sigma d\omega=\int_{\partial\Sigma}\omega. \]

The boundary uses the induced orientation. For a disk oriented by \(dx\wedge dy\), its circle is traversed counterclockwise. This theorem includes the usual circulation and flux theorems of vector calculus.

2.4 Interior product and Lie derivative

For a vector field \(X\), the interior product inserts \(X\) into the first argument of a form:

\[ (\iota_X\omega)(v_2,\ldots,v_p)=\omega(X,v_2,\ldots,v_p). \]

It lowers the degree by one. For example,

\[ \iota_X(dx\wedge dy)=dx(X)\,dy-dy(X)\,dx. \]

More generally,

\[ \iota_X(\alpha\wedge\beta) =(\iota_X\alpha)\wedge\beta+(-1)^p\alpha\wedge\iota_X\beta. \]

The Lie derivative measures the change of a form when it is carried along the flow \(\Phi_s\) of \(X\):

\[ \mathcal L_X\omega =\left.\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}\right|_{s=0}\Phi_s^\ast\omega. \]

For forms, Cartan’s formula gives a way to calculate it:

\[ \mathcal L_X\omega=d(\iota_X\omega)+\iota_X(d\omega). \]

Exercise 6 applies this formula to a dilation.

2.5 The Hodge operator

The previous operations did not need lengths or angles. The Hodge operator uses both a non-degenerate metric and an orientation to map a \(p\)-form to an \((n-p)\)-form. It is defined by

\[ \alpha\wedge\star\beta =\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_g\operatorname{vol}_g, \qquad \langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_g =\frac1{p!}\alpha_{i_1\ldots i_p}\beta^{i_1\ldots i_p}. \]

For example, in oriented Euclidean \(\mathbb R^3\),

\[ \star dx=dy\wedge dz, \qquad \star(dx\wedge dy)=dz. \]

The metric also determines the signs. In Minkowski spacetime, \(dt\wedge dx\) has squared norm \(-1\). Since

\[ (dt\wedge dx)\wedge(-dy\wedge dz)=-\operatorname{vol}, \]

we obtain

\[ \star(dt\wedge dx)=-dy\wedge dz. \]

If the metric has \(s\) negative directions, applying the Hodge operator twice gives

\[ \star^2\big|_{\Omega^p}=(-1)^{p(n-p)+s}\operatorname{id}. \]

Thus \(\star^2=-1\) on 2-forms in four-dimensional Minkowski spacetime. This sign follows from the Lorentzian metric.1

2.6 Codifferential and vector calculus

The formal adjoint of \(d\) is the codifferential \(\delta\), which lowers the degree:

\[ (\delta\omega)_{\mu_2\ldots\mu_p} =-\nabla^{\mu_1}\omega_{\mu_1\mu_2\ldots\mu_p}. \]

Here \(\nabla\) is the covariant derivative compatible with the metric. In flat Cartesian coordinates, it reduces to \(\partial\). The Laplace–de Rham operator is \(d\delta+\delta d\). In these coordinates it acts on each component as \(-\Box\), with \(\Box=-\partial_t^2+\nabla^2\).

In Euclidean \(\mathbb R^3\), associate a vector field \(\mathbf a\) with the 1-form \(a^\flat=a_xdx+a_ydy+a_zdz\). Using the three-dimensional Hodge operator \(\star_3\), we have

\[ df=(\nabla f)^\flat, \qquad \star_3d(a^\flat)=(\nabla\times\mathbf a)^\flat, \qquad \star_3d\star_3(a^\flat)=\nabla\cdot\mathbf a. \]

For example,

\[ \begin{aligned} d(a^\flat)={}&(\partial_ya_z-\partial_za_y)\,dy\wedge dz\\ &+(\partial_za_x-\partial_xa_z)\,dz\wedge dx\\ &+(\partial_xa_y-\partial_ya_x)\,dx\wedge dy. \end{aligned} \]

Applying \(\star_3\) gives the usual curl components. The metric is needed to turn the resulting form back into a vector.

3 Maxwell’s equations on spacetime

3.1 The potential and the field

The scalar potential \(\phi\) and vector potential \(\mathbf A\) form the spacetime 1-form

\[ A=-\phi\,dt+A_xdx+A_ydy+A_zdz. \]

Define the electromagnetic field by \(F=dA\). We will discuss below when a potential can be defined globally. First calculate

\[ d(-\phi\,dt)=-d\phi\wedge dt =\partial_i\phi\,dt\wedge dx^i \]

and

\[ d(A_i dx^i) =\partial_tA_i\,dt\wedge dx^i+\partial_jA_i\,dx^j\wedge dx^i. \]

Combining these terms gives

\[ F=(\partial_i\phi+\partial_tA_i)\,dt\wedge dx^i +\frac12(\partial_iA_j-\partial_jA_i)\,dx^i\wedge dx^j. \]

Using \(\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_t\mathbf A\) and \(\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A\),

\[ \begin{aligned} F={}&-E_x\,dt\wedge dx-E_y\,dt\wedge dy-E_z\,dt\wedge dz\\ &+B_x\,dy\wedge dz+B_y\,dz\wedge dx+B_z\,dx\wedge dy. \end{aligned} \]

The terms containing time describe the electric field. The spatial terms describe magnetic flux. Together they form a single 2-form.

Writing \(F=\frac12F_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu\wedge dx^\nu\) gives

\[ (F_{\mu\nu})= \begin{pmatrix} 0&-E_x&-E_y&-E_z\\ E_x&0&B_z&-B_y\\ E_y&-B_z&0&B_x\\ E_z&B_y&-B_x&0 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Antisymmetry leaves six independent components.

3.2 Hodge dual and the observer

The definition of \(\star\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \star(dt\wedge dx)&=-dy\wedge dz, &\star(dy\wedge dz)&=dt\wedge dx,\\ \star(dt\wedge dy)&=-dz\wedge dx, &\star(dz\wedge dx)&=dt\wedge dy,\\ \star(dt\wedge dz)&=-dx\wedge dy, &\star(dx\wedge dy)&=dt\wedge dz. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \star F={}&B_x\,dt\wedge dx+B_y\,dt\wedge dy+B_z\,dt\wedge dz\\ &+E_x\,dy\wedge dz+E_y\,dz\wedge dx+E_z\,dx\wedge dy. \end{aligned} \]

An observer has a future-directed four-velocity \(u\) with \(g(u,u)=-1\). The electric and magnetic fields measured by this observer are represented by the spatial 1-forms

\[ E_u=-\iota_uF, \qquad B_u=\iota_u\star F. \]

Both vanish when evaluated on \(u\), so they lie in the observer’s rest space. For \(u=\partial_t\), they give \(E_xdx+E_ydy+E_zdz\) and \(B_xdx+B_ydy+B_zdz\). Changing the observer changes the split into electric and magnetic parts of the same \(F\).

3.3 Current as a 3-form

The current four-vector and its associated covector are

\[ j=\rho\,\partial_t+j_x\partial_x+j_y\partial_y+j_z\partial_z, \qquad j^\flat=-\rho\,dt+j_xdx+j_ydy+j_zdz. \]

Define the current 3-form by \(J=\star j^\flat\). Its explicit expression is

\[ \begin{aligned} J={}&\rho\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz -j_x\,dt\wedge dy\wedge dz\\ &-j_y\,dt\wedge dz\wedge dx -j_z\,dt\wedge dx\wedge dy. \end{aligned} \]

The degree is three because charge is integrated over a three-dimensional spatial slice. On \(t=\text{constant}\), the pullback sets \(dt=0\), and

\[ Q=\int_\Sigma J=\int_\Sigma\rho\,dx\,dy\,dz. \]

The terms containing \(dt\) describe charge crossing a boundary during an interval of time.

3.4 Recovering the four vector equations

In vacuum, allowing electric sources but no magnetic charge, Maxwell’s equations are

\[ dF=0, \qquad d\star F=J. \]

To expand them, write \(\mathbf C\cdot d\mathbf S=C_xdy\wedge dz+C_ydz\wedge dx+C_zdx\wedge dy\). Direct differentiation gives

\[ dF=(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz +dt\wedge(\partial_t\mathbf B+\nabla\times\mathbf E)\cdot d\mathbf S. \]

For instance, the coefficient of \(dt\wedge dy\wedge dz\) is

\[ \partial_tB_x+\partial_yE_z-\partial_zE_y. \]

The different basis 3-forms are independent, so \(dF=0\) gives

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0, \qquad \nabla\times\mathbf E+\partial_t\mathbf B=0. \]

Similarly,

\[ d\star F=(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz +dt\wedge(\partial_t\mathbf E-\nabla\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\mathbf S. \]

Comparing with \(J=\rho\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz-dt\wedge\mathbf j\cdot d\mathbf S\) gives

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho, \qquad \nabla\times\mathbf B-\partial_t\mathbf E=\mathbf j. \]

The same equations in tensor notation are

\[ \partial_{[\lambda}F_{\mu\nu]}=0, \qquad \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=-j^\nu. \]

The minus sign in the second equation follows from our definition of \(F\). For \(\nu=0\), \(F^{i0}=-E_i\), so the equation reads \(-\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=-\rho\).

In SI units, the two homogeneous vector equations are unchanged, while the source equations become

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \nabla\times\mathbf B-\frac1{c^2}\partial_t\mathbf E=\mu_0\mathbf j. \]

3.5 Charge conservation

Applying \(d\) to \(d\star F=J\) gives

\[ dJ=d^2\star F=0. \]

From the expression for \(J\),

\[ dJ=(\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j) \,dt\wedge dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. \]

Thus,

\[ \partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=0. \]

For a fixed spatial volume \(V\), integration gives

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\int_V\rho\,d^3x =-\int_{\partial V}\mathbf j\cdot d\mathbf S. \]

Charge decreases inside the volume by exactly the amount that flows out. More generally, Stokes’ theorem gives \(\int_{\partial\mathcal U}J=0\) for a spacetime region \(\mathcal U\).

3.6 Gauge freedom and the wave equation

If \(F=dA\), then \(dF=0\) follows from \(d^2=0\). Conversely, if \(dF=0\), the Poincaré lemma provides a potential on a contractible region.

The potential is not unique. For any smooth function \(\chi\),

\[ A'=A+d\chi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad dA'=dA+d^2\chi=F. \]

In terms of the scalar and vector potentials,

\[ \phi'=\phi-\partial_t\chi, \qquad \mathbf A'=\mathbf A+\nabla\chi. \]

A gauge transformation therefore changes the potential without changing the electromagnetic field.

One useful choice is the Lorenz gauge,

\[ \delta A=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \partial_\mu A^\mu=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \partial_t\phi+\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0. \]

Substituting \(F^{\mu\nu}=\partial^\mu A^\nu-\partial^\nu A^\mu\) into the source equation gives

\[ \Box A^\nu-\partial^\nu(\partial_\mu A^\mu)=-j^\nu, \qquad \Box=-\partial_t^2+\nabla^2. \]

The gauge condition removes the second term, leaving

\[ \Box A^\nu=-j^\nu. \]

Starting from a potential with nonzero divergence, imposing this gauge amounts to solving \(\Box\chi=-\partial_\mu A^\mu\), with suitable boundary or initial conditions. A further transformation preserves the Lorenz gauge when \(\Box\chi=0\).

4 Dynamics and geometry

4.1 Lorentz force

For a particle of charge \(q\) and mass \(m\), let \(u^\mu=dx^\mu/d\tau\). The relativistic equation of motion is

\[ m\frac{du^\mu}{d\tau}=qF^\mu{}_\nu u^\nu. \]

With the insertion convention used above, this is also

\[ m\nabla_u u=-q(\iota_uF)^\sharp. \]

The minus sign follows from \(F_{\nu\mu}=-F_{\mu\nu}\). In an inertial frame, write \(u^\mu=\gamma(1,\mathbf v)\) and \(p^\mu=mu^\mu\). Since \(dt/d\tau=\gamma\), the spatial components give

\[ \frac{dp^i}{dt} =q(F^i{}_0+F^i{}_jv^j) =q\left(E_i+\epsilon_{ijk}v^jB_k\right). \]

Hence

\[ \frac{d\mathbf p}{dt}=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B). \]

The time component gives

\[ \frac{d\mathcal E}{dt}=q\,\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v. \]

The magnetic force does no work because it is perpendicular to the velocity.

4.2 Action principle

For a prescribed current, take

\[ S[A]=-\frac12\int_M F\wedge\star F+\int_M A\wedge J, \qquad F=dA. \]

The source term is the familiar potential-current coupling: \(A\wedge J=A_\mu j^\mu\operatorname{vol}\). We hold the metric and the current fixed and vary \(A\) to \(A+\varepsilon a\), where \(a\) is a 1-form vanishing on the boundary. Then the first-order field change is \(da\).

Because the metric pairing is symmetric,

\[ \left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_0 \frac12(F+\varepsilon da)\wedge\star(F+\varepsilon da) =da\wedge\star F. \]

The first variation is therefore

\[ \delta S=-\int_M da\wedge\star F+\int_M a\wedge J. \]

Use the product rule,

\[ d(a\wedge\star F)=da\wedge\star F-a\wedge d\star F, \]

to integrate by parts:

\[ \delta S=-\int_{\partial M}a\wedge\star F +\int_M a\wedge(J-d\star F). \]

The boundary term vanishes. Since \(a\) is arbitrary in the interior, stationarity gives \(d\star F=J\). The other equation, \(dF=0\), is already built into \(F=dA\).

4.3 Gauge symmetry and the current

Under \(A\mapsto A+d\chi\), the field term is unchanged. The source term changes by

\[ \Delta S_{\mathrm{int}}=\int_M d\chi\wedge J. \]

Since \(d(\chi J)=d\chi\wedge J+\chi\,dJ\),

\[ \Delta S_{\mathrm{int}} =\int_{\partial M}\chi J-\int_M\chi\,dJ. \]

For \(\chi\) with compact support inside \(M\), the boundary term is zero. Invariance for every such \(\chi\) requires \(dJ=0\). A prescribed current must therefore be conserved for its coupling to respect gauge freedom.

4.4 Lorentz invariants

The metric contraction gives

\[ F\wedge\star F =\frac12F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\operatorname{vol} =(\mathbf B^2-\mathbf E^2)\operatorname{vol}. \]

The electric terms contribute negatively because they contain a time index. The spatial magnetic terms contribute positively.

In \(F\wedge F\), two purely spatial 2-forms give zero in three spatial dimensions, and two electric terms repeat \(dt\). Only electric-magnetic cross terms remain. For the \(x\) components, for example,

\[ 2(-E_x\,dt\wedge dx)\wedge(B_x\,dy\wedge dz) =-2E_xB_x\,\operatorname{vol}. \]

Adding the three directions gives

\[ F\wedge F=-2(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B)\operatorname{vol}. \]

Thus \(\mathbf B^2-\mathbf E^2\) and \(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B\) are invariant under proper Lorentz transformations. The latter changes sign under an orientation-reversing transformation.

These quantities constrain what another observer can measure. If \(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B=0\) and \(B^2>E^2\), a frame exists in which \(\mathbf E'=0\). If \(E^2>B^2\), a frame exists in which \(\mathbf B'=0\). Exercise 11 finds these frames explicitly. When both invariants vanish and \(F\neq0\), the field is null; a plane wave is an example. If \(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B\neq0\), neither field can vanish in any inertial frame.

4.5 Electric-magnetic duality

In a region with no sources, \(dF=d\star F=0\). For a constant angle \(\theta\), define

\[ F_\theta=F\cos\theta+\star F\sin\theta. \]

Since \(\star^2=-1\),

\[ \star F_\theta=\star F\cos\theta-F\sin\theta. \]

Taking \(d\) shows that both source-free equations are still satisfied. Reading the components from \(F\) and \(\star F\) gives

\[ \mathbf E_\theta=\mathbf E\cos\theta-\mathbf B\sin\theta, \qquad \mathbf B_\theta=\mathbf B\cos\theta+\mathbf E\sin\theta. \]

In particular, \(\theta=\pi/2\) sends \((\mathbf E,\mathbf B)\) to \((-\mathbf B,\mathbf E)\). Electric sources without magnetic sources do not share this symmetry.

After allowing complex coefficients, define the two parts

\[ F_+=\frac12(F-i\star F), \qquad F_-=\frac12(F+i\star F). \]

They satisfy

\[ F=F_++F_-, \qquad \star F_+=iF_+, \qquad \star F_-=-iF_-. \]

The factors of \(i\) are needed because the square of \(\star\) is \(-1\). These are called the self-dual and anti-self-dual parts. Problem 2 studies their transformation under duality.

4.6 Energy and momentum

The electromagnetic stress-energy tensor is

\[ T^{\mu\nu} =F^{\mu\rho}F^\nu{}_\rho -\frac14\eta^{\mu\nu}F^{\rho\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma}. \]

Using \(F^{0i}=E_i\) and \(F^{\rho\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma}=2(B^2-E^2)\) gives

\[ T^{00} =E^2+\frac12(B^2-E^2) =\frac12(E^2+B^2). \]

The mixed components are

\[ T^{0i}=E_j\epsilon_{ijk}B_k=(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)_i. \]

Thus the energy density is \(u=(E^2+B^2)/2\), and the energy flux is the Poynting vector \(\mathbf S=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B\).

The energy balance can be derived directly from Maxwell’s equations. Dot the Ampère–Maxwell equation with \(\mathbf E\) and Faraday’s equation with \(\mathbf B\), then subtract:

\[ \mathbf E\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B) -\mathbf B\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf E) =\mathbf j\cdot\mathbf E+\partial_t\left[\frac12(E^2+B^2)\right]. \]

Using \(\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B) =\mathbf B\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf E)-\mathbf E\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\) gives

\[ \partial_t u+\nabla\cdot\mathbf S=-\mathbf j\cdot\mathbf E. \]

The field loses the energy given to the charges. The full covariant balance is

\[ \partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=-F^{\nu\rho}j_\rho, \]

whose right-hand side is minus the force density acting on matter.

4.7 Material media

In a material, it is useful to distinguish the field \(F\) from an excitation 2-form \(G\):

\[ dF=0, \qquad dG=J. \]

Write

\[ G=dt\wedge(H_xdx+H_ydy+H_zdz) +D_xdy\wedge dz+D_ydz\wedge dx+D_zdx\wedge dy. \]

The equation \(dG=J\) then gives \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho\) and \(\nabla\times\mathbf H-\partial_t\mathbf D=\mathbf j\), where \(J\) represents the free sources. In vacuum, our units give \(\mathbf D=\mathbf E\), \(\mathbf H=\mathbf B\) and \(G=\star F\).

A constitutive law relates \(G\) to \(F\). For a linear local medium, it has the form \(G=\chi(F)\); for an isotropic medium, this includes \(\mathbf D=\varepsilon\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H\). A dispersive medium requires a relation that also depends on the history of the field.

The exterior equations express the conservation laws. The vacuum metric or the response of the material enters through the constitutive law.

4.8 Local potentials and topology

A closed field need not have one potential valid everywhere. The distinction is recorded by de Rham cohomology:

\[ H^p_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) =\frac{\{\text{closed }p\text{-forms}\}} {\{\text{exact }p\text{-forms}\}}. \]

A global potential exists precisely when the class \([F]\in H^2_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\) is zero. On a contractible region this is automatic. On a space with a hole, it may fail.

For example, a magnetic monopole would give a nonzero flux through a sphere around the source. If a smooth potential existed on the entire sphere, Stokes’ theorem would give

\[ \int_{S^2}F=\int_{S^2}dA=\int_{\partial S^2}A=0, \]

because the sphere has no boundary. The nonzero flux prevents such a global potential. Different patches need different potentials, connected by gauge transformations. Problem 3 works out this construction.

The potential also enters the quantum phase of a charged particle. Under \(A\mapsto A+d\chi\), the wave function transforms as \(\psi\mapsto e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\psi\). The derivative

\[ D\psi=\left(d-\frac{iq}{\hbar}A\right)\psi \]

transforms by the same phase. This is the sense in which \(A\) is a \(U(1)\) gauge connection: it lets us compare phases at nearby points while keeping the description independent of the gauge choice. Its curvature is proportional to the electromagnetic field:

\[ D^2\psi=-\frac{iq}{\hbar}F\,\psi. \]

Around a closed path, the phase factor is

\[ \exp\left(\frac{iq}{\hbar}\oint A\right). \]

In the Aharonov–Bohm setting, the accessible region has \(F=0\), but a path around an excluded magnetic flux can still have a nontrivial phase factor. Locally the potential can be removed by a gauge choice; globally one gauge choice need not cover the whole region.

5.1 Exercise 1 — Wedge-product calculations

In a four-dimensional vector space, consider a dual basis

\[ (e^0,e^1,e^2,e^3) \]

and the forms

\[ \alpha=e^0+2e^1, \qquad \beta=e^1-e^2, \]

\[ \gamma=e^0\wedge e^2+3e^1\wedge e^3. \]

  1. Calculate \(\alpha\wedge\beta\).
  2. Deduce \(\beta\wedge\alpha\).
  3. Calculate \(\alpha\wedge\alpha\).
  4. Calculate \(\gamma\wedge\gamma\) and explain why this result does not have to vanish.
NoteSolution

By bilinearity,

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha\wedge\beta &=(e^0+2e^1)\wedge(e^1-e^2)\\ &=e^0\wedge e^1-e^0\wedge e^2 +2e^1\wedge e^1-2e^1\wedge e^2. \end{aligned} \]

Since \(e^1\wedge e^1=0\),

\[ \alpha\wedge\beta =e^0\wedge e^1-e^0\wedge e^2-2e^1\wedge e^2. \]

Two 1-forms anticommute, so

\[ \beta\wedge\alpha=-\alpha\wedge\beta. \]

Similarly,

\[ \alpha\wedge\alpha=0. \]

For \(\gamma\), let

\[ u=e^0\wedge e^2, \qquad v=3e^1\wedge e^3. \]

Then \(u\wedge u=v\wedge v=0\) because of repeated factors. However, \(u\) and \(v\) have degree two, so

\[ u\wedge v=(-1)^{2\times2}v\wedge u=v\wedge u. \]

Therefore,

\[ \gamma\wedge\gamma=2u\wedge v =6e^0\wedge e^2\wedge e^1\wedge e^3. \]

We must exchange \(e^2\) and \(e^1\) to put the indices in order:

\[ \gamma\wedge\gamma =-6e^0\wedge e^1\wedge e^2\wedge e^3. \]

For even degree, swapping the factors gives a plus sign. The square need not vanish.

5.2 Exercise 2 — Structure of a Grassmann algebra

Consider five Grassmann generators \(\theta^1,\ldots,\theta^5\).

  1. Give the dimension of the space of homogeneous elements of degrees \(0\), \(1\), \(2\), \(3\), \(4\) and \(5\).
  2. Deduce the total dimension of the algebra.
  3. Show that the even and odd subspaces have the same dimension.
  4. Show that every homogeneous element of odd degree has zero square.

\[ \dim\Lambda^p=\binom5p. \]

For degrees \(0,1,2,3,4,5\), the dimensions are respectively \(1,5,10,10,5,1\).

The total dimension is

\[ 1+5+10+10+5+1=32=2^5. \]

The even part has dimension

\[ \binom50+\binom52+\binom54=1+10+5=16, \]

and the odd part

\[ \binom51+\binom53+\binom55=5+10+1=16. \]

5.3 Exercise 3 — Decomposability of a 2-form

In a four-dimensional space, a non-zero 2-form is decomposable if it can be written as

\[ \omega=\alpha\wedge\beta. \]

You may use the following criterion in four dimensions: a nonzero 2-form is decomposable if and only if

\[ \omega\wedge\omega=0. \]

Consider

\[ \omega =e^1\wedge e^2+e^1\wedge e^3 +a\,e^3\wedge e^4+b\,e^2\wedge e^4. \]

  1. Calculate \(\omega\wedge\omega\).
  2. For what relation between \(a\) and \(b\) is the form decomposable?
  3. When this relation holds, factorize \(\omega\) explicitly.
NoteSolution

The square of each of the four terms is zero. Most cross products also vanish because they contain the same 1-form twice.

The only non-zero products are

\[ (e^1\wedge e^2)\wedge(ae^3\wedge e^4) =a\,e^1\wedge e^2\wedge e^3\wedge e^4 \]

and

\[ (e^1\wedge e^3)\wedge(be^2\wedge e^4) =b\,e^1\wedge e^3\wedge e^2\wedge e^4. \]

The second term requires exchanging \(e^3\) and \(e^2\), so

\[ (e^1\wedge e^3)\wedge(be^2\wedge e^4) =-b\,e^1\wedge e^2\wedge e^3\wedge e^4. \]

Each cross product appears twice because 2-forms commute with one another. Therefore,

\[ \omega\wedge\omega =2(a-b)e^1\wedge e^2\wedge e^3\wedge e^4. \]

The decomposability condition is therefore

\[ a=b. \]

When \(a=b\), we group the terms:

\[ \begin{aligned} \omega &=e^1\wedge(e^2+e^3) +a(e^2+e^3)\wedge e^4\\ &=(e^1-ae^4)\wedge(e^2+e^3). \end{aligned} \]

The last equality holds because

\[ -ae^4\wedge(e^2+e^3) =a(e^2+e^3)\wedge e^4. \]

We have therefore obtained a simple form, that is, the wedge product of two 1-forms.

5.4 Exercise 4 — Exterior derivative and nilpotence

On \(\mathbb R^3\) with coordinates \((x,y,z)\), consider

\[ \alpha =xyz\,dx+x^2z\,dy+xy^2\,dz. \]

  1. Calculate \(d\alpha\).
  2. Check directly that \(d(d\alpha)=0\).
  3. Explain why this result was predictable before any calculation.
NoteSolution

Write

\[ \alpha=Pdx+Qdy+Rdz \]

with

\[ P=xyz, \qquad Q=x^2z, \qquad R=xy^2. \]

The exterior derivative is

\[ d\alpha =(\partial_xQ-\partial_yP)dx\wedge dy +(\partial_xR-\partial_zP)dx\wedge dz +(\partial_yR-\partial_zQ)dy\wedge dz. \]

Calculate the three coefficients:

\[ \partial_xQ-\partial_yP=2xz-xz=xz, \]

\[ \partial_xR-\partial_zP=y^2-xy, \]

\[ \partial_yR-\partial_zQ=2xy-x^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ d\alpha =xz\,dx\wedge dy +(y^2-xy)dx\wedge dz +(2xy-x^2)dy\wedge dz. \]

\[ \beta=A\,dx\wedge dy+B\,dx\wedge dz+C\,dy\wedge dz, \]

we have

\[ d\beta =(\partial_xC-\partial_yB+\partial_zA) dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. \]

Here,

\[ \partial_xC=2y-2x, \]

\[ \partial_yB=2y-x, \]

\[ \partial_zA=x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \partial_xC-\partial_yB+\partial_zA =(2y-2x)-(2y-x)+x=0. \]

Thus,

\[ d(d\alpha)=0. \]

5.5 Exercise 5 — Stokes’ theorem on a disk

In the plane oriented by \(dx\wedge dy\), consider the 1-form

\[ \omega=\frac12(x\,dy-y\,dx). \]

Let \(D_R\) be the disk of radius \(R\) with positive orientation, and let \(C_R=\partial D_R\) be its boundary circle.

  1. Calculate \(d\omega\).
  2. Parametrize \(C_R\) and directly calculate \(\displaystyle\oint_{C_R}\omega\).
  3. Recover the result using Stokes’ theorem.
NoteSolution

We have

\[ d(x\,dy)=dx\wedge dy, \]

and

\[ d(y\,dx)=dy\wedge dx=-dx\wedge dy. \]

Therefore,

\[ d\omega =\frac12(dx\wedge dy-dy\wedge dx) =dx\wedge dy. \]

Thus,

\[ d\omega=dx\wedge dy. \]

Parametrize the circle counterclockwise:

\[ x=R\cos\theta, \qquad y=R\sin\theta, \qquad 0\leq\theta\leq2\pi. \]

Then

\[ dx=-R\sin\theta\,d\theta, \qquad dy=R\cos\theta\,d\theta. \]

The pullback of \(\omega\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \omega|_{C_R} &=\frac12\left[ R\cos\theta(R\cos\theta\,d\theta) -R\sin\theta(-R\sin\theta\,d\theta) \right]\\ &=\frac{R^2}{2}d\theta. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \oint_{C_R}\omega =\frac{R^2}{2}\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta =\pi R^2. \]

Stokes’ theorem gives directly

\[ \oint_{C_R}\omega =\int_{D_R}d\omega =\int_{D_R}dx\wedge dy =\operatorname{area}(D_R) =\pi R^2. \]

The form \(\omega\) is therefore a local potential for the area form.

\[ \iota_X\omega=\omega(X). \]

The useful Hodge relations are

\[ \star(dt\wedge dx)=-dy\wedge dz, \]

\[ \star F =E\,dy\wedge dz+B\,dt\wedge dx. \]

5.11 Exercise 11 — Classifying a field using its invariants

Consider uniform orthogonal fields

\[ \mathbf E=E_0\mathbf e_x, \qquad \mathbf B=B_0\mathbf e_y, \]

with \(E_0>0\) and \(B_0>0\).

  1. Calculate the two Lorentz invariants.
  2. If \(B_0>E_0\), find the velocity of a reference frame in which the electric field vanishes.
  3. Determine the magnetic field in this reference frame.
  4. Treat the case \(E_0>B_0\) in the same way.
  5. What happens when \(E_0=B_0\)?
NoteSolution

Since the fields are orthogonal,

\[ \mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B=0. \]

The other invariant is

\[ \mathbf B^2-\mathbf E^2=B_0^2-E_0^2. \]

First suppose that \(B_0>E_0\). For a change to a reference frame with velocity \(\mathbf v\) perpendicular to both fields, the transformations are

\[ \mathbf E'=\gamma(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B), \]

\[ \mathbf B'=\gamma(\mathbf B-\mathbf v\times\mathbf E), \]

where

\[ \gamma=\frac1{\sqrt{1-v^2}}. \]

\[ \mathbf v=\frac{\mathbf E\times\mathbf B}{B^2} =\frac{E_0}{B_0}\mathbf e_z. \]

This velocity is allowed because \(E_0/B_0<1\). Moreover,

\[ \mathbf v\times\mathbf B =\frac{E_0}{B_0}\mathbf e_z\times B_0\mathbf e_y =-E_0\mathbf e_x=-\mathbf E. \]

Therefore \(\mathbf E'=0\).

For the magnetic field,

\[ \mathbf v\times\mathbf E =\frac{E_0^2}{B_0}\mathbf e_y. \]

Thus,

\[ \mathbf B' =\gamma\left(B_0-\frac{E_0^2}{B_0}\right)\mathbf e_y. \]

\[ \gamma =\frac1{\sqrt{1-E_0^2/B_0^2}} =\frac{B_0}{\sqrt{B_0^2-E_0^2}}. \]

\[ \mathbf B'=\sqrt{B_0^2-E_0^2}\,\mathbf e_y. \]

\[ \mathbf v=\frac{\mathbf E\times\mathbf B}{E^2} =\frac{B_0}{E_0}\mathbf e_z. \]

This time \(\mathbf B'=0\) and

\[ \mathbf E'=\sqrt{E_0^2-B_0^2}\,\mathbf e_x. \]

5.12 Exercise 12 — Variation of the action and conserved current

\[ S[A] =-\frac12\int_MdA\wedge\star dA +\int_MA\wedge J. \]

Let \(F=dA\). Under the proposed variation,

\[ \delta F=da. \]

The symmetry of the inner product induced by \(\star\) gives

\[ \delta\left(\frac12F\wedge\star F\right) =da\wedge\star F. \]

Thus,

\[ \delta S =-\int_Mda\wedge\star F+\int_Ma\wedge J. \]

The graded Leibniz rule gives

\[ d(a\wedge\star F) =da\wedge\star F-a\wedge d\star F. \]

We deduce

\[ \delta S =-\int_{\partial M}a\wedge\star F +\int_Ma\wedge(J-d\star F). \]

\[ \delta S=\int_Ma\wedge(J-d\star F). \]

This quantity must vanish for every 1-form \(a\). The fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations then requires

\[ d\star F=J. \]

\[ \Delta S=\int_Md\chi\wedge J. \]

\[ d(\chi J)=d\chi\wedge J+\chi dJ. \]

Therefore,

\[ \Delta S =\int_{\partial M}\chi J-\int_M\chi dJ. \]

\[ \Delta S=-\int_M\chi dJ. \]

\[ dJ=0. \]

\[ dF =i\,k\wedge f\,e^{ik\cdot x}. \]

Similarly,

\[ d\star F =i\,k\wedge\star f\,e^{ik\cdot x}. \]

The source-free equations therefore give

\[ k\wedge f=0,\qquad k\wedge\star f=0. \]

Choose a basis of covectors whose first element is \(k\). Every 2-form can then be written uniquely as

\[ f=k\wedge a+b, \]

where \(a\) is a combination of the other basis covectors and \(b\) contains no factor \(k\). The condition

\[ k\wedge f=k\wedge b=0 \]

requires \(b=0\), because the 3-forms obtained by adding \(k\) to the components of \(b\) are linearly independent. Therefore,

\[ f=k\wedge a. \]

The second equation can be rewritten, up to a sign that does not matter for a homogeneous equation, as

\[ \iota_{k^\sharp}f=0. \]

In components,

\[ f_{\mu\nu}=k_\mu a_\nu-k_\nu a_\mu. \]

By contraction,

\[ \begin{aligned} k^\mu f_{\mu\nu} &=k^\mu k_\mu a_\nu-k^\mu a_\mu k_\nu\\ &=k^2a_\nu-(k\cdot a)k_\nu. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ k^2a-(k\cdot a)k=0. \]

If \(k^2\neq0\), this relation requires

\[ a=\frac{k\cdot a}{k^2}k, \]

therefore

\[ f=k\wedge a=0. \]

A non-trivial solution therefore requires

\[ k^2=0. \]

The remaining equation becomes

\[ -(k\cdot a)k=0, \]

which gives

\[ k\cdot a=0. \]

The wave four-vector is null: \(k^2=0\) gives the dispersion relation for propagation at the speed of light.

The transformation

\[ a\longmapsto a+\lambda k \]

does not change \(f\), because

\[ k\wedge(a+\lambda k)=k\wedge a. \]

The condition \(k\cdot a=0\) leaves three independent components of \(a\), one of which is exactly the redundant direction proportional to \(k\). Therefore, there remain

\[ 3-1=2 \]

physical polarizations.

For the invariants, we have

\[ f\wedge f =(k\wedge a)\wedge(k\wedge a)=0 \]

because of the repeated factor \(k\). Moreover,

\[ \frac12f_{\mu\nu}f^{\mu\nu} =k^2a^2-(k\cdot a)^2=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ F\wedge F=0,\qquad F\wedge\star F=0. \]

Now take

\[ k=-\omega\,dt+\omega\,dz, \]

for which

\[ k^2=-\omega^2+\omega^2=0. \]

We can choose

\[ a=a_xdx+a_ydy. \]

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} f={}&-\omega a_xdt\wedge dx-\omega a_ydt\wedge dy\\ &+\omega a_xdz\wedge dx-\omega a_ydy\wedge dz. \end{aligned} \]

The complex electric and magnetic amplitudes are

\[ \mathbf E_0=\omega(a_x\mathbf e_x+a_y\mathbf e_y), \qquad \mathbf B_0=\omega(-a_y\mathbf e_x+a_x\mathbf e_y). \]

Thus \(\mathbf B_0=\mathbf e_z\times\mathbf E_0\). Taking the real parts after multiplying by \(e^{ik\cdot x}\) gives the same relation for the physical fields:

\[ \mathbf B(x)=\mathbf e_z\times\mathbf E(x), \qquad \mathbf E(x)\cdot\mathbf B(x)=0, \qquad |\mathbf E(x)|=|\mathbf B(x)|. \]

This also verifies that the two invariants vanish for the real field, including elliptical polarization.

To calculate the energy-momentum tensor of the real field, write

\[ b(x)=\operatorname{Re}\!\left(ae^{ik\cdot x}\right), \qquad F_{\mathrm{phys}}=k\wedge b(x). \]

Because \(k\) is real, \(k\cdot b=0\). Using \(k^2=0\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} F_{\mathrm{phys}}^{\mu\rho}F_{\mathrm{phys}}^\nu{}_{\rho} &=(k^\mu b^\rho-k^\rho b^\mu) (k^\nu b_\rho-k_\rho b^\nu)\\ &=b^2k^\mu k^\nu. \end{aligned} \]

The scalar invariant is zero, so

\[ T^{\mu\nu}=b(x)^2k^\mu k^\nu. \]

Energy and momentum travel along \(k^\mu\). For a real transverse amplitude \(a\), the wave is linearly polarized and \(b=a\cos(k\cdot x)\). Then

\[ T^{\mu\nu}=a^2k^\mu k^\nu\cos^2(k\cdot x), \qquad \langle T^{\mu\nu}\rangle=\frac12a^2k^\mu k^\nu. \]

6.2 Problem 2 — Duality and self-dual decomposition

In a source-free region, define

\[ F_\theta=F\cos\theta+\star F\sin\theta. \]

  1. Show that \(F_\theta\) satisfies Maxwell’s equations if \(F\) does.

  2. On the complexified space of 2-forms, introduce

    \[ P_+=\frac12(1-i\star), \qquad P_-=\frac12(1+i\star). \]

    Show that these are complementary projectors.

  3. For

    \[ F_+=P_+F, \qquad F_-=P_-F, \]

    show that

    \[ \star F_+=iF_+, \qquad \star F_-=-iF_-. \]

  4. Show that duality acts through opposite phases on \(F_+\) and \(F_-\).

  5. Study the transformation of \(F\wedge\star F\) and \(F\wedge F\).

  6. Show that the energy density and Poynting vector are invariant.

  7. Explain why duality is an obvious symmetry of the equations, but more subtle in an action written with a single potential \(A\).

NoteSolution

In vacuum,

\[ dF=0, \qquad d\star F=0. \]

We calculate

\[ dF_\theta =\cos\theta\,dF+\sin\theta\,d\star F=0. \]

Moreover, since \(\star^2=-1\) on 2-forms,

\[ \star F_\theta =\star F\cos\theta-F\sin\theta. \]

Therefore,

\[ d\star F_\theta =\cos\theta\,d\star F-\sin\theta\,dF=0. \]

Duality does preserve the source-free equations.

For the projectors, We have

\[ P_+^2 =\frac14(1-2i\star-\star^2) =\frac12(1-i\star) =P_+, \]

because \(\star^2=-1\). Similarly,

\[ P_-^2=P_-. \]

Moreover,

\[ P_+P_- =\frac14(1+\star^2)=0, \]

and

\[ P_++P_-=1. \]

They therefore decompose every complex 2-form into two complementary subspaces.

For the positive part,

\[ \begin{aligned} \star F_+ &=\frac12(\star F-i\star^2F)\\ &=\frac12(\star F+iF)\\ &=iF_+. \end{aligned} \]

Similarly,

\[ \star F_-=-iF_-. \]

Under a duality rotation,

\[ \begin{aligned} (F_\theta)_+ &=\frac12(F_\theta-i\star F_\theta)\\ &=e^{i\theta}F_+, \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ (F_\theta)_-=e^{-i\theta}F_-. \]

Define

\[ I=F\wedge\star F, \qquad K=F\wedge F. \]

The identities

\[ \star F\wedge F=F\wedge\star F, \]

and

\[ \star F\wedge\star F=-F\wedge F \]

give, after expansion,

\[ I_\theta=I\cos(2\theta)-K\sin(2\theta), \]

\[ K_\theta=K\cos(2\theta)+I\sin(2\theta). \]

The two invariants therefore transform like a vector rotating through an angle \(2\theta\).

Reading the coefficients of \(dt\wedge dx^i\) and of the spatial 2-forms gives

\[ \mathbf E_\theta=\mathbf E\cos\theta-\mathbf B\sin\theta, \]

\[ \mathbf B_\theta=\mathbf B\cos\theta+\mathbf E\sin\theta. \]

Adding the two squares gives

\[ \mathbf E_\theta^2+\mathbf B_\theta^2 =\mathbf E^2+\mathbf B^2. \]

For the Poynting vector,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf E_\theta\times\mathbf B_\theta ={}&\cos^2\theta\,\mathbf E\times\mathbf B -\sin^2\theta\,\mathbf B\times\mathbf E\\ &+\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\mathbf E\times\mathbf E -\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\mathbf B\times\mathbf B. \end{aligned} \]

The last two terms vanish and

\[ \mathbf B\times\mathbf E=-\mathbf E\times\mathbf B. \]

Thus,

\[ \mathbf E_\theta\times\mathbf B_\theta =\mathbf E\times\mathbf B. \]

The full energy-momentum tensor is also invariant.

Finally, the usual action uses \(F=dA\). An infinitesimal duality variation would require

\[ \delta F=\theta\star F. \]

For nonzero constant \(\theta\), a local \(\delta A\) can satisfy \(d(\delta A)=\theta\star F\) only when \(d\star F=0\). This construction therefore uses the field equations. It does not define a transformation of this kind for every possible potential \(A\).

On a solution, the variation of the kinetic term is proportional to

\[ \int_MF\wedge F. \]

When \(F=dA\) globally,

\[ F\wedge F=d(A\wedge F), \]

which is a boundary term. Introducing a second potential is one way to describe duality directly in the action.

6.3 Problem 3 — Magnetic monopole and Dirac quantization

Consider the space \(\mathbb R^3\setminus\{0\}\) and, in spherical coordinates \((r,\theta,\varphi)\), the magnetic 2-form

\[ F=g\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi, \]

where \(g\neq0\) is a constant.

  1. Show that \(dF=0\) on \(\mathbb R^3\setminus\{0\}\).

  2. Calculate the flux of \(F\) through any sphere \(S_R^2\) centered at the origin.

  3. Show that there cannot be a smooth global 1-form \(A\) on \(S_R^2\) such that \(F=dA\).

  4. Define two open sets: \(U_N\), which excludes the South Pole, and \(U_S\), which excludes the North Pole. Check that

    \[ A_N=g(1-\cos\theta)d\varphi \]

    is regular on \(U_N\), while

    \[ A_S=-g(1+\cos\theta)d\varphi \]

    is regular on \(U_S\), and that

    \[ dA_N=dA_S=F. \]

  5. Determine the gauge transformation connecting \(A_N\) and \(A_S\) on their intersection.

  6. A particle of charge \(q\) has a wave function that transforms under \(A\mapsto A+d\chi\) according to

    \[ \psi\mapsto e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\psi. \]

    Require the transition function to be single-valued when \(\varphi\) increases by \(2\pi\), then obtain the Dirac quantization condition.

  7. Relate the result to de Rham cohomology and the first Chern number.

NoteSolution

The exterior derivative of the field is

\[ dF =g\,d(\sin\theta)\wedge d\theta\wedge d\varphi. \]

But

\[ d(\sin\theta)=\cos\theta\,d\theta, \]

therefore

\[ dF =g\cos\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\theta\wedge d\varphi=0. \]

Thus,

\[ dF=0 \]

everywhere the coordinates describe space with the source at the origin removed.

On the sphere \(r=R\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \Phi_B &=\int_{S_R^2}F\\ &=g\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\\ &=g(2\pi)(2). \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \Phi_B=4\pi g. \]

The result is independent of \(R\), as expected for a radial field proportional to \(1/R^2\).

Now suppose that a smooth global potential exists on the sphere:

\[ F=dA. \]

Stokes’ theorem would give

\[ \int_{S_R^2}F =\int_{S_R^2}dA =\int_{\partial S_R^2}A=0, \]

because the sphere has no boundary. This result contradicts

\[ \int_{S_R^2}F=4\pi g \]

as soon as \(g\neq0\). Therefore, no regular global potential exists.

Calculate the derivatives of the local potentials:

\[ dA_N =g\,d(1-\cos\theta)\wedge d\varphi =g\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi=F, \]

and

\[ dA_S =-g\,d(1+\cos\theta)\wedge d\varphi =g\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi=F. \]

To check regularity at the poles, use Cartesian coordinates on the sphere:

\[ A_N=g\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{r(r+z)}, \qquad A_S=-g\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{r(r-z)}. \]

The first denominator is nonzero everywhere on \(U_N\), and the second is nonzero everywhere on \(U_S\). Each potential is smooth on its own patch.

On \(U_N\cap U_S\),

\[ \begin{aligned} A_N-A_S &=g(1-\cos\theta)d\varphi +g(1+\cos\theta)d\varphi\\ &=2g\,d\varphi. \end{aligned} \]

On any part of the overlap where we choose a continuous angle \(\varphi\), we can write

\[ A_N=A_S+d\chi, \qquad \chi=2g\varphi. \]

There is no single-valued real function \(\chi=2g\varphi\) on the whole overlap: after one complete turn,

\[ \chi(\varphi+2\pi)-\chi(\varphi)=4\pi g. \]

The quantum transition function is

\[ e^{iq\chi/\hbar}. \]

For it to be single-valued, we need

\[ \exp\left(\frac{iq}{\hbar}4\pi g\right)=1. \]

There is therefore an integer \(n\in\mathbb Z\) such that

\[ \frac{4\pi qg}{\hbar}=2\pi n. \]

We obtain the Dirac condition

\[ \frac{2qg}{\hbar}=n\in\mathbb Z. \]

The wave function can be defined consistently on both patches only if this condition holds.

The form \(F\) is closed but not exact on \(\mathbb R^3\setminus\{0\}\), whose degree-two cohomology is non-trivial:

\[ H^2_{\mathrm{dR}}(\mathbb R^3\setminus\{0\}) \simeq H^2_{\mathrm{dR}}(S^2) \simeq\mathbb R. \]

The quantity

\[ \frac{q}{2\pi\hbar}\int_{S^2}F =\frac{q}{2\pi\hbar}(4\pi g) =\frac{2qg}{\hbar} \]

is the first Chern number of the \(U(1)\) bundle for this charge. On the equator, the transition function is \(e^{in\varphi}\): it turns \(n\) times around the unit circle as \(\varphi\) goes from \(0\) to \(2\pi\). This is why the flux, with the factor \(q/(2\pi\hbar)\), must be an integer.
