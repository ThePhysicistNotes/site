Newtonian oscillators

Harmonic motion, damping and resonance

A mass on a spring keeps moving after it first reaches its equilibrium position. The spring pulls it back, but its velocity carries it past that position. This repeated motion is an oscillation.

We start with this simple model, then add friction, an external force and a second mass. The pendulum will show what changes when the restoring force is no longer proportional to the displacement. Throughout these notes, a dot means a derivative with respect to time: \(\dot x\) is velocity and \(\ddot x\) is acceleration.

1 The harmonic oscillator

1.1 From the force to the equation

Consider a mass \(m\) moving horizontally on a frictionless surface, attached to a spring with positive stiffness \(k\). We measure its displacement \(x\) from equilibrium. Within the range where Hooke’s law applies, the spring force is \(F=-kx\). The minus sign means that the force points towards equilibrium. The weight and the normal force cancel vertically, so Newton’s second law gives

\[ m\ddot x=-kx, \qquad\text{or}\qquad m\ddot x+kx=0. \tag{1}\]

A similar equation describes many other systems near a stable equilibrium. To see why, consider a force that comes from a potential energy \(V(q)\):

\[ m\ddot q=-\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dq}. \tag{2}\]

At equilibrium, \(q=q_e\) and \(V'(q_e)=0\). With \(x=q-q_e\), a Taylor expansion gives

\[ V(q_e+x)=V(q_e)+V'(q_e)x +\frac12V''(q_e)x^2+\frac{1}{3!}V^{(3)}(q_e)x^3+\cdots. \tag{3}\]

Suppose that \(V''(q_e)>0\). The equilibrium is then a local minimum. For small enough \(x\), we can neglect the higher powers and set \(k_{\mathrm{eff}}=V''(q_e)\):

\[ V(q_e+x)\simeq V(q_e)+\frac12k_{\mathrm{eff}}x^2, \qquad F(q_e+x)\simeq-k_{\mathrm{eff}}x. \tag{4}\]

Thus,

\[ m\ddot x+k_{\mathrm{eff}}x=0. \tag{5}\]

The effective stiffness measures the curvature of the potential near equilibrium. If \(V''(q_e)=0\), the higher powers must be kept, even for small motion. If \(V''(q_e)<0\), the force pushes the system away from equilibrium and this position is unstable. Below, we write the positive stiffness simply as \(k\).

1.2 Solution and initial conditions

Dividing by \(m\) gives

\[ \ddot x+\omega_0^2x=0, \qquad \omega_0=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}. \tag{6}\]

The quantity \(\omega_0\) is the natural angular frequency, in radians per second. We look for a solution proportional to \(e^{rt}\). Substitution gives

\[ (r^2+\omega_0^2)e^{rt}=0. \]

Since the exponential does not vanish, \(r=\pm i\omega_0\). The real solution is therefore

\[ x(t)=A\cos(\omega_0t)+B\sin(\omega_0t). \]

For \(x(0)=x_0\) and \(\dot x(0)=v_0\), we have \(A=x_0\) and \(B=v_0/\omega_0\), hence

\[ x(t)=x_0\cos(\omega_0t) +\frac{v_0}{\omega_0}\sin(\omega_0t). \tag{7}\]

We can also write \(x(t)=R\cos(\omega_0t-\varphi)\). Expanding this cosine and comparing the coefficients gives

\[ R=\sqrt{x_0^2+\left(\frac{v_0}{\omega_0}\right)^2}, \qquad \varphi=\operatorname{atan2}\!\left(\frac{v_0}{\omega_0},x_0\right). \tag{8}\]

Here \(\operatorname{atan2}\) chooses the angle in the correct quadrant. The period and the frequency are

\[ T_0=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}, \qquad f_0=\frac{1}{T_0}. \]

A larger mass gives slower motion; a stiffer spring gives faster motion. The period does not depend on the amplitude \(R\). This is called isochronism.

1.3 Energy and phase portrait

Multiplying \(m\ddot x+kx=0\) by \(\dot x\) gives

\[ m\ddot x\dot x+kx\dot x=0. \]

Each term is a time derivative:

\[ m\ddot x\dot x=\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\left(\frac12m\dot x^2\right), \qquad kx\dot x=\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\left(\frac12kx^2\right). \]

It follows that the mechanical energy is constant:

\[ \frac{\mathrm dE}{\mathrm dt}=0, \qquad E=\frac12m\dot x^2+\frac12kx^2. \tag{9}\]

At the turning points \(x=\pm R\), the velocity is zero and the energy is entirely potential. At equilibrium, the speed is greatest and the energy is entirely kinetic.

A phase portrait plots velocity against position, rather than against time. Since \(E=kR^2/2\), energy conservation gives, for \(R>0\),

\[ \frac{x^2}{R^2}+\frac{\dot x^2}{\omega_0^2R^2}=1. \tag{10}\]

The trajectory is an ellipse in the \((x,\dot x)\) plane. It runs clockwise: at \((R,0)\), the acceleration is negative, so the velocity becomes negative. When \(R=0\), the mass stays at the origin.

NoteHooke’s law

Hooke published his law of elasticity in 1678. It relates the force exerted by a spring to its extension. Real springs follow this law only over a limited range; the harmonic oscillator is the model obtained within that range.

1.4 Numerical solution

To solve the equation numerically, set \(v=\dot x\). One second-order equation becomes two first-order equations:

\[ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt} \begin{pmatrix}x\\v\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}v\\-\omega_0^2x\end{pmatrix}. \tag{11}\]

The function below applies the same method to any equation of the form \(\ddot x=a(t,x,v)\).

Python — general solver 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

plt.rcParams.update({
    "figure.figsize": (9, 4.8),
    "axes.grid": True,
    "grid.alpha": 0.25,
    "axes.spines.top": False,
    "axes.spines.right": False,
    "font.size": 11,
})

def resoudre_oscillateur(acceleration, y0, temps, *, args=(), methode="DOP853"):
    """Solve x'' = acceleration(t, x, v, *args)."""
    def systeme(t, y):
        x, v = y
        return (v, acceleration(t, x, v, *args))

    solution = solve_ivp(
        systeme,
        (temps[0], temps[-1]),
        y0,
        t_eval=temps,
        method=methode,
        rtol=1e-10,
        atol=1e-12,
    )
    if not solution.success:
        raise RuntimeError(solution.message)
    return solution.y[0], solution.y[1]

For the harmonic oscillator, we can compare the result with the exact solution. The last graph shows the relative change in energy, \((E-E_0)/E_0\), which would be zero in an exact calculation.

2 Damping and driving

A real oscillator loses energy through friction. We model this by \(F_f=-b\dot x\), with \(b\geq0\). This force opposes the motion and is proportional to speed. It describes viscous damping; dry sliding friction would require a different model.

We can also apply a periodic force, for example through a motor. With amplitude \(F_0\) and angular frequency \(\Omega\), Newton’s law becomes

\[ m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=F_0\cos(\Omega t). \tag{12}\]

The frequency \(\Omega\) belongs to the external force; \(\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}\) belongs to the mass and spring.

2.1 Free damped motion

First set \(F_0=0\). Introduce the damping rate \(\gamma=b/(2m)\) and the damping ratio \(\zeta=\gamma/\omega_0\). The equation is

\[ \ddot x+2\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0. \tag{13}\]

Trying \(x=e^{rt}\) gives

\[ r^2+2\gamma r+\omega_0^2=0, \qquad r_\pm=-\gamma\pm\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}. \tag{14}\]

For \(0<\gamma<\omega_0\), the roots are complex. This is the underdamped regime. With

\[ \omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2} =\omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}, \tag{15}\]

we obtain \(x=e^{-\gamma t}[A\cos(\omega_dt)+B\sin(\omega_dt)]\). The initial conditions give \(A=x_0\) and \(v_0=-\gamma A+\omega_dB\), so

\[ x(t)=e^{-\gamma t} \left[ x_0\cos(\omega_dt) +\frac{v_0+\gamma x_0}{\omega_d}\sin(\omega_dt) \right]. \tag{16}\]

The mass still oscillates, but its amplitude decreases with the envelope \(e^{-\gamma t}\). Multiplying the equation by \(m\dot x\) gives

\[ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt} \left(\frac12m\dot x^2+\frac12kx^2\right) =-b\dot x^2\leq0. \tag{17}\]

This is the energy lost to friction per unit time. The phase trajectory spirals towards the origin.

At \(\gamma=\omega_0\), the two roots are equal. This is critical damping, with \(b_c=2\sqrt{km}\). The two independent solutions are \(e^{-\omega_0t}\) and \(te^{-\omega_0t}\). Applying the initial conditions gives

\[ x(t)=e^{-\omega_0t} \left[x_0+(v_0+\omega_0x_0)t\right]. \tag{18}\]

For \(\gamma>\omega_0\), both roots are real and negative. This is the overdamped regime. With \(\Delta=\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}\) and \(r_\pm=-\gamma\pm\Delta\),

\[ x(t)=C_+e^{r_+t}+C_-e^{r_-t}, \]

where \(C_++C_-=x_0\) and \(r_+C_++r_-C_-=v_0\). Solving these two equations gives

\[ C_+=\frac{v_0-r_-x_0}{r_+-r_-}, \qquad C_-=\frac{r_+x_0-v_0}{r_+-r_-}. \tag{19}\]

For a mass released from rest, critical damping gives a faster return than overdamping, without crossing equilibrium. Very strong friction makes the return slow: for \(\gamma\gg\omega_0\), the slow root is \(r_+\simeq-\omega_0^2/(2\gamma)\). With a nonzero initial velocity, even a critically damped mass can cross equilibrium once.

2.2 Sinusoidal force

For \(b>0\), the full solution is the sum of a free damped solution and a particular solution:

\[ x(t)=x_{\mathrm{trans}}(t)+x_{\mathrm{perm}}(t). \]

The first term depends on the initial conditions and tends to zero. The second is the steady response to the force. In the underdamped case, the transient has an envelope \(e^{-\gamma t}\); in the other regimes, its decay follows the real roots found above.

To find the steady response, write the force as the real part of \(F_0e^{i\Omega t}\) and try \(\widetilde x=Xe^{i\Omega t}\). Then

\[ \dot{\widetilde x}=i\Omega Xe^{i\Omega t}, \qquad \ddot{\widetilde x}=-\Omega^2Xe^{i\Omega t}. \]

Substitution gives

\[ (k-m\Omega^2+ib\Omega)Xe^{i\Omega t}=F_0e^{i\Omega t}, \]

and therefore

\[ X=\frac{F_0}{k-m\Omega^2+ib\Omega}. \tag{20}\]

Writing \(X=Ae^{-i\delta}\) and taking the real part gives

\[ x_{\mathrm{perm}}(t)=A(\Omega)\cos(\Omega t-\delta), \]

with

\[ A(\Omega)=\frac{F_0}{\sqrt{(k-m\Omega^2)^2+(b\Omega)^2}}, \tag{21}\]

and

\[ \delta(\Omega)=\operatorname{atan2}(b\Omega,k-m\Omega^2). \tag{22}\]

At low frequency, \(A\simeq F_0/k\) and \(\delta\simeq0\): the spring follows the force almost as if it were static. At high frequency, \(A\simeq F_0/(m\Omega^2)\) and \(\delta\simeq\pi\). At \(\Omega=\omega_0\), the displacement lags the force by a quarter of a cycle, \(\delta=\pi/2\).

2.3 Resonance and energy transfer

For \(F_0>0\), the amplitude is largest when the denominator of Equation 21 is smallest. Let

\[ D(\Omega)=(k-m\Omega^2)^2+b^2\Omega^2. \]

Differentiating,

\[ D'(\Omega)=2\Omega\left[-2m(k-m\Omega^2)+b^2\right]. \]

A peak at a nonzero frequency therefore occurs at

\[ \Omega_r=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-2\gamma^2} =\omega_0\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}, \tag{23}\]

provided \(0<\zeta<1/\sqrt2\). For larger damping, the displacement amplitude decreases from its value at \(\Omega=0\), with no nonzero-frequency peak. The free oscillation frequency \(\omega_d\), the displacement resonance frequency \(\Omega_r\) and the natural frequency \(\omega_0\) are distinct. They are close when damping is weak.

For the graphs, use \(r=\Omega/\omega_0\) and divide the amplitude by \(F_0/k\):

\[ \frac{A}{F_0/k} =\frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2}}. \tag{24}\]

Multiplying Equation 12 by \(\dot x\) gives the power balance \(\dot E=-b\dot x^2+F(t)\dot x\). In steady motion with \(\Omega>0\), the average of \(\dot E\) over one period is zero. Since the average of \(\sin^2(\Omega t-\delta)\) is \(1/2\),

\[ \langle P_{\mathrm{diss}}\rangle =b\langle\dot x^2\rangle =\frac12b\Omega^2A^2. \tag{25}\]

The average power supplied by the force is

\[ \langle P_{\mathrm{ext}}\rangle =\frac12F_0A\Omega\sin\delta =\langle P_{\mathrm{diss}}\rangle. \]

For fixed \(F_0\) and \(b>0\), this power is greatest at \(\Omega=\omega_0\), which differs from the frequency of maximum displacement. The quality factor is

\[ Q=\frac{m\omega_0}{b}=\frac{1}{2\zeta}. \tag{26}\]

A large \(Q\) means weak damping and a narrow resonance peak. When \(Q\gg1\), the frequency width between the two half-power points is approximately \(\Delta\Omega\simeq\omega_0/Q\). For free weakly damped motion, the energy decreases over many oscillations.

2.4 Without damping: beats and resonance

If \(b=0\), the free part does not decay. For a mass initially at rest at equilibrium and \(\Omega\neq\omega_0\), the solution is

\[ x(t)=\frac{F_0/m}{\omega_0^2-\Omega^2} \left[\cos(\Omega t)-\cos(\omega_0t)\right]. \]

The difference of cosines can be written as

\[ \cos(\Omega t)-\cos(\omega_0t) =-2\sin\left(\frac{\Omega+\omega_0}{2}t\right) \sin\left(\frac{\Omega-\omega_0}{2}t\right). \]

When the two frequencies are close, the first factor oscillates rapidly and the second varies slowly. The resulting changes in amplitude are called beats. Taking the limit \(\Omega\to\omega_0\) gives

\[ x(t)=\frac{F_0}{2m\omega_0}\,t\sin(\omega_0t). \]

At exact resonance, the amplitude grows linearly with time in this ideal model. There is no finite steady amplitude.

3 Coupled and parametric oscillators

3.1 Two coupled masses

Consider two identical masses \(m\). Each is connected to a fixed support by a spring of stiffness \(k\), and a spring of stiffness \(\kappa\) connects the two masses. Their displacements from equilibrium are \(x_1\) and \(x_2\).

The extension of the middle spring is \(x_2-x_1\). It exerts a force \(\kappa(x_2-x_1)\) on the first mass and the opposite force on the second. Newton’s equations are

\[ \begin{aligned} m\ddot x_1+(k+\kappa)x_1-\kappa x_2&=0,\\ m\ddot x_2+(k+\kappa)x_2-\kappa x_1&=0. \end{aligned} \tag{27}\]

In matrix form,

\[ \mathbf M\ddot{\mathbf x}+\mathbf K\mathbf x=0, \quad \mathbf M=m\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf K=\begin{pmatrix}k+\kappa&-\kappa\\-\kappa&k+\kappa\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf x=\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{pmatrix}. \tag{28}\]

A normal mode is a motion in which the two masses oscillate at one frequency with a fixed ratio of amplitudes. Set \(\mathbf x(t)=\mathbf u\cos(\omega t+\phi)\). We obtain

\[ (\mathbf K-\omega^2\mathbf M)\mathbf u=0. \tag{29}\]

For a nonzero vector \(\mathbf u\), the determinant must vanish:

\[ \det(\mathbf K-\omega^2\mathbf M) =(k+\kappa-m\omega^2)^2-\kappa^2=0. \]

This gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \omega_+&=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, &\mathbf u_+&=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\\ \omega_-&=\sqrt{\frac{k+2\kappa}{m}}, &\mathbf u_-&=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} \tag{30}\]

In the symmetric mode, the masses move together and the middle spring does not stretch. In the antisymmetric mode, they move in opposite directions. The middle spring then adds a restoring force, giving the higher frequency \(\omega_-\).

The normal coordinates are

\[ Q_+=\frac{x_1+x_2}{\sqrt2}, \qquad Q_-=\frac{x_1-x_2}{\sqrt2}. \tag{31}\]

Adding and subtracting Newton’s equations gives two independent equations:

\[ \ddot Q_++\omega_+^2Q_+=0, \qquad \ddot Q_-+\omega_-^2Q_-=0. \tag{32}\]

For example, displace only the first mass by \(A\) and release both from rest. Then \(Q_+(0)=Q_-(0)=A/\sqrt2\). Returning to \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} x_1(t)&=\frac A2\left[\cos(\omega_+t)+\cos(\omega_-t)\right],\\ x_2(t)&=\frac A2\left[\cos(\omega_+t)-\cos(\omega_-t)\right]. \end{aligned} \]

Python code 
m, k, kappa = 1.0, 1.0, 0.12
omega_plus = np.sqrt(k / m)
omega_moins = np.sqrt((k + 2 * kappa) / m)
A = 1.0
temps = np.linspace(0, 180, 5000)

# Initial conditions: only the first mass is displaced.
x1 = 0.5 * A * (np.cos(omega_plus * temps) + np.cos(omega_moins * temps))
x2 = 0.5 * A * (np.cos(omega_plus * temps) - np.cos(omega_moins * temps))
Q_plus = (x1 + x2) / np.sqrt(2)
Q_moins = (x1 - x2) / np.sqrt(2)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4.0))

axes[0].plot(temps, x1, label=r"$x_1$")
axes[0].plot(temps, x2, label=r"$x_2$", alpha=0.85)
axes[0].set(xlabel=r"$t$ (s)", ylabel="displacement")
axes[0].legend()

axes[1].plot(temps, Q_plus, label=r"$Q_+$")
axes[1].plot(temps, Q_moins, label=r"$Q_-$")
axes[1].set(xlabel=r"$t$ (s)", ylabel="normal coordinate")
axes[1].legend()

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

For weak coupling, the two frequencies are close and beats appear. Energy passes back and forth between the masses through the middle spring. The total energy, including the energy in that spring, remains constant.

For several masses, the same calculation leads to \(\mathbf K\mathbf u_n=\omega_n^2\mathbf M\mathbf u_n\). Each normal coordinate then behaves like a harmonic oscillator.

3.2 A time-dependent stiffness

Energy can also enter a system when one of its parameters changes. Suppose that a device varies the spring stiffness according to

\[ k(t)=k_0[1+h\cos(\Omega t)], \]

where \(h\) is the relative modulation amplitude. With \(\omega_0=\sqrt{k_0/m}\), the equation becomes

\[ \ddot x+2\gamma\dot x +\omega_0^2[1+h\cos(\Omega t)]x=0. \tag{33}\]

This is a damped Mathieu equation. There is no added force on the right-hand side, but the device can still do work. For \(E=m\dot x^2/2+k(t)x^2/2\),

\[ \dot E=-b\dot x^2+\frac12\dot k(t)x^2. \]

Increasing the stiffness when \(|x|\) is large adds energy. Decreasing it when \(x\) is near zero removes less energy. Since \(x^2\) has two maxima during one oscillation, the main parametric resonance occurs near

\[ \Omega\simeq2\omega_0. \tag{34}\]

We can estimate the growth rate when \(0<h\ll1\), \(\gamma\ll\omega_0\) and \(\Omega=2\omega_0\). Write

\[ x(t)=a(t)\cos(\omega_0t)+c(t)\sin(\omega_0t), \]

where \(a\) and \(c\) vary slowly. Neglect their second derivatives and products such as \(\gamma\dot a\). The terms containing \(\cos(2\omega_0t)\) produce frequencies \(\omega_0\) and \(3\omega_0\). Keeping the terms at \(\omega_0\), which drive the slow change, gives

\[ \dot a=-\gamma a-\frac{h\omega_0}{4}c, \qquad \dot c=-\gamma c-\frac{h\omega_0}{4}a. \]

The combination \(a-c\) therefore satisfies

\[ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(a-c) =\left(\frac{h\omega_0}{4}-\gamma\right)(a-c). \]

The growing component has an envelope1

\[ R(t)\propto \exp\left[\left(\frac{h\omega_0}{4}-\gamma\right)t\right]. \tag{35}\]

To this order, growth begins when \(h\omega_0/4>\gamma\). The exact state \(x=\dot x=0\) stays at rest, but a small disturbance with a growing component is amplified. The next example starts with a small nonzero displacement.

Python code 
omega0 = 1.0
gamma = 0.02
Omega = 2.0 * omega0
temps = np.linspace(0, 220, 6000)

def acceleration_parametrique(t, x, v, omega0, gamma, h, Omega):
    return -2 * gamma * v - omega0**2 * (1 + h * np.cos(Omega * t)) * x

for h, couleur in [(0.05, "tab:blue"), (0.16, "tab:red")]:

    ax.semilogy(temps, amplitude_instantanee, color=couleur, label=rf"$h={h}$")

ax.legend(title="modulation")

4 Nonlinear oscillators

4.1 The pendulum

Consider a point mass \(m\) at the end of a rigid, massless rod of length \(\ell\). The pivot is fixed and \(\theta\) is measured from the downward vertical. Gravity gives the torque \(-mg\ell\sin\theta\), while the moment of inertia is \(m\ell^2\). Thus,

\[ m\ell^2\ddot\theta=-mg\ell\sin\theta, \]

or

\[ \ddot\theta+\frac g\ell\sin\theta=0. \tag{36}\]

For \(|\theta|\ll1\) in radians,

\[ \sin\theta=\theta-\frac{\theta^3}{6}+\mathcal O(\theta^5). \]

Keeping the first term gives

\[ \ddot\theta+\omega_0^2\theta=0, \qquad \omega_0=\sqrt{\frac g\ell}. \tag{37}\]

The neglected term is smaller than the linear term by about \(\theta^2/6\). The small-angle approximation is therefore controlled by the angle, not by the mass or the rod length.

Multiplying the exact equation by \(m\ell^2\dot\theta\) gives the conserved energy

\[ E=\frac12m\ell^2\dot\theta^2+mg\ell(1-\cos\theta). \tag{38}\]

The top position \(\theta=\pi\) has energy \(E_s=2mg\ell\). For \(0<E<E_s\), the pendulum swings between two turning points; this motion is called libration. For \(E>E_s\), it can pass over the top and keep rotating. At \(E=E_s\), a moving pendulum approaches the top only as \(t\to\infty\). This limiting trajectory is the separatrix.

4.2 Why the period increases with amplitude

Release the pendulum from rest at an angle \(0<\theta_{\max}<\pi\). Energy conservation gives

\[ \frac12m\ell^2\dot\theta^2 =mg\ell(\cos\theta-\cos\theta_{\max}), \]

so

\[ |\dot\theta| =\sqrt{\frac{2g}{\ell}(\cos\theta-\cos\theta_{\max})}. \]

A quarter of a period is the time taken to travel from \(0\) to \(\theta_{\max}\). Therefore,

\[ \frac T4=\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{2g}} \int_0^{\theta_{\max}} \frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\sqrt{\cos\theta-\cos\theta_{\max}}}. \]

Use \(\sin(\theta/2)=\sin(\theta_{\max}/2)\sin\phi\). The limits become \(0\) and \(\pi/2\), giving

\[ T(\theta_{\max}) =4\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{g}}\, K\left(\sin^2\frac{\theta_{\max}}2\right), \tag{39}\]

where

\[ K(p)=\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\mathrm d\phi}{\sqrt{1-p\sin^2\phi}}. \]

This integral is called the complete elliptic integral of the first kind. Expanding its integrand in powers of \(p\) gives

\[ K(p)=\frac\pi2 \left(1+\frac p4+\frac{9p^2}{64}+\cdots\right). \]

Using \(p=\sin^2(\theta_{\max}/2)\) then gives, with the angle in radians,

\[ T(\theta_{\max}) =T_0\left[ 1+\frac{\theta_{\max}^2}{16} +\frac{11\theta_{\max}^4}{3072} +\mathcal O(\theta_{\max}^6) \right], \qquad T_0=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{g}}. \tag{40}\]

The period increases with amplitude. As \(\theta_{\max}\to\pi\), the pendulum spends longer near the unstable top position and the period diverges.

4.3 The Duffing oscillator

A spring may become harder or softer to stretch as its displacement increases. If the force is symmetric about equilibrium, \(F(-x)=-F(x)\), its expansion contains odd powers of \(x\). Keeping the first nonlinear term gives \(F=-kx-\alpha x^3\). With damping and driving,

\[ m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx+\alpha x^3 =F_0\cos(\Omega t). \tag{41}\]

This is the Duffing equation. Without damping or driving, its potential energy is

\[ V(x)=\frac12kx^2+\frac14\alpha x^4. \tag{42}\]

For \(k>0\) and \(\alpha>0\), the restoring force grows faster than in the harmonic model, so the oscillation frequency increases with amplitude. For \(\alpha<0\), it decreases for small enough amplitudes. The latter model is only local: the negative quartic term cannot describe a stable system at arbitrarily large \(|x|\).

Another case is \(k<0\) and \(\alpha>0\). Solving \(V'(x)=x(k+\alpha x^2)=0\) gives

\[ x=0, \qquad x=\pm\sqrt{-\frac{k}{\alpha}}. \]

Since \(V''(0)=k<0\) and \(V''(\pm\sqrt{-k/\alpha})=-2k>0\), the origin is unstable and the other two positions are stable. This double-well model can describe a bent strip that can move between two stable shapes.

The following example uses the dimensionless equation \(x''+\delta x'-x+x^3=f\cos(\Omega t)\). With driving, the motion can cross between the wells and become irregular. We plot both \(x(t)\) and a Poincaré section: one point \((x,\dot x)\) is taken at each driving period \(T=2\pi/\Omega\), after the initial transient.

Python code 
# Dimensionless equation: x'' + delta*x' - x + x^3 = f*cos(Omega*t)
delta, f, Omega = 0.20, 0.30, 1.20
periode = 2 * np.pi / Omega
n_periodes = 550
points_par_periode = 90
temps = np.linspace(0, n_periodes * periode,
                    n_periodes * points_par_periode + 1)

def acceleration_duffing(t, x, v, delta, f, Omega):
    return -delta * v + x - x**3 + f * np.cos(Omega * t)

x, v = resoudre_oscillateur(
    acceleration_duffing, (0.1, 0.0), temps, args=(delta, f, Omega)
)

# Remove the transient and take one point per period.
indices_poincare = np.arange(150, n_periodes + 1) * points_par_periode
masque_fin = temps > (n_periodes - 25) * periode

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4.0))
axes[0].plot(temps[masque_fin] / periode, x[masque_fin], lw=0.9)
axes[0].set(xlabel="number of periods", ylabel=r"$x(t)$")

axes[1].scatter(x[indices_poincare], v[indices_poincare], s=9, alpha=0.65)
axes[1].set(xlabel=r"$x(nT)$", ylabel=r"$\dot x(nT)$")

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

A response that repeats after one driving period gives one point in this section. A response that repeats after several periods gives several points. A more complex set can occur in chaotic motion, where nearby initial states separate rapidly. The section alone is not a proof of chaos; that requires a separate stability study.

4.4 Energy balance

For a fixed potential \(V(x)\), the models above can be written as

\[ m\ddot x+b\dot x+\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dx} =F_{\mathrm{ext}}(x,t). \tag{43}\]

Multiplication by \(\dot x\) gives

\[ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt} \left(\frac12m\dot x^2+V(x)\right) =-b\dot x^2+F_{\mathrm{ext}}(x,t)\dot x. \tag{44}\]

Friction removes energy, while the external force can add or remove it depending on its direction relative to the velocity. For a time-dependent potential, an additional term \(\partial V/\partial t\) appears, as in the parametric oscillator.

5 Numerical methods

5.1 State variables and scales

The general equation becomes

\[ \dot x=v, \qquad \dot v=\frac1m\left[F_{\mathrm{ext}}(x,t)-bv-V'(x)\right]. \tag{45}\]

This is the system used by the Python function at the beginning of the chapter.

For the driven linear oscillator with \(F_0>0\), choose \(\tau=\omega_0t\) and \(x_s=F_0/k\), then set \(X=x/x_s\). Since \(\dot x=x_s\omega_0X'\) and \(\ddot x=x_s\omega_0^2X''\), division by \(kx_s=F_0\) gives

\[ X''+2\zeta X'+X=\cos(r\tau), \qquad r=\frac{\Omega}{\omega_0}. \tag{46}\]

Primes now mean derivatives with respect to \(\tau\). In these units, the response depends only on \(\zeta\) and \(r\), together with the initial conditions. If \(F_0=0\), a nonzero initial amplitude can provide the length scale instead.

5.2 Velocity Verlet

The Python figures use an adaptive Runge–Kutta method. For a conservative force depending only on position, velocity Verlet is another useful choice. With \(a_n=F(x_n)/m\) and a fixed time step \(\Delta t\),

\[ \begin{aligned} x_{n+1}&=x_n+v_n\Delta t+\frac12a_n\Delta t^2,\\ a_{n+1}&=\frac{F(x_{n+1})}{m},\\ v_{n+1}&=v_n+\frac12(a_n+a_{n+1})\Delta t. \end{aligned} \]

The position step uses the current acceleration; the velocity step uses the average of the old and new accelerations. This method does not conserve the energy exactly, but with a suitably small step it often keeps the energy error bounded over long integrations.

Python — velocity Verlet 
def verlet_vitesse(force, x0, v0, temps, m=1.0):
    """Symplectic integration of m*x'' = force(x), on a uniform grid."""
    temps = np.asarray(temps)
    dt = temps[1] - temps[0]
    if not np.allclose(np.diff(temps), dt):
        raise ValueError("The time grid must be uniform.")

    x = np.empty_like(temps, dtype=float)
    v = np.empty_like(temps, dtype=float)
    x[0], v[0] = x0, v0
    a = force(x[0]) / m

    for n in range(len(temps) - 1):
        x[n + 1] = x[n] + v[n] * dt + 0.5 * a * dt**2
        a_suivante = force(x[n + 1]) / m
        v[n + 1] = v[n] + 0.5 * (a + a_suivante) * dt
        a = a_suivante

    return x, v

For a harmonic oscillator, compare the numerical result with Equation 7. For a nonlinear conservative system, check the energy and repeat the calculation with a smaller time step. These comparisons tell us whether a visible change comes from the physics or from the numerical method.
