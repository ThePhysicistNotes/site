1 Fields and particles

The Poincaré group classifies a massive particle by its mass \(m\) and spin \(s\). A massless particle is instead classified by its helicity. This tells us what a particle is, but it does not yet give a local theory for its motion or interactions. For that purpose we introduce fields.

A field transforms in a finite-dimensional representation of the Lorentz group. Its components are not all necessarily physical. For example, a four-vector has four components, while a massive spin-\(1\) particle has three polarization states and a photon has only two. Equations of motion, constraints and gauge symmetry remove the extra components.

We use natural units, \(\hbar=c=1\), and the metric

\[ \eta_{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1). \]

Therefore

\[ p^2=(p^0)^2-\mathbf p^2, \qquad \Box=\partial_t^2-\nabla^2. \]

The main question throughout the chapter is simple: starting from a covariant field, how do we identify its physical particle states?

1.1 Lorentz representations

Finite-dimensional irreducible representations of the proper Lorentz group are labelled by two half-integers,

\[ (j_L,j_R). \]

A scalar transforms as \((0,0)\). Left- and right-handed Weyl spinors transform as \((1/2,0)\) and \((0,1/2)\). A four-vector transforms as \((1/2,1/2)\). Under ordinary rotations,

\[ (j_L,j_R)ig|_{SO(3)} =\bigoplus_{j=|j_L-j_R|}^{j_L+j_R}j. \tag{1}\]

Thus a four-vector contains \(j=0\) and \(j=1\) sectors before its dynamics is imposed. A field representation and a physical particle representation are related, but they are not the same object.

The two Casimir operators of the Poincaré group are \(P^2\) and \(W^2\), where

\[ W^\mu=\frac12\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} P_\nu M_{\rho\sigma} \]

is the Pauli–Lubanski vector. For a massive one-particle state,

\[ P^2=m^2, \qquad W^2=-m^2s(s+1). \tag{2}\]

In the rest frame, \(P^\mu=(m,\mathbf0)\) and \(\mathbf W=m\mathbf J\). This is why the second invariant measures spin. For a massless particle with discrete helicity,

\[ W^\mu=hP^\mu. \]

2 The scalar field

2.1 Klein–Gordon equation

The simplest relativistic field is a real scalar \(\phi(x)\). Its free action is

\[ S=\int d^4x\,\mathcal L, \qquad \mathcal L=\frac12\partial_\mu\phi\partial^\mu\phi -\frac12m^2\phi^2. \tag{3}\]

The field Euler–Lagrange equation is

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi} -\partial_\mu\frac{\partial\mathcal L} {\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}=0. \]

Since

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi}=-m^2\phi, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)} =\partial^\mu\phi, \]

we obtain the Klein–Gordon equation

\[ \boxed{(\Box+m^2)\phi=0.} \tag{4}\]

For a plane wave

\[ \phi(x)=Ae^{-ip\cdot x}, \]

the equation gives

\[ p^2=m^2, \qquad E_{\mathbf p}=\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2}. \tag{5}\]

Both signs \(p^0=\pm E_{\mathbf p}\) solve the classical equation. After quantization, they will be reinterpreted as particle creation and annihilation modes; they are not negative-energy particles propagating freely.

The canonical momentum is

\[ \pi(x)=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\phi}=\dot\phi. \]

The Hamiltonian density is

\[ \mathcal H=\pi\dot\phi-\mathcal L =\frac12\pi^2+\frac12(\nabla\phi)^2+\frac12m^2\phi^2. \tag{6}\]

It is non-negative for \(m^2\geq0\). This is an important test of the stability of the free theory.

NoteThe first relativistic wave equation

The Klein–Gordon equation appeared before the Dirac equation as the direct quantum version of \(E^2=\mathbf p^2+m^2\). Its conserved density is not positive definite, so it could not serve as a one-particle probability density. In quantum field theory this difficulty disappears: the field describes particles and antiparticles, and its conserved current is interpreted as a charge current.

2.2 Complex scalar field

A charged scalar field must be complex. Its Lagrangian density is

\[ \mathcal L =\partial_\mu\phi^*\partial^\mu\phi-m^2\phi^*\phi. \tag{7}\]

It is invariant under the global phase transformation

\[ \phi\to e^{-iq\alpha}\phi, \qquad \phi^*\to e^{iq\alpha}\phi^*. \]

The Noether current is

\[ j^\mu=iq\left( \phi^*\partial^\mu\phi-phi\partial^\mu\phi^* \right), \qquad \partial_\mu j^\mu=0. \tag{8}\]

The field and its complex conjugate carry opposite charges. After quantization, their two sets of modes create particles and antiparticles.

2.3 Degrees of freedom

The Klein–Gordon equation is second order in time. A real solution is fixed by two real functions, \(\phi(t_0,\mathbf x)\) and \(\dot\phi(t_0,\mathbf x)\). In momentum space, these data combine into one annihilation amplitude and its complex conjugate. The quantized real field therefore describes one neutral spin-\(0\) particle species.

A complex field is equivalent to two real fields,

\[ \phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\phi_1+i\phi_2), \]

and describes a charged particle together with its antiparticle.

3 The Dirac field

3.1 Linearizing the relativistic dispersion relation

The Klein–Gordon equation is second order in time. Dirac looked for an equation that was first order in both time and space,

\[ (i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu-m)\psi=0. \tag{9}\]

We use the slash notation

\[ \not\!p=\gamma^\mu p_\mu, \qquad \not\!\partial=\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu. \]

To recover the relativistic mass-shell relation, multiply Equation 9 by \((i\not\!\partial+m)\):

\[ (i\not\!\partial+m)(i\not\!\partial-m)\psi =\left[-\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\partial_\mu\partial_\nu-m^2\right]\psi. \]

The derivatives are symmetric in \(\mu\) and \(\nu\), so only the symmetric part of the matrix product contributes. We must impose the Clifford algebra

\[ \boxed{\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu\}=2\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathbf1_4.} \tag{10}\]

Then every component of the spinor satisfies

\[ (\Box+m^2)\psi=0. \]

No set of ordinary numbers can satisfy Equation 10. In four dimensions, the smallest matrices are \(4\times4\), so a Dirac spinor has four complex components.

In the Dirac representation,

\[ \gamma^0= \begin{pmatrix}I&0\\0&-I\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \gamma^i= \begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma^i\\-\sigma^i&0\end{pmatrix}. \]

The explicit matrices depend on the chosen basis, but physical results do not.

3.2 Action and Lorentz covariance

The Dirac adjoint is defined by

\[ \bar\psi=\psi^\dagger\gamma^0. \]

The free action is

\[ S=\int d^4x\,\bar\psi(i\not\!\partial-m)\psi. \tag{11}\]

Varying \(\bar\psi\) gives the Dirac equation. Varying \(\psi\) and integrating by parts gives the adjoint equation

\[ i(\partial_\mu\bar\psi)\gamma^\mu+m\bar\psi=0. \tag{12}\]

Under a Lorentz transformation,

\[ \psi'(x')=S(\Lambda)\psi(x). \]

Covariance of the equation requires

\[ S^{-1}(\Lambda)\gamma^\mu S(\Lambda) =\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}\gamma^\nu. \]

For an infinitesimal transformation,

\[ S(\Lambda)=1-\frac{i}{4}\omega_{\mu\nu}\sigma^{\mu\nu}, \qquad \sigma^{\mu\nu}=\frac{i}{2}[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]. \tag{13}\]

The matrices \(\sigma^{\mu\nu}/2\) are the Lorentz generators in the spinor representation.

The phase symmetry \(\psi\to e^{-iq\alpha}\psi\) gives the current

\[ j^\mu=q\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi. \tag{14}\]

Using Equation 9 and Equation 12,

\[ \partial_\mu j^\mu=0. \]

Its time component is

\[ j^0=q\psi^\dagger\psi. \]

For \(q>0\) it is non-negative. In field theory, however, \(j^0\) is interpreted as a charge density, and the quantized field contains states of both charges.

3.3 Plane-wave spinors

Positive-frequency solutions are written

\[ \psi(x)=u_s(p)e^{-ip\cdot x}, \]

and satisfy

\[ (\not\!p-m)u_s(p)=0. \tag{15}\]

Negative-frequency solutions are written

\[ \psi(x)=v_s(p)e^{ip\cdot x}, \]

and satisfy

\[ (\not\!p+m)v_s(p)=0. \tag{16}\]

At rest, \(p^\mu=(m,\mathbf0)\). In the Dirac basis, two independent \(u\) solutions occupy the upper components and two independent \(v\) solutions occupy the lower components. They correspond to the two spin projections of the particle and antiparticle.

For general momentum, a useful normalization is

\[ \bar u_r(p)u_s(p)=2m\delta_{rs}, \qquad \bar v_r(p)v_s(p)=-2m\delta_{rs}. \]

The completeness relations are

\[ \sum_su_s(p)\bar u_s(p)=\not\!p+m, \]

\[ \sum_sv_s(p)\bar v_s(p)=\not\!p-m. \tag{17}\]

They remove explicit spinors when squared amplitudes are calculated.

One possible explicit form is

\[ u_s(p)= \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{E_{\mathbf p}+m}\,\xi_s\\ \dfrac{\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p} {\sqrt{E_{\mathbf p}+m}}\,\xi_s \end{pmatrix}, \]

where \(\xi_s\) is a two-component spinor. The lower components become small when \(|\mathbf p|\ll m\), which leads to the nonrelativistic Pauli theory.

NoteDirac and the positron

Dirac’s equation combined special relativity with a first-order time evolution and naturally described spin \(1/2\). Its second family of solutions could not be removed consistently. In quantum field theory these modes create antiparticles. The positron, discovered by Carl Anderson in 1932, was the first experimental example.

3.4 Chirality and helicity

Define

\[ \gamma^5=i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3. \]

It satisfies

\[ (\gamma^5)^2=1, \qquad \{\gamma^5,\gamma^\mu\}=0. \]

The chiral projectors are

\[ P_L=\frac{1-\gamma^5}{2}, \qquad P_R=\frac{1+\gamma^5}{2}, \]

so

\[ \psi_L=P_L\psi, \qquad \psi_R=P_R\psi, \qquad \psi=\psi_L+\psi_R. \tag{18}\]

In terms of these fields, the mass term is

\[ m\bar\psi\psi =m(\bar\psi_L\psi_R+\bar\psi_R\psi_L). \tag{19}\]

Mass therefore couples the two chiralities. When \(m=0\), the left- and right-handed equations separate.

Helicity is the projection of spin along momentum,

\[ h=\frac{\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf p}{|\mathbf p|}. \]

For a massive particle, a faster observer can reverse the direction of its momentum without reversing its spin, so helicity is frame-dependent. Chirality is defined by the Lorentz representation and is not changed by a proper Lorentz transformation. For massless positive-energy particles, chirality and helicity agree. For antiparticles, the relation must be handled with the corresponding \(v\) solutions.

The axial current is

\[ j_5^\mu=\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\gamma^5\psi. \]

Classically,

\[ \partial_\mu j_5^\mu=2im\bar\psi\gamma^5\psi. \tag{20}\]

It is conserved for a massless free Dirac field, although quantum interactions may introduce an anomaly.

3.5 Discrete symmetries

Parity reverses spatial coordinates,

\[ P:(t,\mathbf x)\to(t,-\mathbf x), \]

and acts on a Dirac field, up to an intrinsic phase, as

\[ \psi_P(t,\mathbf x)=\gamma^0\psi(t,-\mathbf x). \]

It exchanges left and right chirality. Charge conjugation transforms a particle field into its antiparticle field,

\[ \psi^C=C\bar\psi^{\,T}, \]

where

\[ C^{-1}\gamma^\mu C=-(\gamma^\mu)^T. \]

A Majorana field satisfies \(\psi^C=\psi\) and is therefore neutral. Time reversal is antiunitary; the complex conjugation contained in an antiunitary transformation is essential. A local Lorentz-invariant quantum field theory with the usual assumptions is invariant under the combined CPT transformation.

4 Vector fields

4.1 Massive vector: Proca theory

For a real vector field, define

\[ F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\mu. \]

The Proca Lagrangian is

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm P} =-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} +\frac12m^2A_\mu A^\mu. \tag{21}\]

Varying \(A_\nu\) gives

\[ \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}+m^2A^\nu=0. \tag{22}\]

Taking the divergence,

\[ \partial_\nu\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} +m^2\partial_\nu A^\nu=0. \]

The first term vanishes because \(F^{\mu\nu}\) is antisymmetric, while the derivatives are symmetric. If \(m\neq0\),

\[ \boxed{\partial_\mu A^\mu=0.} \tag{23}\]

Using this constraint in Equation 22 gives

\[ (\Box+m^2)A^\nu=0. \]

The four components are therefore reduced by one constraint, leaving three physical polarizations. In the rest frame they are the three spin projections of a massive spin-\(1\) particle.

For a plane wave \(A^\mu=\varepsilon^\mu(p)e^{-ip\cdot x}\),

\[ p_\mu\varepsilon^\mu(p)=0. \]

With the normalization \(\varepsilon^{(\lambda)}\cdot\varepsilon^{(\lambda')}=-\delta_{\lambda\lambda'}\), the polarization sum is

\[ \sum_{\lambda=1}^{3} \varepsilon_\mu^{(\lambda)}(p) \varepsilon_\nu^{(\lambda)*}(p) =-\eta_{\mu\nu}+\frac{p_\mu p_\nu}{m^2}. \tag{24}\]

4.2 Massless vector: Maxwell theory

Setting \(m=0\) gives

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm M}=-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}. \tag{25}\]

The action is invariant under

\[ A_\mu\to A_\mu+\partial_\mu\alpha(x). \tag{26}\]

This is a redundancy: potentials related by this transformation describe the same electric and magnetic fields. The Maxwell equation in vacuum is

\[ \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=0. \]

The Lorenz condition

\[ \partial_\mu A^\mu=0 \]

does not remove all gauge freedom. Transformations satisfying \(\Box\alpha=0\) preserve it. The constraint and the remaining gauge freedom leave two transverse photon polarizations.

For momentum along the \(z\) axis, they may be chosen as

\[ \varepsilon_{(1)}^\mu=(0,1,0,0), \qquad \varepsilon_{(2)}^\mu=(0,0,1,0). \]

Their circular combinations have helicities \(+1\) and \(-1\). There is no physical helicity-zero photon.

When the field is coupled to a current,

\[ \mathcal L=-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}-j_\mu A^\mu, \]

gauge invariance requires

\[ \partial_\mu j^\mu=0. \]

Indeed, the interaction changes by a boundary term plus \(\alpha\partial_\mu j^\mu\). Charge conservation is therefore necessary for a consistent gauge coupling.

The limit \(m\to0\) of Proca theory is not obtained by simply setting \(m=0\) in every expression. The term \(p_\mu p_\nu/m^2\) in Equation 24 becomes singular. Gauge symmetry appears only in the massless theory and changes the way degrees of freedom are counted.

NoteFrom Proca to gauge fields

Alexandru Proca introduced the relativistic equation for a massive spin-\(1\) field in 1936. Maxwell theory has a similar kinetic term, but its vanishing mass gives it a gauge symmetry. This changes both its constraint structure and its physical number of polarizations.

5 Canonical quantization

5.1 Real scalar field

The classical field is replaced by an operator and equal-time canonical commutators are imposed:

\[ [\phi(t,\mathbf x),\pi(t,\mathbf y)] =i\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x-\mathbf y), \]

\[ [\phi(t,\mathbf x),\phi(t,\mathbf y)]=0, \qquad [\pi(t,\mathbf x),\pi(t,\mathbf y)]=0. \tag{27}\]

The field is expanded in normal modes,

\[ \phi(x)=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}} \left[ a(\mathbf p)e^{-ip\cdot x} +a^\dagger(\mathbf p)e^{ip\cdot x} \right]. \tag{28}\]

The normalization is chosen so that

\[ [a(\mathbf p),a^\dagger(\mathbf q)] =(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q). \]

The vacuum satisfies \(a(\mathbf p)|0\rangle=0\). A one-particle state is

\[ |\mathbf p\rangle=\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}\, a^\dagger(\mathbf p)|0\rangle, \]

with relativistic normalization

\[ \langle\mathbf q|\mathbf p\rangle =2E_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^3 \delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q). \]

Substituting the expansion into the Hamiltonian gives

\[ H=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3} E_{\mathbf p} \left[a^\dagger(\mathbf p)a(\mathbf p) +\frac12(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(0)\right]. \]

The infinite second term is the vacuum energy. In a theory without gravity it is often removed by normal ordering,

\[ :H:=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3} E_{\mathbf p}a^\dagger(\mathbf p)a(\mathbf p). \]

For a complex scalar field, independent operators \(a\) and \(b\) are needed:

\[ \phi(x)=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}} \left[a(\mathbf p)e^{-ip\cdot x} +b^\dagger(\mathbf p)e^{ip\cdot x}\right]. \tag{29}\]

\(a^\dagger\) creates a particle and \(b^\dagger\) creates its antiparticle. The normal-ordered charge is proportional to

\[ Q=q\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3} \left[a^\dagger a-b^\dagger b\right]. \]

5.2 Dirac field and anticommutators

The Dirac field expansion is

\[ \psi(x)=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}} \sum_s\left[ b_s(\mathbf p)u_s(p)e^{-ip\cdot x} +d_s^\dagger(\mathbf p)v_s(p)e^{ip\cdot x} \right]. \tag{30}\]

\(b_s^\dagger\) creates a fermion and \(d_s^\dagger\) creates an antifermion. The non-zero anticommutators are

\[ \{b_r(\mathbf p),b_s^\dagger(\mathbf q)\} =(2\pi)^3\delta_{rs}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q), \]

\[ \{d_r(\mathbf p),d_s^\dagger(\mathbf q)\} =(2\pi)^3\delta_{rs}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q). \tag{31}\]

Because \((b_s^\dagger)^2=0\), two identical fermions cannot occupy the same one-particle state. Anticommutators also make the Hamiltonian bounded below and ensure microcausality for fermionic observables. The connection between half-integer spin and Fermi–Dirac statistics is a general theorem, not a special choice for the Dirac field.

After normal ordering,

\[ H=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3} \sum_sE_{\mathbf p} \left[b_s^\dagger b_s+d_s^\dagger d_s\right], \]

and

\[ Q=q\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3} \sum_s\left[b_s^\dagger b_s-d_s^\dagger d_s\right]. \]

Particles and antiparticles have positive energy and opposite charge.

5.3 Vector fields

A massive vector field has an expansion over its three physical polarizations,

\[ A_\mu(x)=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}} \sum_{\lambda=1}^{3} \left[ a_\lambda(\mathbf p)\varepsilon_\mu^{(\lambda)}(p)e^{-ip\cdot x} +a_\lambda^\dagger(\mathbf p)\varepsilon_\mu^{(\lambda)*}(p)e^{ip\cdot x} \right]. \]

For the electromagnetic field, direct canonical quantization is more delicate because \(A^0\) has no independent canonical momentum and gauge-related configurations describe the same state. One may quantize only the transverse modes, or first add a gauge-fixing term and later select the physical states. Both approaches must produce two photon helicities.

6 Propagators and causality

6.1 Scalar propagator

The scalar Feynman propagator is

\[ \Delta_F(x-y) =\langle0|T\phi(x)\phi(y)|0\rangle. \]

In momentum space,

\[ \boxed{ \Delta_F(x-y) =\int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4} \frac{i\,e^{-ip\cdot(x-y)}}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}.} \tag{32}\]

It satisfies

\[ (\Box_x+m^2)\Delta_F(x-y) =-i\delta^{(4)}(x-y). \]

The \(i\epsilon\) prescription specifies how the poles are passed and produces time ordering. Retarded and advanced Green functions obey the same differential equation but different boundary conditions; they answer different physical questions.

The field commutator is

\[ [\phi(x),\phi(y)]=i\Delta(x-y), \]

where \(\Delta\) is the Pauli–Jordan function. It vanishes for spacelike separation,

\[ (x-y)^2<0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad [\phi(x),\phi(y)]=0. \tag{33}\]

This is microcausality: measurements in spacelike-separated regions cannot influence one another.

6.2 Spinor and vector propagators

The Dirac propagator is

\[ S_F(x-y)=\langle0|T\psi(x)\bar\psi(y)|0\rangle. \]

Using

\[ (\not\!p-m)(\not\!p+m)=p^2-m^2, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{ S_F(x-y)=\int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4} \frac{i(\not\!p+m)e^{-ip\cdot(x-y)}} {p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}.} \tag{34}\]

For the Proca field,

\[ \boxed{ D_{\mu\nu}(p) =\frac{-i}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon} \left(\eta_{\mu\nu}-\frac{p_\mu p_\nu}{m^2}\right).} \tag{35}\]

The numerator reflects the massive spin-\(1\) polarization sum. For a massless gauge field, the kinetic operator cannot be inverted until a gauge is fixed. With

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{gf}} =-\frac{1}{2\xi}(\partial_\mu A^\mu)^2, \]

the photon propagator is

\[ D_{\mu\nu}(p) =\frac{-i}{p^2+i\epsilon} \left[ \eta_{\mu\nu}-(1-\xi)\frac{p_\mu p_\nu}{p^2} \right]. \tag{36}\]

The parameter \(\xi\) changes the propagator but not physical amplitudes coupled to conserved currents, because terms proportional to \(p_\mu\) then vanish.

A propagator is not a classical trajectory. It is a two-point correlation function and the inverse of a quadratic differential operator with specified boundary conditions.

7 Higher spins

7.1 Spin \(3/2\)

A Rarita–Schwinger field \(\psi_\mu\) carries both a vector and a spinor index. The vector-spinor contains more rotation sectors than spin \(3/2\), so constraints are required. For a free massive field one imposes

\[ (i\not\!\partial-m)\psi_\mu=0, \]

with

\[ \gamma^\mu\psi_\mu=0, \qquad \partial^\mu\psi_\mu=0. \tag{37}\]

The first removes the gamma trace and the second removes the longitudinal part. The remaining particle states have \(2s+1=4\) spin projections, together with four antiparticle states for a charged field.

For a massless spin-\(3/2\) field, a gauge-like redundancy

\[ \psi_\mu\to\psi_\mu+\partial_\mu\epsilon \]

removes unphysical components and leaves helicities \(\pm3/2\).

7.2 Spin \(2\)

A massive spin-\(2\) field is described by a symmetric tensor \(h_{\mu\nu}\). A symmetric tensor in four dimensions has ten components, while a massive spin-\(2\) particle has five physical polarizations. The Fierz–Pauli equations imply

\[ (\Box+m^2)h_{\mu\nu}=0, \]

\[ \partial^\mu h_{\mu\nu}=0, \qquad h^\mu{}_{\mu}=0. \tag{38}\]

The four divergence constraints and the trace constraint reduce ten components to five. The relative form of the mass term is important: a generic quadratic mass term introduces an extra unstable scalar mode.

In the massless case, linearised diffeomorphism invariance,

\[ h_{\mu\nu}\to h_{\mu\nu} +\partial_\mu\xi_\nu+\partial_\nu\xi_\mu, \]

leaves two helicities, \(+2\) and \(-2\). They correspond to the two polarizations of a gravitational wave.

Higher-spin theories make the general lesson of this chapter especially clear. Covariance alone is not enough. The field equations and their constraints must remove all lower-spin components without producing negative-energy or negative-norm states. Interactions make these consistency conditions even stronger.

8 Summary of the method

To analyse a free relativistic theory, begin with its Lorentz representation and action. Derive the equations instead of reading them directly from the final result. Then identify every constraint and every gauge redundancy before counting degrees of freedom. Solve the plane-wave equations and compare the remaining modes with the Poincaré labels \((m,s)\) or \((0,h)\). Finally, quantize the modes with the statistics required by their spin and read the propagator from the inverse quadratic operator.

For the main examples studied here, the result is straightforward. A real scalar field gives one neutral spin-\(0\) species. A Dirac field gives a spin-\(1/2\) particle and antiparticle, each with two spin states. A Proca field gives three massive spin-\(1\) polarizations. A Maxwell field gives two photon helicities. The same reasoning extends to spin \(3/2\) and spin \(2\), but the constraints become increasingly important.

11.1 A. Scalar fields, covariance, and degrees of freedom

11.1.1 Exercise 1 — Canonical dimensions of free fields ★

In \(d=4\) spacetime dimensions, the action is dimensionless. Find the mass dimensions of \(x^\mu\), \(\partial_\mu\), a scalar field \(\phi\), a Dirac spinor \(\psi\), and a vector field \(A_\mu\). Check that the mass terms \(m^2\phi^2\), \(m\overline\psi\psi\), and \(m^2A_\mu A^\mu\) have the dimension of a Lagrangian density.

NoteSolution

In natural units, length has the inverse dimension of energy. Therefore,

\[ [x^\mu]=-1, \qquad [\partial_\mu]=1, \qquad [d^4x]=-4. \]

Since \(S=\int d^4x\,\mathcal L\) is dimensionless, \([\mathcal L]=4\).

The scalar kinetic term gives

\[ [\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi] =2+2[\phi]=4, \]

so

\[ \boxed{[\phi]=1.} \]

For the Dirac field,

\[ [\overline\psi i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi] =1+2[\psi]=4, \]

because the gamma matrices are dimensionless. Thus,

\[ \boxed{[\psi]=[\overline\psi]=\frac32.} \]

Finally, \(F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\mu\) and \(F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\) require

\[ 2(1+[A_\mu])=4, \]

so

\[ \boxed{[A_\mu]=1.} \]

The three mass terms then have the respective dimensions

\[ [m^2\phi^2]=2+2=4, \quad [m\overline\psi\psi]=1+\frac32+\frac32=4, \quad [m^2A^2]=2+2=4. \]

This calculation will later help us quickly classify interactions by their dimension.

11.1.2 Exercise 2 — Klein–Gordon plane waves ★

Consider a real scalar field satisfying

\[ (\Box+m^2)\phi=0. \]

  1. Insert a plane wave \(\phi(x)=A e^{-ip\cdot x}\) and recover the dispersion relation.
  2. Calculate the phase velocity and the group velocity.
  3. Show that the group velocity remains less than or equal to \(1\).
NoteSolution

The derivative acts on the plane wave as

\[ \partial_\mu e^{-ip\cdot x}=-ip_\mu e^{-ip\cdot x}, \qquad \Box e^{-ip\cdot x}=-p^2e^{-ip\cdot x}. \]

The equation therefore gives

\[ (-p^2+m^2)A e^{-ip\cdot x}=0. \]

For a nonzero solution,

\[ \boxed{p^2=m^2}, \qquad (p^0)^2=\mathbf p^2+m^2. \]

The positive-energy branch is \(E_{\mathbf p}=\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2}\). For \(|\mathbf p|=p\),

\[ v_{\rm ph}=\frac{E_p}{p} =\sqrt{1+\frac{m^2}{p^2}}, \]

which can be greater than \(1\). This is not the speed at which information travels. The velocity of a wave packet is

\[ v_{\rm g}=\frac{dE_p}{dp} =\frac{p}{\sqrt{p^2+m^2}} =\frac{p}{E_p}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{0\leq v_{\rm g}<1\quad(m>0),} \]

and \(v_{\rm g}=1\) in the massless limit. We also find \(v_{\rm ph}v_{\rm g}=1\).

11.1.3 Exercise 3 — From the action to Klein–Gordon ★

For a real scalar field,

\[ S[\phi]=\int d^4x\left( \frac12\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi -\frac12m^2\phi^2\right). \]

Carefully derive the Euler–Lagrange equation. Identify the boundary term and explain why it does not contribute.

NoteSolution

Make an arbitrary variation \(\phi\to\phi+\delta\phi\):

\[ \delta S =\int d^4x\left( \partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\delta\phi -m^2\phi\,\delta\phi\right). \]

Integrating the first term by parts gives

\[ \partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\delta\phi =\partial_\mu(\partial^\mu\phi\,\delta\phi) -(\Box\phi)\delta\phi. \]

We obtain

\[ \delta S =\int_{\partial\Omega}d\Sigma_\mu\, \partial^\mu\phi\,\delta\phi -\int_\Omega d^4x\, (\Box\phi+m^2\phi)\delta\phi. \]

We fix the field at the boundary, so \(\delta\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=0\), or assume that the variation has compact support. The boundary term vanishes. Since \(\delta\phi\) is arbitrary inside,

\[ \boxed{(\Box+m^2)\phi=0.} \]

Integration by parts is not a trick: it transfers the derivative from the variation to the field so that setting \(\delta S\) to zero gives a local equation.

11.1.4 Exercise 4 — Energy–momentum tensor of the scalar field ★★

Starting from the real Lagrangian in the previous exercise:

  1. calculate the canonical tensor \(T^{\mu\nu}\) associated with translations;
  2. show that \(\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=0\) for solutions;
  3. write \(T^{00}\) explicitly and check that the energy is positive.
NoteSolution

For a translation, the canonical tensor is

\[ T^{\mu\nu} =\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)} \partial^\nu\phi-\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathcal L. \]

Since \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)=\partial^\mu\phi\),

\[ \boxed{ T^{\mu\nu}=\partial^\mu\phi\,\partial^\nu\phi -\eta^{\mu\nu} \left(\frac12\partial_\rho\phi\partial^\rho\phi -\frac12m^2\phi^2\right). } \]

Its divergence is

\[ \begin{aligned} \partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} ={}&(\Box\phi)\partial^\nu\phi +\partial^\mu\phi\,\partial_\mu\partial^\nu\phi -\partial^\nu\mathcal L. \end{aligned} \]

But

\[ \partial^\nu\mathcal L =\partial_\rho\phi\,\partial^\nu\partial^\rho\phi -m^2\phi\partial^\nu\phi. \]

The terms containing two derivatives cancel, so

\[ \partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} =(\Box\phi+m^2\phi)\partial^\nu\phi=0. \]

Finally,

\[ \mathcal L =\frac12\dot\phi^2- rac12(\boldsymbol\nabla\phi)^2 -\frac12m^2\phi^2, \]

so

\[ \boxed{ T^{00}=\frac12\dot\phi^2 +\frac12(\boldsymbol\nabla\phi)^2 +\frac12m^2\phi^2\geq0. } \]

Positivity is clear here for \(m^2\geq0\).

11.1.5 Exercise 5 — Current of the complex scalar field ★★

Consider

\[ \mathcal L=\partial_\mu\phi^*\partial^\mu\phi-m^2\phi^*\phi \]

and the global transformation \(\phi\to e^{-iq\alpha}\phi\).

  1. Derive the Noether current.
  2. Check its conservation directly using the equations of motion.
  3. Explain why the same current is identically zero for a real field.
NoteSolution

To first order in \(\alpha\),

\[ \delta\phi=-iq\alpha\phi, \qquad \delta\phi^*=+iq\alpha\phi^*. \]

After removing the constant factor \(\alpha\), the Noether current is

\[ \begin{aligned} j^\mu &=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}(-iq\phi) +\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi^*)}(iq\phi^*)\\ &=-iq\,\partial^\mu\phi^*\phi +iq\,\partial^\mu\phi\phi^*. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ j^\mu=iq\left(\phi^*\partial^\mu\phi -\phi\partial^\mu\phi^*\right). } \]

Its divergence is

\[ \partial_\mu j^\mu =iq\left(\phi^*\Box\phi-\phi\Box\phi^*\right), \]

because the cross terms cancel. Using \(\Box\phi=-m^2\phi\) and its complex conjugate,

\[ \partial_\mu j^\mu =iq(-m^2\phi^*\phi+m^2\phi\phi^*)=0. \]

If \(\phi=\phi^*\), the two terms in the current are equal and opposite: \(j^\mu=0\). A real field does not have the continuous phase symmetry that distinguishes particles from antiparticles.

11.1.6 Exercise 6 — Negative frequencies and antiparticles ★★

  2. Explain why the quantized theory nevertheless contains no negative-energy particles.
  3. Give the interpretation of the two creation operators.
NoteSolution

We can write

\[ e^{+ip\cdot x}=e^{-i(-p)\cdot x}. \]

The four-vector \(k=-p\) has \(k^0=-E_{\mathbf p}\): classically, this is therefore a negative-frequency solution.

After quantization, the expansion takes the form

\[ \phi(x)=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}} \left[a(\mathbf p)e^{-ipx} +b^\dagger(\mathbf p)e^{+ipx}\right]. \]

The coefficient of the negative-frequency mode becomes a creation operator. The normal-ordered Hamiltonian is

Thus, \(a^\dagger\) creates a particle with energy \(E_{\mathbf p}\) and charge \(q\), while \(b^\dagger\) creates an antiparticle with energy \(E_{\mathbf p}\) and charge \(-q\):

11.1.7 Exercise 7 — Counting Cauchy data ★★

Compare a real scalar field, a complex scalar field, and a free Dirac field. For each one, state:

  1. the number of real components of the field;
  2. the time order of the equation;
  3. the independent data that must be specified on a hypersurface \(t=t_0\).

Why is the number of components alone not yet the number of polarizations of a particle?

NoteSolution

A real scalar has one real component, and the Klein–Gordon equation is second order. We must therefore specify

\[ \phi(t_0,\mathbf x), \qquad \dot\phi(t_0,\mathbf x), \]

that is, two real functions.

A complex scalar is equivalent to two real scalars. We must specify \(\phi\) and \(\dot\phi\), which means four real functions. After quantization, this doubling allows independent particles and antiparticles.

A Dirac spinor has four complex components, or eight real components, but the Dirac equation is first order in time. It is enough to specify \(\psi(t_0,\mathbf x)\): eight real functions, with no independent data for \(\dot\psi\).

These data include the particle and antiparticle branches. The number of polarizations at fixed momentum is found after imposing the equation of motion, any constraints, and the energy condition. For example, a Dirac spinor has two spin states for the particle and two for the antiparticle, not four spin orientations for one particle.

11.1.8 Exercise 8 — Local representation and physical particle ★★★

Explain the following two statements, which do not contradict each other:

  • a scalar field transforms in the \((0,0)\) representation of the Lorentz group;
  • a particle created by this field carries a unitary representation of the Poincaré group characterized by \((m,s=0)\).

Also show why the finite matrix that transforms the components of a vector field is not required to be unitary.

NoteSolution

A Lorentz transformation acts on a field according to

\[ U(\Lambda)\Phi_A(x)U^{-1}(\Lambda) =D(\Lambda^{-1})_A{}^B\Phi_B(\Lambda x). \]

Two distinct objects are involved:

  • \(U(\Lambda)\) acts on the Hilbert space of states; it must be unitary to preserve probabilities;
  • \(D(\Lambda)\) acts on the local indices of the field and belongs to a finite-dimensional representation of the Lorentz group.

For a scalar, \(D=1\): the value of the field is not mixed with other components. However, the state

\[ |\mathbf p\rangle=a^\dagger(\mathbf p)|0\rangle \]

is transformed by \(U(\Lambda)\) into a state with momentum \(\Lambda p\). The set of these states carries a representation of the Poincaré group, whose invariants are \(P^2=m^2\) and \(W^2=0\), so \(s=0\).

The Lorentz group is noncompact. Its nontrivial finite-dimensional representations, such as \((1/2,1/2)\) for a four-vector, are generally not unitary for a positive inner product. This is not a problem: \(A^\mu\) is not a state vector, and its four components are not four probabilities. Physical unitarity belongs to \(U(\Lambda)\) on Fock space.

11.2.1 Exercise 9 — First consequences of the Clifford algebra ★

Starting from \(\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu\}=2\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathbf1_4\), show that

\[ (\gamma^0)^2=\mathbf1_4, \qquad (\gamma^i)^2=-\mathbf1_4, \qquad \gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu=-\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu\quad(\mu\neq\nu). \]

Deduce that \((\not\! p)^2=p^2\mathbf1_4\).

NoteSolution

Setting \(\mu=\nu\), the Clifford relation becomes

\[ 2(\gamma^\mu)^2=2\eta^{\mu\mu}\mathbf1_4. \]

With the \((+---)\) signature, we immediately obtain

\[ (\gamma^0)^2=\mathbf1_4, \qquad (\gamma^i)^2=-\mathbf1_4. \]

If \(\mu\neq\nu\), then \(\eta^{\mu\nu}=0\), so the anticommutator vanishes.

Finally,

\[ (\not\! p)^2 =\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu p_\mu p_\nu. \]

The product \(p_\mu p_\nu\) is symmetric, so only the symmetric part of the gamma matrices contributes:

\[ \gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu p_\mu p_\nu =\frac12\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu\}p_\mu p_\nu =\eta^{\mu\nu}p_\mu p_\nu\mathbf1_4. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{(\not\! p)^2=p^2\mathbf1_4.} \]

11.2.2 Exercise 10 — Dirac implies Klein–Gordon ★★

Show that a solution of

\[ (i\not\!\partial-m)\psi=0 \]

also satisfies the Klein–Gordon equation component by component. Why is the converse false?

NoteSolution

Multiply the equation on the left by the conjugate operator \(i\not\!\partial+m\):

\[ (i\not\!\partial+m)(i\not\!\partial-m)\psi=0. \]

The cross terms cancel, and

\[ (i\not\!\partial)^2 =-\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\partial_\mu\partial_\nu. \]

Since the derivatives commute, the antisymmetric part of \(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\) does not contribute. This leaves

\[ (i\not\!\partial)^2 =-\frac12\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu\} \partial_\mu\partial_\nu =-\Box. \]

Therefore,

\[ -(\Box+m^2)\psi=0, \]

that is,

\[ \boxed{(\Box+m^2)\psi=0.} \]

The converse is false because Klein–Gordon only imposes the mass-shell condition on each of the four components. The Dirac equation adds linear relations between these components and selects the spin-\(1/2\) content. Every Dirac solution is a Klein–Gordon solution, but an arbitrary collection of four scalar solutions is not a physical Dirac spinor.

11.2.3 Exercise 11 — Dirac current and positivity ★★

Starting from the Dirac equation and its adjoint equation:

  1. show that \(j^\mu=q\overline\psi\gamma^\mu\psi\) is conserved;
  2. calculate \(j^0\);
  3. compare its positivity with that of the complex scalar charge density.
NoteSolution

The equation and its adjoint are

\[ i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi=m\psi, \qquad i(\partial_\mu\overline\psi)\gamma^\mu=-m\overline\psi. \]

The divergence is

\[ \partial_\mu j^\mu =q(\partial_\mu\overline\psi)\gamma^\mu\psi +q\overline\psi\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi. \]

The first equation gives \(\overline\psi\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi=-im\overline\psi\psi\), while the adjoint gives \((\partial_\mu\overline\psi)\gamma^\mu\psi=+im\overline\psi\psi\). The two terms cancel:

\[ \boxed{\partial_\mu j^\mu=0.} \]

\[ j^0=q\overline\psi\gamma^0\psi =q\psi^\dagger\gamma^0\gamma^0\psi =q\psi^\dagger\psi. \]

For \(q>0\), \(j^0/q\) is positive at the one-particle wave-function level. The Klein–Gordon current contains a time derivative and does not have a definite sign. \[ \overline{\psi^C}\Gamma\psi^C =\overline\psi\,(C^{-1}\Gamma C)^T\psi \]

with the exchange sign already included in this identity.

For \(\Gamma=\gamma^\mu\),

\[ (C^{-1}\gamma^\mu C)^T =\big[-(\gamma^\mu)^T\big]^T =-\gamma^\mu, \]

so

\[ \boxed{j_V^\mu\xrightarrow C-j_V^\mu.} \]

For \(\Gamma=\gamma^\mu\gamma^5\), use \(C^{-1}\gamma^5C=(\gamma^5)^T\) and the anticommutation of \(\gamma^5\) with \(\gamma^\mu\). The two signs cancel:

\[ \boxed{j_A^\mu\xrightarrow C+j_A^\mu.} \]

The charge \(Q=\int d^3x\,j_V^0\) therefore changes sign: charge conjugation exchanges particles and antiparticles. The axial current measures a spin and chirality structure that is not reversed in the same way.

11.2.13 Exercise 21 — Majorana condition ★★★

A Majorana field satisfies \(\psi=\psi^C\).

  1. Show that its vector current vanishes.
  2. Explain why such a field cannot carry a nonzero conserved \(U(1)\) charge.
  3. Compare the number of degrees of freedom of a massive Dirac field and a massive Majorana field.
NoteSolution

The vector current is odd under \(C\):

\[ \overline{\psi^C}\gamma^\mu\psi^C =-\overline\psi\gamma^\mu\psi. \]

If \(\psi=\psi^C\), the left-hand side is the same current. We obtain

\[ j_V^\mu=-j_V^\mu, \]

so

\[ \boxed{j_V^\mu=0.} \]

A continuous transformation \(\psi\to e^{-iq\alpha}\psi\) is compatible with \(\psi=\psi^C\) only if \(q=0\), because charge conjugation replaces the phase by its complex conjugate. A Majorana particle must therefore be neutral under this charge.

A massive Dirac field has two spin states for the particle and two for the antiparticle: four states at fixed momentum. The Majorana condition identifies the particle with its antiparticle, leaving two spin states:

\[ \boxed{N_{\rm Dirac}=4, \qquad N_{\rm Majorana}=2.} \]

The condition does not remove a spin direction; it removes the independence of the particle and antiparticle sectors.

11.3.1 Exercise 22 — Proca constraint ★★

The Proca field satisfies

\[ \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}+m^2A^\nu=0, \qquad F^{\mu\nu}=\partial^\mu A^\nu-\partial^\nu A^\mu. \]

Take the divergence of the equation, obtain the constraint on \(A^\mu\), then show that each component satisfies Klein–Gordon.

NoteSolution

Apply \(\partial_\nu\):

\[ \partial_\nu\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} +m^2\partial_\nu A^\nu=0. \]

The first term is zero. Indeed, \(\partial_\nu\partial_\mu\) is symmetric under \(\mu\leftrightarrow\nu\), while \(F^{\mu\nu}\) is antisymmetric. For \(m\neq0\),

\[ \boxed{\partial_\mu A^\mu=0.} \]

Now expand the equation:

\[ \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} =\Box A^\nu-\partial^\nu(\partial\cdot A). \]

The constraint makes the second term vanish, so

\[ \boxed{(\Box+m^2)A^\nu=0.} \]

Here, the condition \(\partial\cdot A=0\) is not a gauge choice: it is a dynamical consequence of the massive equation. It removes one combination of the four components, leaving three physical polarizations.

11.3.2 Exercise 23 — Polarizations of a massive vector ★★★

For a massive particle with momentum \(p^\mu=(E,0,0,p)\), construct three polarization vectors \(\varepsilon_\lambda^\mu\) satisfying

up to phases. The three states correspond to the spin projections \(-1,0,+1\) of a massive particle.

11.3.3 Exercise 24 — Proca polarization sum ★★★

Show that the sum over the three physical polarizations of a massive vector is

\[ \sum_{\lambda=1}^3 \varepsilon_\lambda^\mu(p) \varepsilon_\lambda^{*\nu}(p) =-\eta^{\mu\nu}+\frac{p^\mu p^\nu}{m^2}. \]

\[ p_\mu S^{\mu\nu}=(A+Bp^2)p^\nu=0. \]

\[ \varepsilon_i^\mu=(0,\mathbf e_i), \qquad i=1,2,3. \]

The sum is then \(\operatorname{diag}(0,1,1,1)\). But

\[ -\eta^{\mu\nu}+\frac{p^\mu p^\nu}{m^2} =\operatorname{diag}(0,1,1,1) \]

for \(p=(m,\mathbf0)\). Therefore \(A=-1\), and

Its mixed trace is \(S^\mu{}_\mu=-3\), which encodes the three states, since each vector has negative norm with our signature.

11.3.4 Exercise 25 — Gauge invariance of Maxwell theory ★

Show that the transformation

\[ A_\mu\longrightarrow A_\mu+\partial_\mu\alpha \]

leaves \(F_{\mu\nu}\) and the Lagrangian \(-\tfrac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\) invariant. Why is the term \(\tfrac12m^2A_\mu A^\mu\) not invariant?

NoteSolution

After the transformation,

\[ F'_{\mu\nu} =\partial_\mu(A_\nu+\partial_\nu\alpha) -\partial_\nu(A_\mu+\partial_\mu\alpha). \]

The second derivatives cancel because they commute:

\[ F'_{\mu\nu}=F_{\mu\nu}. \]

The Maxwell Lagrangian is therefore exactly invariant.

In contrast,

\[ A_\mu A^\mu \longrightarrow A_\mu A^\mu+2A^\mu\partial_\mu\alpha +\partial_\mu\alpha\partial^\mu\alpha, \]

which is not equal to the original term, even up to a boundary term for an arbitrary function \(\alpha(x)\). A Proca mass therefore breaks gauge redundancy and makes the longitudinal polarization physical.

11.3.5 Exercise 26 — Counting photon polarizations ★★

11.3.6 Exercise 27 — Coupling to a conserved current ★★

\[ \delta S_{\rm int} =-\int d^4x\,J^\mu\partial_\mu\alpha. \]

11.3.7 Exercise 28 — The \(m\to0\) limit of Proca theory ★★★

\[ \varepsilon_L^\mu=\frac1m(|\mathbf p|,0,0,E) \]

NoteSolution

At fixed nonzero momentum,

\[ \varepsilon_L^\mu\sim\frac{p^\mu}{m} \]

as \(m\to0\). The longitudinal polarization therefore diverges in this basis. The propagator

\[ D_{\rm P}^{\mu\nu}(p) =\frac{-i}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon} \left(\eta^{\mu\nu}-\frac{p^\mu p^\nu}{m^2}\right) \]

also contains an apparently divergent term \(p^\mu p^\nu/m^2\).

\[ \mathcal A\propto J_\mu D_{\rm P}^{\mu\nu}J'_\nu, \]

11.3.8 Exercise 29 — Electric and magnetic fields under \(P\) and \(T\) ★★

Using \(\mathbf E=-\boldsymbol\nabla A^0-\partial_t\mathbf A\) and \(\mathbf B=\boldsymbol\nabla\times\mathbf A\), recover the transformations

NoteSolution

The vacuum contractions give

\[ \Delta_F(z) =\theta(z^0)\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3,2E_{\mathbf p}}e^{-ipz} +\theta(-z^0)\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3,2E_{\mathbf p}}e^{ipz}. \]

Now consider the covariant integral. The denominator factorizes as

\[ (p^0)^2-E_{\mathbf p}^2+i\epsilon \simeq(p^0-E_{\mathbf p}+i\epsilon) (p^0+E_{\mathbf p}-i\epsilon). \]

The pole \(+E_{\mathbf p}\) lies slightly below the real axis, and the pole \(-E_{\mathbf p}\) lies slightly above it.

For \(z^0>0\), the factor \(e^{-ip^0z^0}\) decreases in the lower half-plane. The clockwise contour selects the pole \(p^0=+E_{\mathbf p}-i\epsilon\) and produces the first term.

For \(z^0<0\), close the contour in the upper half-plane; the pole \(p^0=-E_{\mathbf p}+i\epsilon\) produces the second term after \(\mathbf p\to-\mathbf p\).

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \Delta_F(z)=\int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4} \frac{i e^{-ipz}}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}. } \]

The \(i\epsilon\) prescription therefore encodes time ordering exactly; it is not merely an arbitrary regularization.

11.4.12 Exercise 41 — Scalar Green equation ★★

Apply \((\Box_x+m^2)\) to the Feynman propagator and show that

\[ (\Box_x+m^2)\Delta_F(x-y)=-i\delta^{(4)}(x-y). \]

Why is the right-hand side not zero even though every free mode satisfies Klein–Gordon?

NoteSolution

Inside the integral,

\[ (\Box_x+m^2)e^{-ip(x-y)}=(-p^2+m^2)e^{-ip(x-y)}. \]

Thus,

\[ \begin{aligned} (\Box_x+m^2)\Delta_F(x-y) &=\int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4} (-p^2+m^2) \frac{i e^{-ip(x-y)}}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}\\ &=-i\int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4}e^{-ip(x-y)}, \end{aligned} \]

in the distributional limit \(\epsilon\to0^+\). By Fourier transformation,

\[ \boxed{(\Box_x+m^2)\Delta_F(x-y)=-i\delta^{(4)}(x-y).} \]

For \(x\neq y\), the right-hand side is zero and the propagator does satisfy the homogeneous equation. The point source appears at \(x=y\) because the time derivatives also act on the functions \(\theta(x^0-y^0)\) in the time-ordered product. The propagator is the inverse of the kinetic operator, not a regular free solution everywhere.

11.4.13 Exercise 42 — Dirac propagator from the scalar propagator ★★

Setting

\[ S_F(x-y)=(i\not\!\partial_x+m)\Delta_F(x-y), \]

show that

\[ (i\not\!\partial_x-m)S_F(x-y)=i\delta^{(4)}(x-y) \]

and recover \(S_F(p)\).

NoteSolution

Compose the two operators:

\[ (i\not\!\partial-m)(i\not\!\partial+m) =-\Box-m^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ (i\not\!\partial_x-m)S_F(x-y) =-(\Box_x+m^2)\Delta_F(x-y). \]

The scalar Green equation then gives

\[ \boxed{ (i\not\!\partial_x-m)S_F(x-y) =i\delta^{(4)}(x-y). } \]

In momentum space, \(i\partial_\mu\) acts as \(p_\mu\), so

\[ \boxed{ S_F(p)=\frac{i(\not\! p+m)}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}. } \]

We can check directly that

\[ (\not\! p-m)\frac{i(\not\! p+m)}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon} \longrightarrow i\mathbf1_4. \]

The numerator is therefore required by the matrix inversion of the Dirac operator and, on the mass shell, is the same as the positive-energy spin sum.

11.4.14 Exercise 43 — Inverting the Proca operator ★★★

The Proca kinetic operator in momentum space is

\[ K^{\mu\nu}=(-p^2+m^2)\eta^{\mu\nu}+p^\mu p^\nu. \]

Look for its inverse in the form \(D^{\mu\nu}=A\eta^{\mu\nu}+Bp^\mu p^\nu\) and recover the Proca propagator, including the factors of \(i\).

NoteSolution

Contract the two tensors:

\[ K^{\mu\rho}D_\rho{}^\nu =(-p^2+m^2)A\delta^\mu{}_{\nu} +\left[(-p^2+m^2)B+A+Bp^2\right]p^\mu p_\nu. \]

The coefficient of the second term simplifies to \(A+m^2B\). For \(D\) to be the propagator, we require \(KD=i\delta\) according to the quadratic-action convention. Choose

\[ A=\frac{-i}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}, \qquad B=-\frac{A}{m^2}. \]

We obtain

\[ \boxed{ D_{\rm P}^{\mu\nu}(p) =\frac{-i}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon} \left(\eta^{\mu\nu}-\frac{p^\mu p^\nu}{m^2}\right). } \]

On the mass shell, the opposite numerator,

\[ -\eta^{\mu\nu}+\frac{p^\mu p^\nu}{m^2}, \]

is exactly the sum over the three physical polarizations. Off shell, the same structure is the extension required to invert the kinetic operator.

11.4.15 Exercise 44 — Independence from the gauge parameter ★★★

The photon propagator in a covariant gauge is

\[ D_\xi^{\mu\nu}(p) =\frac{-i}{p^2+i\epsilon} \left[\eta^{\mu\nu}-(1-\xi)\frac{p^\mu p^\nu}{p^2}\right]. \]

Show that an amplitude \(J_\mu D_\xi^{\mu\nu}J'_\nu\) between two conserved currents is independent of \(\xi\). Why must we still fix a gauge?

NoteSolution

The part that depends on \(\xi\) is proportional to

\[ J_\mu p^\mu\,p^\nu J'_\nu =(p\cdot J)(p\cdot J'). \]

Current conservation in momentum space requires

\[ p_\mu J^\mu=0, \qquad p_\nu J'^{\nu}=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ J_\mu D_\xi^{\mu\nu}J'_\nu =\frac{-i}{p^2+i\epsilon}J_\mu\eta^{\mu\nu}J'_\nu, } \]

with no dependence on \(\xi\).

It would still be wrong not to fix the gauge. Without a gauge-fixing term, the Maxwell operator has null directions \(A_\mu\propto p_\mu\) and is not invertible. 11.5.1 Exercise 45 — Counting spin \(3/2\) ★★★

A Rarita–Schwinger field \(\psi_\mu\) is both a four-vector and a Dirac spinor.

  1. How many complex components does it initially have?
  2. Explain the role of the conditions

\[ (i\not\!\partial-m)\psi_\mu=0, \qquad \gamma^\mu\psi_\mu=0, \qquad \partial^\mu\psi_\mu=0. \]

  1. How many physical states are expected for a massive spin-\(3/2\) particle?
NoteSolution

The naive product has

\[ 4\ \text{vector components} \times4\ \text{spinor components} =16 \]

complex components. But the vector-spinor representation contains several spins. In the rest frame, the product of the rotation content shows this mixing:

\[ (1\oplus0)\otimes\frac12 =\frac32\oplus\frac12\oplus\frac12. \]

The Dirac equation imposes massive propagation for each vector index and relates the particle and antiparticle components. The gamma-trace \(\gamma^\mu\psi_\mu=0\) removes one spin-\(1/2\) sector. Transversality \(\partial^\mu\psi_\mu=0\) removes another unwanted longitudinal component. These constraints are therefore not decorative: they isolate the desired irreducible representation.

A massive spin-\(s\) particle has \(2s+1\) polarizations. Therefore,

\[ \boxed{2\times\frac32+1=4} \]

states for the particle, and four for the antiparticle if the field is of Dirac type. A Majorana-type field identifies these two sectors.

11.5.2 Exercise 46 — Fierz–Pauli constraints ★★★

A massive spin-\(2\) field is described by a symmetric tensor \(h_{\mu\nu}\). Starting from its ten components in four dimensions, explain how the conditions

\[ \partial^\mu h_{\mu\nu}=0, \qquad h^\mu{}_{\mu}=0 \]

lead to the five expected polarizations. What would happen if the scalar trace were not removed?

NoteSolution

A symmetric \(4\times4\) tensor has

\[ \frac{4(4+1)}2=10 \]

components. Transversality gives four independent relations for a massive plane wave,

\[ p^\mu\varepsilon_{\mu\nu}=0, \]

which reduces the count to \(10-4=6\). The trace condition

\[ \varepsilon^\mu{}_{\mu}=0 \]

removes one more component:

\[ \boxed{10-4-1=5=2s+1\quad(s=2).} \]

In the rest frame, transversality removes the time components \(h_{0\mu}\); what remains is a symmetric \(3\times3\) spatial tensor with six components. Its trace part is a scalar under rotations. Removing it leaves a symmetric traceless spatial tensor with five components, which realizes spin \(2\).

If the trace were not removed, the field would also propagate a spin-\(0\) mode. Depending on the form of the action, this extra mode may be unwanted and can even become a ghost if its kinetic term has the wrong sign. The special tuning of the Fierz–Pauli mass term is precisely what avoids this pathological scalar degree of freedom at the linear level.

12.0.1 Problem 1 — The charged scalar field, from Noether to Fock ★★★

Consider a complex field with Lagrangian

\[ \mathcal L=\partial_\mu\phi^*\partial^\mu\phi-m^2\phi^*\phi. \]

  1. Derive the equations of motion for \(\phi\) and \(\phi^*\) by treating them as independent variables.
  2. Calculate the conjugate momenta \(\Pi_\phi\) and \(\Pi_{\phi^*}\), then the Hamiltonian density.
  3. Recover the current associated with \(\phi\to e^{-iq\alpha}\phi\).
  4. Quantize the field and write the equal-time commutators.
  5. Deduce the algebras of the \(a,b\) operators.
  6. Calculate the operators \(:H:\) and \(:Q:\).
  7. Use commutators to show the charges of the states \(a^\dagger|0\rangle\) and \(b^\dagger|0\rangle\).
  8. Construct the nonzero propagator \(\langle0|T\{\phi(x)\phi^\dagger(y)\}|0\rangle\). Why does \(\langle0|T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\}|0\rangle\) vanish?
NoteSolution

1. Equations of motion. The variations of \(\phi\) et \(\phi^*\) are independent. For \(\phi^*\),

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi^*}=-m^2\phi, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi^*)}=\partial^\mu\phi. \]

The Euler–Lagrange equation gives

\[ (\Box+m^2)\phi=0. \]

Varying \(\phi\) gives the conjugate equation \((\Box+m^2)\phi^*=0\).

2. Hamiltonian structure. Separating time,

\[ \mathcal L=\dot\phi^*\dot\phi -\boldsymbol\nabla\phi^*\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla\phi -m^2\phi^*\phi. \]

The momenta are

\[ \Pi_\phi=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\phi}=\dot\phi^*, \qquad \Pi_{\phi^*}=\dot\phi. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathcal H =\Pi_\phi\dot\phi+\Pi_{\phi^*}\dot\phi^*-\mathcal L =|\dot\phi|^2+|\boldsymbol\nabla\phi|^2+m^2|\phi|^2. } \]

3. Internal symmetry. For \(\delta\phi=-iq\alpha\phi\) et \(\delta\phi^*=iq\alpha\phi^*\),

\[ \boxed{j^\mu=iq(\phi^*\partial^\mu\phi-\phi\partial^\mu\phi^*),} \]

and the Klein–Gordon equations ensure \(\partial_\mu j^\mu=0\).

4. Quantization. Impose

\[ [\phi(t,\mathbf x),\Pi_\phi(t,\mathbf y)] =i\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x-\mathbf y), \]

\[ [\phi^*(t,\mathbf x),\Pi_{\phi^*}(t,\mathbf y)] =i\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x-\mathbf y), \]

with all other fundamental commutators equal to zero. The expansions are

\[ \phi(x)=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}} \left[a(\mathbf p)e^{-ipx}+b^\dagger(\mathbf p)e^{ipx}\right], \]

\[ \phi^*(x)=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}} \left[a^\dagger(\mathbf p)e^{ipx}+b(\mathbf p)e^{-ipx}\right]. \]

5. Mode algebra. The canonical relations require

\[ [a(\mathbf p),a^\dagger(\mathbf q)] =[b(\mathbf p),b^\dagger(\mathbf q)] =(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q), \]

with all commutators between the \(a\) and \(b\) families equal to zero.

6. Conserved observables. Inserting the modes and then normal ordering gives

\[ \boxed{ :H:=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3}E_{\mathbf p} (a^\dagger a+b^\dagger b), } \]

\[ \boxed{ :Q:=q\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3} (a^\dagger a-b^\dagger b). } \]

Each excitation has positive energy; the two species have opposite charges.

7. Charges of the quanta. Since \(Q|0\rangle=0\),

\[ Q a^\dagger|0\rangle=[Q,a^\dagger]|0\rangle =q a^\dagger|0\rangle, \]

\[ Q b^\dagger|0\rangle=[Q,b^\dagger]|0\rangle =-q b^\dagger|0\rangle. \]

8. Propagation. The only possible contraction pairs a particle or antiparticle annihilation operator with its creation operator:

\[ \boxed{ \langle0|T\{\phi(x)\phi^\dagger(y)\}|0\rangle =\int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4} \frac{i e^{-ip(x-y)}}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}. } \]

In \(\langle0|T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\}|0\rangle\), the possible operators are \(a,b^\dagger\) in both factors. There is no same-species \(aa^\dagger\) pair and no \(bb^\dagger\) pair in the correct field, so the expectation value is zero. Charge gives the same conclusion: the product \(\phi\phi\) carries nonzero charge, and its expectation value in a neutral vacuum must vanish.

12.0.2 Problem 2 — Anatomy of a Dirac wave ★★★

Consider a particle of mass \(m\) and momentum \(p^\mu=(E,0,0,p)\), \(p>0\).

  1. Construct the two helicity spinors \(u_h(p)\) with \(h=\pm1\) in the Dirac representation.
  2. Check \((\not\! p-m)u_h=0\) and \(u_h^\dagger u_{h'}=2E\delta_{hh'}\).
  3. Calculate \(\overline u_hu_h\) and \(\overline u_h\gamma^\mu u_h\).
  4. Calculate the chiral weights \(u_h^\dagger P_{R,L}u_h/(2E)\).
  5. Study the ultrarelativistic and nonrelativistic limits.
  6. Show that the sum over the two helicities gives \(\not\! p+m\).
NoteSolution

1. Helicity basis. For momentum along \(+z\),

\[ \sigma^3\xi_+=+\xi_+, \qquad \sigma^3\xi_-=-\xi_-, \]

with \(\xi_+=(1,0)^T\) and \(\xi_-=(0,1)^T\). The general formula gives

\[ \boxed{ u_h(p)= \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{E+m}\,\xi_h\\[1mm] h\dfrac{p}{\sqrt{E+m}}\,\xi_h \end{pmatrix}. } \]

2. Equation and norm. The two blocks of \((\not\! p-m)u=0\) are

\[ (E-m)\varphi-p\sigma^3\chi=0, \qquad p\sigma^3\varphi-(E+m)\chi=0. \]

The second is satisfied by \(\chi=hp\xi_h/\sqrt{E+m}\); the first follows from \(p^2=(E-m)(E+m)\). The norm is

\[ u_h^\dagger u_{h'} =\left(E+m+\frac{p^2}{E+m}\right)\delta_{hh'} =2E\delta_{hh'}. \]

3. Bilinears. With \(\overline u=u^\dagger\gamma^0\),

\[ \overline u_hu_h =(E+m)-\frac{p^2}{E+m}=2m. \]

The vector current is

\[ \boxed{\overline u_h\gamma^\mu u_h=2p^\mu=(2E,0,0,2p),} \]

independently of \(h\).

4. Chiral content. Since

\[ \frac{u_h^\dagger\gamma^5u_h}{u_h^\dagger u_h} =h\frac pE, \]

we find

\[ \boxed{ w_R(h)=\frac12\left(1+h\frac pE\right), \qquad w_L(h)=\frac12\left(1-h\frac pE\right). } \]

5. Limits. If \(p\gg m\), \(p/E\to1\). The state \(h=+\) becomes almost purely right-handed and \(h=-\) almost purely left-handed. If \(p\ll m\), \(p/E\to0\): each helicity state contains both chiralities with weight \(1/2\). This shows exactly why helicity and chirality are identical only in the massless limit.

6. Completeness. Since \(\sum_h\xi_h\xi_h^\dagger=\mathbf1_2\), the sum of outer products has the block form

\[ \sum_hu_h\overline u_h =\begin{pmatrix} (E+m)\mathbf1&-p\sigma^3\\ p\sigma^3&-(E-m)\mathbf1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

But for \(p^\mu=(E,0,0,p)\),

\[ \not\! p+m =E\gamma^0-p\gamma^3+m, \]

which has exactly these blocks. Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\sum_hu_h(p)\overline u_h(p)=\not\! p+m.} \]

12.0.3 Problem 3 — From Proca to the photon ★★★★

Consider

\[ \mathcal L_m=-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} +\frac12m^2A_\mu A^\mu-J_\mu A^\mu. \]

  1. Derive the equation of motion.
  2. Assuming \(\partial_\mu J^\mu=0\), determine \(\partial_\mu A^\mu\) for \(m\neq0\).
  3. Construct the three free polarizations for \(p=(E,0,0,p)\) and find their sum.
  4. Invert the kinetic operator and recover the Proca propagator.
  5. Study the amplitude between two conserved currents.
  6. Now set \(m=0\). Explain why the kinetic operator is no longer invertible.
  7. Add \(-\tfrac1{2\xi}(\partial\cdot A)^2\) and give the propagator.
  8. Show independence from \(\xi\) between conserved currents.
  9. Carefully compare the degrees of freedom for \(m>0\) and \(m=0\).
NoteSolution

1. Equation. Varying \(F_{\mu\nu}\) gives, after integration by parts,

\[ \boxed{\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}+m^2A^\nu=J^\nu.} \]

2. Massive constraint. Take the divergence. The term \(\partial_\nu\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}\) vanishes by antisymmetry, so

\[ m^2\partial_\nu A^\nu=\partial_\nu J^\nu. \]

For a conserved current and \(m\neq0\),

\[ \boxed{\partial\cdot A=0.} \]

3. Polarizations. For the free theory,

\[ \varepsilon_1=(0,1,0,0), \quad \varepsilon_2=(0,0,1,0), \quad \varepsilon_L=\frac1m(p,0,0,E). \]

They satisfy \(p\cdot\varepsilon_\lambda=0\) and \(\varepsilon_\lambda\cdot\varepsilon_{\lambda'}^*=-\delta_{\lambda\lambda'}\). By covariance or direct verification,

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{\lambda=1}^3\varepsilon_\lambda^\mu \varepsilon_\lambda^{*\nu} =-\eta^{\mu\nu}+\frac{p^\mu p^\nu}{m^2}. } \]

4. Massive propagator. The quadratic operator is

\[ K^{\mu\nu}=(-p^2+m^2)\eta^{\mu\nu}+p^\mu p^\nu. \]

The ansatz \(D=A\eta+Bpp\) gives \(A=-i/(p^2-m^2+i\epsilon)\) and \(B=-A/m^2\). Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ D_{\rm P}^{\mu\nu}(p) =\frac{-i}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon} \left(\eta^{\mu\nu}-\frac{p^\mu p^\nu}{m^2}\right). } \]

5. Conserved sources. The exchange amplitude is

\[ \mathcal A_m\propto \frac{-i}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon} \left[J\cdot J'-\frac{(p\cdot J)(p\cdot J')}{m^2}\right]. \]

Conservation removes the second term:

\[ \mathcal A_m\propto\frac{-iJ\cdot J'}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}. \]

6. Massless case. For \(m=0\),

\[ K^{\mu\nu}=-p^2\eta^{\mu\nu}+p^\mu p^\nu \]

and \(K^{\mu\nu}p_\nu=0\). The direction \(A^\mu\propto p^\mu\) is a gauge direction; \(K\) has no inverse on the full space.

7. Gauge fixing. The term \(-(\partial\cdot A)^2/(2\xi)\) removes this degeneracy. The propagator becomes

\[ \boxed{ D_\xi^{\mu\nu}(p) =\frac{-i}{p^2+i\epsilon} \left[\eta^{\mu\nu}-(1-\xi)\frac{p^\mu p^\nu}{p^2}\right]. } \]

8. Observable. Between conserved currents, the whole part proportional to \(p^\mu p^\nu\) vanishes, so

\[ \boxed{ \mathcal A_0\propto\frac{-iJ\cdot J'}{p^2+i\epsilon}, } \]

independently of \(\xi\).

9. Counting. The massive field has four components and one dynamical constraint \(\partial\cdot A=0\): \(4-1=3\) polarizations. In the massless case, the Lorenz condition removes one component and the residual gauge freedom removes another: \(4-1-1=2\) polarizations. We therefore cannot obtain the photon simply by replacing \(m\) by zero in the count: at \(m=0\), a new gauge redundancy appears.

12.0.4 Problem 4 — Causality, Green functions, and the \(i\epsilon\) prescription ★★★★

Consider a free real scalar field.

  1. Construct the Wightman functions

\[ \Delta^+(x-y)=\langle0|\phi(x)\phi(y)|0\rangle, \qquad \Delta^-(x-y)=\langle0|\phi(y)\phi(x)|0\rangle. \]

  1. Relate their difference to the Pauli–Jordan function.
  2. Show microcausality at spacelike separation.
  3. Express \(\Delta_F\) in terms of \(\Delta^\pm\).
  4. Recover its momentum-space representation and the positions of the poles.
  5. Show that it is a Green function.
  6. Compare the Feynman, retarded, and advanced propagators conceptually.
  7. Explain why a nonzero vacuum correlation at spacelike separation cannot transmit a signal.
NoteSolution

1. Wightman functions. Only the contraction \(aa^\dagger\) survives:

\[ \boxed{ \Delta^+(z)=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3,2E_{\mathbf p}}e^{-ipz}, \qquad \Delta^-(z)=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3,2E_{\mathbf p}}e^{ipz}. } \]

In general, each one is nonzero, including for \(z^2<0\).

2. Commutator. Their difference is

\[ \Delta^+(z)-\Delta^-(z) =\langle0|[\phi(x),\phi(y)]|0\rangle =i\Delta(z). \]

For a free field, the commutator is a multiple of the identity, so this expectation value determines the operator itself.

3. Microcausality. If \(z^2<0\), there is a frame in which \(z^0=0\). In that frame, the two integrals are exchanged by \(\mathbf p\to-\mathbf p\), so their difference vanishes. The invariance of \(d^3p/(2E_p)\) extends the result to every frame:

\[ \boxed{[\phi(x),\phi(y)]=0\quad(z^2<0).} \]

4. Time-ordered product. By definition,

\[ \boxed{ \Delta_F(z)=\theta(z^0)\Delta^+(z) +\theta(-z^0)\Delta^-(z). } \]

5. Pole prescription. The same expression can be written as

\[ \boxed{ \Delta_F(z)=\int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4} \frac{i e^{-ipz}}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}. } \]

The poles are shifted according to

\[ p^0=+E_{\mathbf p}-i\epsilon, \qquad p^0=-E_{\mathbf p}+i\epsilon. \]

The first contributes for \(z^0>0\), and the second for \(z^0<0\).

6. Green function. Applying Klein–Gordon gives

\[ \boxed{(\Box+m^2)\Delta_F(z)=-i\delta^{(4)}(z).} \]

The point singularity shows that \(\Delta_F\) is the distributional inverse of the kinetic operator with Feynman boundary conditions.

7. Boundary conditions. The three functions invert the same operator but differ in their prescriptions:

  • \(\Delta_R\) responds only after the source and has support in the future cone;
  • \(\Delta_A\) responds only before the source and has support in the past cone;
  • \(\Delta_F\) time-orders the operators and is suited to quantum amplitudes between asymptotic states.

The differential equation alone is therefore not enough to choose an inverse.

8. Correlation and signal. A local operation at \(x\) changes the expectation value of an observable at \(y\) through terms controlled by their commutator. Wightman functions can reveal a vacuum correlation, but

\[ [\mathcal O(x),\mathcal O(y)]=0 \]

for bosonic observables at spacelike separation. The choice or order of measurement at \(x\) therefore cannot change the local statistics at \(y\). Correlation does not mean controllable influence.

12.0.5 Problem 5 — Full quantization of the Dirac field ★★★★

Start from the symmetrized Lagrangian

\[ \mathcal L =\frac i2\left[ \overline\psi\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi -(\partial_\mu\overline\psi)\gamma^\mu\psi \right]-m\overline\psi\psi. \]

  1. Derive the Dirac equation and its adjoint.
  2. Construct the \(U(1)\) current and check its conservation.
  3. Write the mode expansions of \(\psi\) and \(\overline\psi\).
  4. Impose the anticommutators of the mode operators and recover the equal-time anticommutator of the field.
  5. Derive \(:H:\) and \(:Q:\).
  6. Show that the anticommutator at spacelike separation can be written using the Pauli–Jordan function.
  7. Construct the Feynman propagator and check its Green equation.
  8. In the same formalism, explain the existence of antiparticles, Pauli exclusion, and positive energy.

1. Equations. Independent variation of \(\overline\psi\) gives

\[ \boxed{(i\not\!\partial-m)\psi=0,} \]

and varying \(\psi\), after integration by parts, gives

\[ \boxed{i(\partial_\mu\overline\psi)\gamma^\mu+m\overline\psi=0.} \]

The symmetrized Lagrangian differs from \(\overline\psi(i\not\!\partial-m)\psi\) by a boundary term and leads to the same dynamics.

2. Current. Under \(\psi\to e^{-iq\alpha}\psi\),

\[ \boxed{j^\mu=q\overline\psi\gamma^\mu\psi.} \]

Its divergence vanishes because the contributions \(+im\overline\psi\psi\) and \(-im\overline\psi\psi\) from the two equations cancel.

3. Modes. With the normalizations \(u^\dagger u=v^\dagger v=2E\),

\[ \psi(x)=\sum_s\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}} \left[b_s(\mathbf p)u_s(p)e^{-ipx} +d_s^\dagger(\mathbf p)v_s(p)e^{ipx}\right], \]

\[ \{b_s(\mathbf p),b_r^\dagger(\mathbf q)\} =\{d_s(\mathbf p),d_r^\dagger(\mathbf q)\} =(2\pi)^3\delta_{sr}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q), \]

with all other anticommutators equal to zero. Using

\[ \sum_su_s\overline u_s=\not\! p+m, \qquad \sum_sv_s\overline v_s=\not\! p-m, \]

we obtain at equal times

\[ \boxed{ \{\psi_\alpha(t,\mathbf x),\psi_\beta^\dagger(t,\mathbf y)\} =\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x-\mathbf y). } \]

The terms in \(\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p\) cancel after \(\mathbf p\to-\mathbf p\), while the energy terms reconstruct the delta function.

5. Energy and charge. After fermionic reordering,

\[ \boxed{ :H:=\sum_s\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3}E_{\mathbf p} (b_s^\dagger b_s+d_s^\dagger d_s), } \]

\[ \boxed{ :Q:=q\sum_s\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3} (b_s^\dagger b_s-d_s^\dagger d_s). } \]

6. Locality. A similar calculation, without setting equal times, gives

\[ \boxed{ \{\psi_\alpha(x),\overline\psi_\beta(y)\} =\left(i\not\!\partial_x+m\right)_{\alpha\beta}i\Delta(x-y). } \]

The function \(\Delta\) and its derivatives vanish throughout any spacelike region; the fermionic anticommutator is therefore zero there.

7. Propagator. Fermionic time ordering includes a sign when the fields are exchanged. The spin sums give

\[ \boxed{ S_F(x-y)=(i\not\!\partial_x+m)\Delta_F(x-y), } \]

that is,

\[ \boxed{ S_F(p)=\frac{i(\not\! p+m)}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}. } \]

We check that

\[ (i\not\!\partial_x-m)S_F(x-y) =i\delta^{(4)}(x-y). \]

8. Physical interpretation. The mode \(v e^{ipx}\) appears with \(d^\dagger\): it creates an antiparticle rather than a negative-energy state. Anticommutation implies \((b_s^\dagger)^2=(d_s^\dagger)^2=0\), and therefore Pauli exclusion. Finally, reordering \(-Edd^\dagger=-E+Ed^\dagger d\) makes antiparticle energy positive. The three facts are different aspects of the same quantized structure.

12.0.6 Problem 6 — Representations, constraints, and the spin table ★★★★

For each of the following free fields, state the usual Lorentz representation, the main equation, the constraints or redundancies, the number of physical states, and the statistics:

  1. massive real scalar;
  2. massive complex scalar;
  3. massless Weyl spinor;
  4. massive Dirac field;
  5. massive Majorana field;
  6. massive Proca field;
  7. Maxwell field;
  8. massive Rarita–Schwinger field;
  9. massive Fierz–Pauli field.

Justify the counts, then explain why the three numbers “field components,” “Cauchy data,” and “particle polarizations” must never be confused.

NoteSolution

The real scalar transforms as \((0,0)\) and has one bosonic polarization. Making it complex doubles the charge sector rather than the spin sector: there is one scalar particle and one scalar antiparticle.

A Weyl field transforms as \((1/2,0)\) or \((0,1/2)\) and contains one helicity for each chosen chiral field. A massive Dirac field combines both representations. Its \(u_s\) solutions give two particle spin states and its \(v_s\) solutions give two antiparticle states. The Majorana condition \(\psi=\psi^C\) identifies the particle and antiparticle families, leaving two physical spin states. These spinor fields are fermionic.

A Proca field transforms as \((1/2,1/2)\). Its four components obey \(\partial\cdot A=0\), leaving three massive bosonic polarizations. The Maxwell field uses the same local vector representation, but the constraint and gauge freedom leave only two helicities.

The Rarita–Schwinger vector-spinor is reduced by transversality and its gamma trace to \(2s+1=4\) fermionic states per particle sector. A symmetric Fierz–Pauli tensor has ten components; four transverse conditions and one trace condition leave five bosonic spin-\(2\) polarizations.

Why three counts? The number of components describes the chosen local representation before the equations are imposed. Cauchy data also depend on the time order of the equation: Klein–Gordon requires the field and its velocity, while Dirac requires only the field. Finally, polarizations are counted after restricting to an on-shell momentum, applying constraints, taking the gauge quotient, and possibly separating particles from antiparticles.

The safe rule is therefore

\[ \boxed{ \text{covariant components} \xrightarrow{\text{equations, constraints, gauge}} \text{physical degrees of freedom}. } \]

In a local relativistic theory with positive energy, statistics follows spin: integer spins use commutators, while half-integer spins use anticommutators.

NoteSolution

Part A — Classical construction

  1. A real, local, quadratic, Lorentz-invariant density with at most two derivatives must contract the indices. Up to a boundary term, the two structures are \((\partial\phi)^2\) and \(\phi^2\). Canonical normalization of the kinetic term gives

\[ \boxed{\mathcal L=\frac12(\partial\phi)^2-\frac12m^2\phi^2.} \]

The relative sign is chosen to obtain \(E_{\mathbf p}^2=\mathbf p^2+m^2\) and energy bounded from below.

  1. The variation gives

\[ \boxed{(\Box+m^2)\phi=0.} \]

For \(\phi=Ae^{-ipx}\), \(\Box\phi=-p^2\phi\), so

\[ \boxed{p^2=m^2, \qquad p^0=\pm E_{\mathbf p}, \qquad E_{\mathbf p}=\sqrt{\mathbf p^2+m^2}.} \]

  1. The conjugate momentum and Hamiltonian density are

\[ \pi=\dot\phi, \]

\[ \boxed{ \mathcal H=\frac12\pi^2+ rac12(\boldsymbol\nabla\phi)^2 +\frac12m^2\phi^2. } \]

The Noether tensor associated with translations is

\[ \boxed{ T^{\mu\nu}=\partial^\mu\phi\partial^\nu\phi -\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathcal L. } \]

For solutions, \(\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=0\).

  1. Since \(T^{00}=\mathcal H\) is a sum of squares with positive coefficients for \(m^2\geq0\),

\[ H=\int d^3x\,T^{00}\geq0. \]

Part B — Modes and quantization

  1. The reality condition \(\phi^*=\phi\) requires

\[ \phi(x)=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}} \left[c(\mathbf p)e^{-ipx}+c^*(\mathbf p)e^{ipx}\right]. \]

The two branches are therefore not two independent species. After quantization,

\[ c\to a, \qquad c^*\to a^\dagger. \]

  1. Impose

\[ [\phi(t,\mathbf x),\pi(t,\mathbf y)] =i\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x-\mathbf y). \]

The Fourier calculation fixes

\[ \boxed{ \phi(x)=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}} (ae^{-ipx}+a^\dagger e^{ipx}), } \]

\[ \boxed{ [a(\mathbf p),a^\dagger(\mathbf q)] =(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q). } \]

  1. The vacuum satisfies \(a(\mathbf p)|0\rangle=0\). With covariant normalization,

\[ |\mathbf p\rangle=\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}a^\dagger(\mathbf p)|0\rangle, \]

\[ \langle\mathbf q|\mathbf p\rangle =2E_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q). \]

An \(n\)-quantum state is symmetric under exchange because the creation operators commute. The normal-ordered operators are

\[ :H:=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3}E_{\mathbf p}a^\dagger a, \]

\[ :\mathbf P:=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3}\mathbf p,a^\dagger a. \]

Each application of \(a^\dagger(\mathbf p)\) therefore adds \((E_{\mathbf p},\mathbf p)\).

  1. In \(H\), the \(aa\) and \(a^\dagger a^\dagger\) terms cancel because \(E_p^2=\mathbf p^2+m^2\). This leaves

\[ H=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3}E_{\mathbf p} \left[a^\dagger a+\frac12(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf0)\right]. \]

The last term is the sum of the energies \(E_p/2\) of all the oscillators. Normal ordering subtracts it in a theory without gravity, where only energy differences are studied here.

Part C — Locality and propagation

  1. The mode algebra gives

\[ [\phi(x),\phi(y)] =\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3,2E_p} (e^{-ip(x-y)}-e^{ip(x-y)}) \equiv i\Delta(x-y). \]

If \((x-y)^2<0\), a boost places the two events at the same time. The two terms then cancel under \(\mathbf p\to-\mathbf p\). Since the measure is invariant,

\[ \boxed{\Delta(x-y)=0\quad\text{outside the light cone}.} \]

  1. The time-ordered product gives

\[ \Delta_F(z)=\theta(z^0)\Delta^+(z)+\theta(-z^0)\Delta^-(z). \]

The poles \(+E_p-i\epsilon\) and \(-E_p+i\epsilon\) reproduce the two functions depending on the half-plane in which the contour is closed. Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \Delta_F(z)=\int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4} \frac{i e^{-ipz}}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}, } \]

et

\[ \boxed{(\Box+m^2)\Delta_F(z)=-i\delta^{(4)}(z).} \]

  1. The pole \(p^2=m^2\) reveals the mass of an excitation that can become real. A detectable asymptotic state is on this shell and has positive energy. An internal line is integrated over all \(p\) and is generally off shell: the word virtual describes this role in the amplitude, not an observable hidden path.

Part D — Charge and antiparticle

  1. The complex field has the global \(U(1)\) symmetry,

\[ \phi\to e^{-iq\alpha}\phi, \]

with

\[ \boxed{j^\mu=iq(\phi^*\partial^\mu\phi-\phi\partial^\mu\phi^*).} \]

  1. The two frequencies become independent:

\[ \boxed{ \phi(x)=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3\sqrt{2E_p}} (ae^{-ipx}+b^\dagger e^{ipx}). } \]

There are two identical, mutually commuting bosonic algebras for \(a\) and \(b\).

  1. We find

\[ \boxed{:H:=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3}E_p(a^\dagger a+b^\dagger b),} \]

\[ \boxed{:Q:=q\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3}(a^\dagger a-b^\dagger b).} \]

\(a^\dagger\) creates a particle with charge \(q\), and \(b^\dagger\) creates an antiparticle with charge \(-q\), both with positive energy.

  1. A real field has one family of neutral quanta: the particle is its own antiparticle. A complex field is classically equivalent to two real fields and has two families of quanta with opposite charges. In both cases, each particle has one spin-\(0\) polarization.

13.0.2 Comprehensive problem 2 — The Dirac field, from covariance to antiparticles ★★★★

NoteSolution

Part A — Building the equation

  1. In momentum space,

\[ H=\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p+\beta m. \]

To require \(H^2=(\mathbf p^2+m^2)\mathbf1\), the cross terms must vanish and the coefficients of the squares must be the identity:

\[ \boxed{ \{\alpha^i,\alpha^j\}=2\delta^{ij}, \qquad \{\alpha^i,\beta\}=0, \qquad \beta^2=1. } \]

These relations cannot be satisfied by numbers; a matrix structure and several components are needed.

  1. With \(\gamma^0=\beta\) and \(\gamma^i=\beta\alpha^i\), we check

\[ (\gamma^0)^2=1, \qquad (\gamma^i)^2=-1, \qquad \{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu\}=2\eta^{\mu\nu}. \]

  1. The equation becomes

\[ \boxed{(i\not\!\partial-m)\psi=0.} \]

Multiplication by \(i\not\!\partial+m\) gives \((\Box+m^2)\psi=0\). Klein–Gordon therefore fixes the dispersion of each component, while the first-order equation imposes the relations that organize the spin.

  1. The action

\[ S=\int d^4x\,\overline\psi(i\not\!\partial-m)\psi \]

gives the Dirac equation by varying \(\overline\psi\) and the adjoint equation by varying \(\psi\). The global phase gives

\[ \boxed{j^\mu=q\overline\psi\gamma^\mu\psi, \qquad\partial_\mu j^\mu=0.} \]

Part B — Solutions and spin

  1. At rest,

\[ u_s(0)=\sqrt{2m}\begin{pmatrix}\xi_s\\0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad v_s(0)=\sqrt{2m}\begin{pmatrix}0\\\eta_s\end{pmatrix}. \]

In motion, one convention is

\[ \boxed{ u_s(p)= \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{E+m}\,\xi_s\\ \dfrac{\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p}{\sqrt{E+m}}\xi_s \end{pmatrix}, \quad v_s(p)= \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p}{\sqrt{E+m}}\eta_s\\ \sqrt{E+m}\,\eta_s \end{pmatrix}. } \]

  1. They satisfy

\[ u_s^\dagger u_r=v_s^\dagger v_r=2E\delta_{sr}, \]

\[ \overline u_su_r=2m\delta_{sr}, \qquad \overline v_sv_r=-2m\delta_{sr}, \]

and covariance fixes

\[ \boxed{ \sum_su_s\overline u_s=\not\! p+m, \qquad \sum_sv_s\overline v_s=\not\! p-m. } \]

  1. Helicity is the projection of spin along \(\widehat{\mathbf p}\). Chirality is the eigenvalue of \(\gamma^5\). The projectors

\[ P_{L,R}=\frac{1\mp\gamma^5}{2} \]

split \(\psi=\psi_L+\psi_R\). The kinetic term separates, but

\[ -m\overline\psi\psi =-m(\overline\psi_L\psi_R+\overline\psi_R\psi_L), \]

so mass couples the chiralities.

  1. For a helicity state \(h=\pm1\),

\[ \frac{u_h^\dagger\gamma^5u_h}{u_h^\dagger u_h} =h\frac{|\mathbf p|}{E}. \]

Therefore,

\[ w_{R,L}=\frac12\left(1\pm h\frac{|\mathbf p|}{E}\right). \]

When \(m/E\to0\), \(|\mathbf p|/E\to1\): for particles, \(h=+\) becomes right-handed and \(h=-\) left-handed. At nonzero mass, each helicity contains both chiralities.

Part C — Quantization

  1. Write

\[ \boxed{ \psi(x)=\sum_s\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3\sqrt{2E_p}} \left[b_su_se^{-ipx}+d_s^\dagger v_se^{ipx}\right]. } \]

The fundamental relations are

\[ \{b_s(\mathbf p),b_r^\dagger(\mathbf q)\} =\{d_s(\mathbf p),d_r^\dagger(\mathbf q)\} =(2\pi)^3\delta_{sr}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q). \]

  1. Using the completeness relations and changing \(\mathbf p\to-\mathbf p\) in the \(v\) term, the appropriate spatial and mass parts combine to give

\[ \boxed{ \{\psi_\alpha(t,\mathbf x),\psi_\beta^\dagger(t,\mathbf y)\} =\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x-\mathbf y). } \]

  1. After normal ordering,

\[ \boxed{:H:=\sum_s\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3}E_p (b_s^\dagger b_s+d_s^\dagger d_s),} \]

\[ \boxed{:Q:=q\sum_s\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3} (b_s^\dagger b_s-d_s^\dagger d_s).} \]

\(b_s^\dagger\) creates the particle and \(d_s^\dagger\) the antiparticle, both with positive energy and opposite charges.

  1. With commutators, the negative-branch term would give \(-Ed^\dagger d\) after reordering. Since bosonic occupation would be unlimited, the energy would decrease to \(-\infty\). In contrast, anticommutators turn \(-Edd^\dagger\) into \(Ed^\dagger d\) plus a constant.

  2. Fock space is generated by products of \(b_s^\dagger\) and \(d_s^\dagger\). But

\[ (b_s^\dagger(\mathbf p))^2 =(d_s^\dagger(\mathbf p))^2=0. \]

The same mode cannot be occupied twice: this is Pauli exclusion. Exchanging two creation operators produces a minus sign, so the states are antisymmetric.

Part D — Locality, propagation, and symmetries

  1. The spin sums give

\[ \boxed{ \{\psi_\alpha(x),\overline\psi_\beta(y)\} =\left(i\not\!\partial_x+m\right)_{\alpha\beta}i\Delta(x-y). } \]

Since \(\Delta\) and its derivatives vanish for \((x-y)^2<0\), the anticommutator is zero there. Observable bilinears, which contain an even number of fermions, then commute at spacelike separation.

  1. The fermionic sign in time ordering and the completeness relations lead to

\[ \boxed{ S_F(x-y)=(i\not\!\partial_x+m)\Delta_F(x-y), \qquad S_F(p)=\frac{i(\not\! p+m)}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}. } \]

It satisfies

\[ (i\not\!\partial_x-m)S_F(x-y)=i\delta^{(4)}(x-y). \]

  1. For \(j_A^\mu=\overline\psi\gamma^\mu\gamma^5\psi\),

\[ \boxed{\partial_\mu j_A^\mu=2im\overline\psi\gamma^5\psi.} \]

The axial current is therefore classically conserved in the massless limit, where left and right decouple.

  1. Parity reverses \(\mathbf x\) and exchanges the left- and right-handed representations; one possible convention is \(\psi\to\gamma^0\psi(t,-\mathbf x)\). Charge conjugation exchanges \(b\) and \(d\) and reverses the vector current. Time reversal is antiunitary and reverses momenta and angular momenta. Intrinsic phases are conventional, but their actions on observables are not.

If \(\psi=\psi^C\), then

\[ \overline\psi\gamma^\mu\psi =\overline{\psi^C}\gamma^\mu\psi^C =-\overline\psi\gamma^\mu\psi, \]

so \(j_V^\mu=0\). The Majorana field is its own antiparticle and cannot carry a nonzero vector \(U(1)\) charge.

\[ (\Box+m_0^2)\phi=0, \]

\[ (i\not\!\partial-m_{1/2})\psi=0, \]

\[ \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}+m_1^2A^\nu=0. \]

The three fields therefore obey the same type of dispersion relation, but their indices transform in different Lorentz representations and their constraints select spin \(0\), \(1/2\), and \(1\), respectively. The mass shell alone does not determine spin.

  1. In momentum space, the inverses are

The scalar has no matrix structure to invert. For Dirac, \((\not\! p-m)(\not\! p+m)=p^2-m^2\). For Proca, the inversion is performed in the transverse and longitudinal subspaces.

et

The numerators therefore carry the spin content, while the denominators locate the mass shell.

Part C — Statistics, Fock space, and causality

  1. The mode structures are

\[ \phi\sim ae^{-ipx}+a^\dagger e^{ipx}, \]

\[ \psi\sim b_su_se^{-ipx}+d_s^\dagger v_se^{ipx}, \]

\[ A^\mu\sim\sum_\lambda \left[a_\lambda\varepsilon_\lambda^\mu e^{-ipx} +a_\lambda^\dagger\varepsilon_\lambda^{*\mu}e^{ipx}\right]. \]

Integer-spin fields use commutators; the half-integer-spin field uses anticommutators. For the covariant photon, the intermediate space contains unphysical polarizations that must be removed by the appropriate physical-state condition.

A real or Majorana field has no \(U(1)\) charge equal to particle number minus antiparticle number. For a complex or charged Dirac field,

\[ :Q:=q(N_{\rm particles}-N_{\rm antiparticles}). \]

  1. For bosons, the occupation of a mode is unrestricted; for fermions it is \(0\) or \(1\). Anticommutation turns the negative-frequency Dirac sector into positive-energy antiparticles. In a local relativistic theory with positive norm, the wrong choice of statistics destroys positive energy or locality: this is the physical content of the spin–statistics connection.

  2. For the scalar field,

\[ [\phi(x),\phi(y)]=i\Delta(x-y), \]

and \(\Delta(z)=0\) if \(z^2<0\), because an equal-time frame exists. For Dirac,

\[ \{\psi_\alpha(x),\overline\psi_\beta(y)\} =\left(i\not\!\partial_x+m\right)_{\alpha\beta}i\Delta(x-y), \]

which vanishes in the same region. Fermionic bilinear observables are bosonic and therefore commute at spacelike separation.

Part D — Symmetries and limits

  1. A real scalar has one family of neutral quanta and is its own antiparticle. A complex scalar has two families with opposite charges. Similarly, a Dirac field has independent particles and antiparticles, while the Majorana condition \(\psi=\psi^C\) identifies them and forces the vector current to vanish.

  2. Helicity is the projection of spin along momentum and depends on motion; chirality is the eigenvalue of \(\gamma^5\) and characterizes a Lorentz representation. For a massive particle,

\[ \langle\gamma^5\rangle_h=h\frac{|\mathbf p|}{E}, \]

so a helicity state does not have pure chirality. When \(m/E\to0\), the two notions agree for particles, with the corresponding care needed for antiparticles.

  1. \(C\) exchanges particles and antiparticles and reverses the vector current. \(P\) reverses momentum and exchanges left and right; helicity changes sign. \(T\) is antiunitary and reverses both momentum and spin, so helicity remains unchanged. For electromagnetism,

\[ P:(\mathbf E,\mathbf B)\to(-\mathbf E,+\mathbf B), \]

\[ T:(\mathbf E,\mathbf B)\to(+\mathbf E,-\mathbf B). \]

The intrinsic phases chosen in field transformations are not observable.

Part E — Beyond spin \(1\)

  1. The vector-spinor has \(4\times4=16\) complex components and contains several sectors under rotations. The conditions

\[ (i\not\!\partial-m)\psi_\mu=0, \qquad \gamma^\mu\psi_\mu=0, \qquad \partial^\mu\psi_\mu=0 \]

remove the unwanted spin-\(1/2\) components and isolate the four polarizations \(2s+1=4\) of massive spin \(3/2\) in each particle sector.

  1. A symmetric tensor has ten components. For Fierz–Pauli,

\[ (\Box+m^2)h_{\mu\nu}=0, \qquad \partial^\mu h_{\mu\nu}=0, \qquad h^\mu{}_{\mu}=0. \]

The count \(10-4-1=5\) gives the five spin-\(2\) polarizations. If not removed, the trace would add a potentially pathological scalar sector.

  1. The real scalar has one Bose polarization, while a complex scalar has one for each charge. Dirac and Majorana fields have two Fermi spin states per particle, with the Majorana particle identical to its antiparticle. Proca theory has three Bose polarizations and Maxwell theory has two. The Rarita–Schwinger and Fierz–Pauli constraints leave respectively four Fermi states and five Bose states.

  2. The general principle is

\[ \boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{choose a local Lorentz representation}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{build a covariant action}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{solve the equations and constraints}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{take the quotient by gauge redundancies}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{quantize the modes with consistent statistics}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{obtain the physical Poincaré states} \end{array} } \]

Covariance determines which objects can be written; equations and constraints remove the extra components; quantization turns mode amplitudes into creation and annihilation operators; Fock space finally realizes particles with definite mass, spin, or helicity. This full chain, rather than one isolated equation, forms a consistent theory of a free field.
