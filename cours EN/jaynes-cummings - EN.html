The Jaynes–Cummings model

A two-level atom coupled to a quantized field mode

1 The atom and the field

An excited atom placed in a cavity can emit a photon into a cavity mode and later absorb it again. The Jaynes–Cummings model describes this exchange when two atomic levels and one field mode are enough to describe the system. Both the atom and the field are treated quantum mechanically.

We take a constant coupling and neglect photon loss and emission into other modes. The evolution is therefore closed and coherent.

1.1 The uncoupled system

Let \(|g\rangle\) and \(|e\rangle\) be the atomic ground and excited states, separated by \(\hbar\omega_a\). Choosing the energy origin halfway between them gives

\[ H_a=\frac{\hbar\omega_a}{2}\sigma_z, \qquad \sigma_z=|e\rangle\langle e|-|g\rangle\langle g|. \]

The transition operators raise or lower the atom:

\[ \sigma_+=|e\rangle\langle g|, \qquad \sigma_-=|g\rangle\langle e|. \]

Thus \(\sigma_+|g\rangle=|e\rangle\) and \(\sigma_-|e\rangle=|g\rangle\), while \(\sigma_+|e\rangle=\sigma_-|g\rangle=0\).

A field mode of angular frequency \(\omega_c\) is a quantum harmonic oscillator. Dropping its constant zero-point energy,

\[ H_c=\hbar\omega_c a^\dagger a, \qquad [a,a^\dagger]=1. \]

The Fock state \(|n\rangle\) contains exactly \(n\) photons, and

\[ a|n\rangle=\sqrt n\,|n-1\rangle, \qquad a^\dagger|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}\,|n+1\rangle. \]

Here \(a|0\rangle=0\). We write product states as \(|e,n\rangle=|e\rangle\otimes|n\rangle\). Atomic and field operators act on different factors, so they commute. Identity operators on the other factor will be left implicit. The free Hamiltonian is \(H_0=H_a+H_c\).

1.2 The interaction

In the electric dipole approximation, \(H_I=-\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf E\). For a dipole-allowed transition with no diagonal dipole moments, the two-level dipole operator contains \(\sigma_+\) and \(\sigma_-\). The mode’s electric field contains \(a\) and \(a^\dagger\). With a suitable phase choice, this gives

\[ H_I=\hbar g(a+a^\dagger)(\sigma_++\sigma_-), \]

where \(g>0\) has units of angular frequency. It depends on the transition dipole, the field strength per photon and the atom’s position in the mode.

The terms \(a\sigma_+\) and \(a^\dagger\sigma_-\) exchange one excitation: the atom absorbs or emits one photon. The other terms, \(a\sigma_-\) and \(a^\dagger\sigma_+\), remove or create two excitations at once.

In the interaction picture defined by \(H_0\),

\[ a(t)=ae^{-i\omega_ct}, \qquad \sigma_+(t)=\sigma_+e^{i\omega_at}. \]

The exchange terms therefore oscillate at \(\Delta=\omega_a-\omega_c\), while the other two oscillate at \(\pm(\omega_a+\omega_c)\). Near resonance, the latter vary much faster. If

\[ |\Delta|\ll\omega_a+\omega_c, \qquad g\sqrt{n+1}\ll\omega_a+\omega_c \]

for the photon numbers that are populated, their effect averages out on the exchange timescale. Dropping them is the rotating-wave approximation. It gives

\[ \boxed{ H_{\mathrm{JC}}=\hbar\omega_c a^\dagger a +\frac{\hbar\omega_a}{2}\sigma_z +\hbar g(a\sigma_++a^\dagger\sigma_-). } \tag{1}\]

2 Doublets and dressed states

2.1 Conserved excitation number

The operator

\[ N=a^\dagger a+\sigma_+\sigma_- =a^\dagger a+|e\rangle\langle e| \]

counts photons plus the atomic excitation. Absorption decreases the photon number by one and raises the atomic contribution by one; emission does the reverse. This gives \([H_{\mathrm{JC}},N]=0\), proved explicitly in Exercise 2.

The states \(|e,n\rangle\) and \(|g,n+1\rangle\) both have \(N=n+1\). The Hamiltonian only mixes these two states, so we solve it in each subspace

\[ \mathcal H_n=\operatorname{span}\{|e,n\rangle,|g,n+1\rangle\}, \qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots. \]

The state \(|g,0\rangle\) is uncoupled and has energy \(-\hbar\omega_a/2\).

2.2 A two-by-two Hamiltonian

Set \(G_n=g\sqrt{n+1}\). Applying the Hamiltonian gives

\[ \begin{aligned} H_{\mathrm{JC}}|e,n\rangle &=\hbar\left(n\omega_c+\frac{\omega_a}{2}\right)|e,n\rangle +\hbar G_n|g,n+1\rangle,\\ H_{\mathrm{JC}}|g,n+1\rangle &=\hbar G_n|e,n\rangle +\hbar\left((n+1)\omega_c-\frac{\omega_a}{2}\right)|g,n+1\rangle. \end{aligned} \]

In the ordered basis \((|e,n\rangle,|g,n+1\rangle)\), subtracting the mean of the diagonal entries gives

\[ H_n=\hbar\omega_c\left(n+\frac12\right)I +\hbar K_n, \qquad K_n=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta/2&G_n\\G_n&-\Delta/2\end{pmatrix}. \tag{2}\]

If \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(K_n\), its characteristic equation is

\[ \det(K_n-\lambda I)=\lambda^2-\frac{\Delta^2}{4}-G_n^2=0. \]

Define the generalized Rabi angular frequency

\[ \Omega_n=\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2(n+1)}. \tag{3}\]

The energies are then

\[ E_{n,\pm}=\hbar\omega_c\left(n+\frac12\right) \pm\frac{\hbar\Omega_n}{2}. \tag{4}\]

The eigenstates are called dressed states because they combine atomic and field excitations. To specify the mixing angle for either sign of \(\Delta\), choose \(0<\theta_n<\pi/2\) with

\[ \cos(2\theta_n)=\frac{\Delta}{\Omega_n}, \qquad \sin(2\theta_n)=\frac{2G_n}{\Omega_n}. \]

The corresponding normalized states are

\[ \begin{aligned} |n,+\rangle&=\cos\theta_n|e,n\rangle+\sin\theta_n|g,n+1\rangle,\\ |n,-\rangle&=-\sin\theta_n|e,n\rangle+\cos\theta_n|g,n+1\rangle. \end{aligned} \]

At resonance, \(\Delta=0\) and \(\theta_n=\pi/4\). The two states are equal-weight combinations, and their energy separation is \(2\hbar g\sqrt{n+1}\). For \(n=0\), the splitting \(2\hbar g\) is the vacuum Rabi splitting.

3 Rabi oscillations

Suppose the initial state is \(|e,n\rangle\). The evolution stays within \(\mathcal H_n\). Since

\[ K_n^2=\frac{\Omega_n^2}{4}I, \]

the even and odd powers in the exponential give

\[ e^{-iK_nt}=I\cos\frac{\Omega_nt}{2} -\frac{2iK_n}{\Omega_n}\sin\frac{\Omega_nt}{2}. \]

Applying this matrix to \((1,0)^T\) and restoring the common phase gives

\[ \begin{aligned} |\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-i\omega_c(n+1/2)t}\Bigg[& \left(\cos\frac{\Omega_nt}{2} -i\frac{\Delta}{\Omega_n}\sin\frac{\Omega_nt}{2}\right)|e,n\rangle\\ &-i\frac{2G_n}{\Omega_n}\sin\frac{\Omega_nt}{2}|g,n+1\rangle\Bigg]. \end{aligned} \tag{5}\]

Taking the squared modulus of the first amplitude yields

\[ P_e^{(n)}(t)=1-\frac{4g^2(n+1)}{\Omega_n^2} \sin^2\left(\frac{\Omega_nt}{2}\right). \tag{6}\]

At resonance this becomes \(P_e^{(n)}(t)=\cos^2(g\sqrt{n+1}\,t)\). The excitation moves back and forth between \(|e,n\rangle\) and \(|g,n+1\rangle\). For \(n=0\), the initially excited atom exchanges its excitation with the empty mode. This is a vacuum Rabi oscillation; the energy comes from the excited atom, not from the vacuum.

Away from resonance, the maximum transfer probability is

\[ P_{e,n\to g,n+1}^{\max} =\frac{4g^2(n+1)}{\Delta^2+4g^2(n+1)}<1. \]

Detuning therefore reduces the transfer, even though the oscillation frequency \(\Omega_n\) increases.

4 A coherent field: collapse and revival

A Fock state has a fixed photon number. A coherent state is a superposition,

\[ |\alpha\rangle=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}}|n\rangle, \]

with Poisson probabilities

\[ p_n=e^{-\bar n}\frac{\bar n^n}{n!}, \qquad \bar n=|\alpha|^2. \]

Start from \(|e\rangle\otimes|\alpha\rangle\) at resonance. Each component \(|e,n\rangle\) evolves with its own Rabi frequency. To find the probability of an excited atom, we sum over all final photon numbers. Their orthogonality removes cross terms, leaving

\[ P_e(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_n\cos^2(g\sqrt{n+1}\,t). \tag{7}\]

Initially the oscillations add together. As their phases separate, their sum approaches \(1/2\) and the visible oscillations collapse. At later times, neighboring components partly return to phase and the oscillations revive. The total evolution remains unitary: the collapse is not energy loss.

For \(\bar n\gg1\), the first revival occurs at approximately

\[ t_{\mathrm{rev}}\simeq\frac{2\pi\sqrt{\bar n}}{g}. \]

Exercise 5 derives this estimate. The collapse time is of order \(1/g\), so collapse and revival occur on different timescales when \(\bar n\) is large.

Show Python code 
nbar = 16.0
nmax = int(nbar + 10 * np.sqrt(nbar) + 40)
n_values = np.arange(nmax + 1)

# Poisson probabilities from their recurrence relation.
poisson = np.empty(nmax + 1)
poisson[0] = np.exp(-nbar)
for n in range(nmax):
    poisson[n + 1] = poisson[n] * nbar / (n + 1)
poisson /= poisson.sum()

t_rev = 2 * np.pi * np.sqrt(nbar)  # scaled time g t
tau = np.linspace(0, 2.15 * t_rev, 4500)
phases = np.sqrt(n_values[:, None] + 1) * tau[None, :]
p_excited = np.sum(poisson[:, None] * np.cos(phases) ** 2, axis=0)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 4.8))
ax.plot(tau, p_excited, color=BLEU, lw=1.5)

5 Exercises

5.1 Exercise 1 — Interaction processes

Consider \(V=\hbar g(a+a^\dagger)(\sigma_++\sigma_-)\).

  1. Expand \(V\) into four terms.
  2. Give the nonzero action of each term on \(|g,n\rangle\) or \(|e,n\rangle\).
  3. Identify which terms conserve \(N\).
  4. Explain why the other two can be neglected in the rotating-wave approximation.
NoteSolution

Expanding the product gives

\[ V=\hbar g(a\sigma_++a^\dagger\sigma_-+a\sigma_-+a^\dagger\sigma_+). \]

Apply the field and atomic operators separately:

\[ \begin{aligned} a\sigma_+|g,n\rangle&=\sqrt n\,|e,n-1\rangle,\\ a^\dagger\sigma_-|e,n\rangle&=\sqrt{n+1}\,|g,n+1\rangle,\\ a\sigma_-|e,n\rangle&=\sqrt n\,|g,n-1\rangle,\\ a^\dagger\sigma_+|g,n\rangle&=\sqrt{n+1}\,|e,n+1\rangle. \end{aligned} \]

The terms containing \(a\) give zero when \(n=0\). Each term also gives zero on the other atomic state because \(\sigma_+|e\rangle=\sigma_-|g\rangle=0\).

For \(a\sigma_+\), the field loses one excitation and the atom gains one. For \(a^\dagger\sigma_-\), the changes are reversed. Both conserve \(N\). The other two change \(N\) by \(-2\) and \(+2\).

In the interaction picture, the exchange terms have factors \(e^{\pm i\Delta t}\). The counter-rotating terms have factors \(e^{\pm i(\omega_a+\omega_c)t}\). Near resonance and at weak coupling, these fast terms average out on the slower exchange timescale, leaving the Jaynes–Cummings interaction.

5.2 Exercise 2 — Conservation of excitations

Let \(N=a^\dagger a+\sigma_+\sigma_-\).

  1. Show explicitly that \([N,a\sigma_+]=0\).
  2. Show that \([N,a^\dagger\sigma_-]=0\).
  3. Deduce \([H_{\mathrm{JC}},N]=0\).
  4. Find the product basis states with exactly two excitations.
NoteSolution

Since atomic and field operators commute,

\[ [N,a\sigma_+]=[a^\dagger a,a]\sigma_+ +a[\sigma_+\sigma_-,\sigma_+]. \]

The field commutator is \([a^\dagger a,a]=-a\). For the atom, \(\sigma_+\sigma_-=|e\rangle\langle e|\), so

\[ [|e\rangle\langle e|,\sigma_+]=\sigma_+. \]

Thus \([N,a\sigma_+]=-a\sigma_++a\sigma_+=0\). Similarly,

\[ [a^\dagger a,a^\dagger]=a^\dagger, \qquad [|e\rangle\langle e|,\sigma_-]=-\sigma_-, \]

which gives \([N,a^\dagger\sigma_-]=a^\dagger\sigma_--a^\dagger\sigma_-=0\).

The free terms also commute with \(N\), since \(|e\rangle\langle e|=(I+\sigma_z)/2\). Every term of the Hamiltonian therefore commutes with \(N\).

Two excitations mean either an excited atom with one photon, \(|e,1\rangle\), or a ground-state atom with two photons, \(|g,2\rangle\). They span \(\mathcal H_1\).

5.3 Exercise 3 — Vacuum oscillations and entanglement

The atom starts in \(|e\rangle\) and the cavity in \(|0\rangle\), at resonance.

  1. Write \(|\psi(t)\rangle\) in the interaction picture.
  2. Find the first time when the excitation has been transferred completely to the field.
  3. Find the first time when the atom and field are maximally entangled.
  4. Calculate the reduced atomic density matrix at that time.
NoteSolution

Here \(n=0\) and \(\Omega_0=2g\). Removing the free evolution from Equation 5 gives

\[ |\psi(t)\rangle=\cos(gt)|e,0\rangle-i\sin(gt)|g,1\rangle. \]

Complete transfer requires \(\cos(gt)=0\). The first positive time is

\[ t_{\mathrm{transfer}}=\frac{\pi}{2g}. \]

At that time the state is \(-i|g,1\rangle\), a product state. Entanglement is instead maximal when the two coefficients have equal magnitude:

\[ \cos^2(gt)=\sin^2(gt)=\frac12, \qquad t_{\mathrm{ent}}=\frac{\pi}{4g}. \]

Then

\[ |\psi(t_{\mathrm{ent}})\rangle =\frac{|e,0\rangle-i|g,1\rangle}{\sqrt2}. \]

To trace out the field, use

\[ \operatorname{Tr}_c\bigl(|a,n\rangle\langle b,m|\bigr) =\langle m|n\rangle\,|a\rangle\langle b|. \]

The cross terms vanish because \(\langle0|1\rangle=0\), so

\[ \rho_a=\frac12|e\rangle\langle e|+\frac12|g\rangle\langle g| =\frac I2. \]

The total state is pure, while the atom is maximally mixed. Since the displayed two terms are already orthogonal product states with equal weights, they give maximal entanglement between the atom and this two-dimensional field subspace.

5.4 Exercise 4 — Detuning

Start from \(|e,n\rangle\) with arbitrary \(\Delta\).

  1. Find \(P_{g,n+1}(t)\).
  2. Find its maximum value and the first time it is reached.
  3. Evaluate these quantities for \(\Delta=2g\sqrt{n+1}\).
  4. Interpret the limit \(|\Delta|\gg g\sqrt{n+1}\).
NoteSolution

Squaring the second amplitude in Equation 5 gives

\[ P_{g,n+1}(t)=\frac{4g^2(n+1)}{\Omega_n^2} \sin^2\left(\frac{\Omega_nt}{2}\right). \]

The first maximum has \(\Omega_nt/2=\pi/2\), hence

\[ P_{g,n+1}^{\max}=\frac{4g^2(n+1)}{\Delta^2+4g^2(n+1)}, \qquad t_{\max}=\frac{\pi}{\Omega_n}. \]

For \(\Delta=2g\sqrt{n+1}\),

\[ \Omega_n=2\sqrt2\,g\sqrt{n+1}, \qquad P_{g,n+1}^{\max}=\frac12, \qquad t_{\max}=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt2\,g\sqrt{n+1}}. \]

For large detuning,

\[ P_{g,n+1}^{\max}\simeq\frac{4g^2(n+1)}{\Delta^2}\ll1. \]

Very little excitation is exchanged. The interaction still shifts the energies, as Exercise 6 shows.

5.5 Exercise 5 — Revival time

For an initially excited atom and a coherent field at resonance, the atomic inversion is

\[ W(t)=2P_e(t)-1=\sum_{n=0}^\infty p_n\cos(\omega_nt), \qquad \omega_n=2g\sqrt{n+1}. \]

Assume \(\bar n\gg1\).

  1. Expand \(\omega_n\) to first order about \(n=\bar n\).
  2. Require neighboring components to recover a phase difference of \(2\pi\) and estimate \(t_{\mathrm{rev}}\).
  3. Evaluate the large-\(\bar n\) estimate for \(\bar n=25\) and \(g/(2\pi)=50\ \mathrm{kHz}\).
  4. Explain why a single Fock state does not show collapse and revival.
NoteSolution

Since \(d\omega_n/dn=g/\sqrt{n+1}\),

\[ \omega_n\simeq2g\sqrt{\bar n+1} +\frac{g}{\sqrt{\bar n+1}}(n-\bar n). \]

Neighboring frequencies differ by approximately \(g/\sqrt{\bar n+1}\). Setting their phase difference to \(2\pi\) gives

\[ t_{\mathrm{rev}}\simeq\frac{2\pi\sqrt{\bar n+1}}g \simeq\frac{2\pi\sqrt{\bar n}}g. \]

Higher terms in the expansion prevent all the components from returning exactly to phase, so the revival is partial.

For the given values, \(g=2\pi\times50\times10^3\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}\) and \(\sqrt{\bar n}=5\). Thus

\[ t_{\mathrm{rev}}\simeq \frac{2\pi\times5}{2\pi\times50\times10^3} =10^{-4}\ \mathrm s=100\ \mu\mathrm s. \]

A single Fock state has only one Rabi frequency. Its oscillations are periodic, with no set of frequencies to lose and recover their relative phase.

5.6 Exercise 6 — The dispersive regime

Work within the Jaynes–Cummings model, with \(\Delta>0\) and \(g\sqrt{n+1}\ll\Delta\). Let \(H_0=H_a+H_c\).

  1. Expand \(\Omega_n\) through second order in \(g/\Delta\).

  2. Find the energy shifts of the states close to \(|e,n\rangle\) and \(|g,n+1\rangle\).

  3. Show that, to this order, the effective Hamiltonian in the approximately uncoupled basis is

    \[ H_{\mathrm{eff}}=H_0+\hbar\chi \left[(a^\dagger a+1)|e\rangle\langle e| -a^\dagger a|g\rangle\langle g|\right], \qquad\chi=\frac{g^2}{\Delta}. \]

  4. Find the atomic transition angular frequency when the field contains \(n\) photons.

NoteSolution

Expand the square root using \(\sqrt{1+x}=1+x/2+O(x^2)\):

\[ \Omega_n=\Delta\sqrt{1+\frac{4g^2(n+1)}{\Delta^2}} =\Delta+\frac{2g^2(n+1)}{\Delta} +O\!\left(\frac{g^4(n+1)^2}{\Delta^3}\right). \]

For \(\Delta>0\), the upper dressed state is close to \(|e,n\rangle\) and the lower one to \(|g,n+1\rangle\). Substituting the expansion into Equation 4 gives

\[ \delta E_{e,n}\simeq\frac{\hbar g^2(n+1)}{\Delta}, \qquad \delta E_{g,n+1}\simeq-\frac{\hbar g^2(n+1)}{\Delta}. \]

For a ground-state atom with \(n\) photons, relabel the second result:

\[ \delta E_{g,n}\simeq-\hbar\chi n, \qquad \delta E_{e,n}\simeq\hbar\chi(n+1). \]

The shift of \(|g,0\rangle\) is zero, as it must be for the uncoupled state. These shifts are reproduced by

\[ H_{\mathrm{eff}}=H_0+\hbar\chi \left[(a^\dagger a+1)|e\rangle\langle e| -a^\dagger a|g\rangle\langle g|\right]. \]

Using \(|e\rangle\langle e|=(I+\sigma_z)/2\) and \(|g\rangle\langle g|=(I-\sigma_z)/2\), the correction becomes

\[ \hbar\chi\left[\left(a^\dagger a+\frac12\right)\sigma_z+\frac12I\right]. \]

The last term is a common energy shift. For a fixed photon number, subtracting the two atomic energies gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \hbar\omega_a(n) &\simeq\hbar\omega_a+\delta E_{e,n}-\delta E_{g,n}\\ &=\hbar\omega_a+\hbar\chi(2n+1). \end{aligned} \]

Therefore \(\omega_a(n)\simeq\omega_a+\chi(2n+1)\). The photon number shifts the atomic frequency while excitation exchange remains weak. This is the basis of dispersive measurements.

5.7 Exercise 7 — Numerical diagonalization

Keep field states \(|0\rangle,\ldots,|N_{\max}\rangle\).

  1. Construct the matrices \(a\), \(a^\dagger\) and \(a^\dagger a\).
  2. Build \(H_{\mathrm{JC}}\) using Kronecker products.
  3. For \(\hbar=1\), \(\omega_a=\omega_c=1\) and \(g=0.08\), compare the lowest numerical eigenvalues with the analytical ones.
  4. Explain why the truncation leaves an artificial uncoupled state at the upper boundary.
NoteSolution

In the photon basis, \(a_{n-1,n}=\sqrt n\) and all other entries vanish. The creation matrix is its conjugate transpose.

The code uses the order \(\mathcal H_{\mathrm{field}}\otimes\mathcal H_{\mathrm{atom}}\), with atomic basis \((|g\rangle,|e\rangle)\). This only changes the ordering of basis vectors; the state \(|e,n\rangle\) is represented by \(|n\rangle\otimes|e\rangle\) in the code.

Show Python code 
import numpy as np

nmax = 6
omega_c = 1.0
omega_a = 1.0
g = 0.08

# Field operators.
a = np.zeros((nmax + 1, nmax + 1), dtype=complex)
for n in range(1, nmax + 1):
    a[n - 1, n] = np.sqrt(n)

adag = a.conj().T
number = adag @ a
identity_field = np.eye(nmax + 1)

# Atomic operators in the basis (|g>, |e>).
sigma_z = np.array([[-1, 0], [0, 1]], dtype=complex)
sigma_plus = np.array([[0, 0], [1, 0]], dtype=complex)
sigma_minus = sigma_plus.conj().T
identity_atom = np.eye(2)

H = (
    omega_c * np.kron(number, identity_atom)
    + 0.5 * omega_a * np.kron(identity_field, sigma_z)
    + g * (
        np.kron(a, sigma_plus)
        + np.kron(adag, sigma_minus)
    )
)

energies = np.linalg.eigvalsh(H)
print("Numerical:", np.round(energies[:7], 6))

# Analytical energies: |g,0>, then the doublets n = 0, 1, 2.
analytic = [-0.5]
for n in range(3):
    analytic.extend([
        (n + 0.5) - g * np.sqrt(n + 1),
        (n + 0.5) + g * np.sqrt(n + 1),
    ])

print("Analytical:", np.round(sorted(analytic), 6))
Numerical: [-0.5       0.42      0.58      1.386863  1.613137  2.361436  2.638564]
Analytical: [-0.5       0.42      0.58      1.386863  1.613137  2.361436  2.638564]

The isolated state has energy \(E_{g,0}=-1/2\). At resonance the doublets have

\[ E_{n,\pm}=n+\frac12\pm g\sqrt{n+1}. \]

The first pair is \(0.5\pm0.08\), giving \(0.42\) and \(0.58\). The next is \(1.5\pm0.08\sqrt2\), giving approximately \(1.3869\) and \(1.6131\). The numerical results agree with these values.

In the full space, \(|e,N_{\max}\rangle\) couples to \(|g,N_{\max}+1\rangle\). The latter is absent from the truncated basis, so the former is artificially uncoupled. With the parameters above it lies at the top of the numerical spectrum. It must be excluded from the doublet comparison; complete doublets with \(n<N_{\max}\) are represented exactly.

6 Further reading

Keeping the counter-rotating terms gives the quantum Rabi model. Cavity loss and emission into other modes require an open-system description, usually a master equation.

For a longer treatment of dressed states, Rabi oscillations and revivals, see Matteo Bina, The coherent interaction between matter and radiation: a tutorial on the Jaynes–Cummings model.
