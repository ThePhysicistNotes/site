Symmetries, groups and conservation laws

From Noether’s theorem to internal symmetries

Author

Mohammed Reguig

← Back to the course list

1 Why symmetries matter

A symmetry is a transformation that changes the mathematical description of a system without changing its physical laws. For example, an isolated experiment gives the same result if it is performed one hour later or if the whole apparatus is rotated. These two observations are related to the conservation of energy and angular momentum.

The relation between symmetry and conservation is not an extra rule added to mechanics. It follows from the action principle. This chapter first derives this connection, then introduces the group language used to describe continuous symmetries. The last sections explain how space-time symmetries define mass and spin, while internal symmetries organise electric charge, isospin and colour.

We use natural units,

\[ \hbar=c=1, \]

and the Minkowski metric

\[ \eta_{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1). \]

Greek indices label space-time components. Repeated indices are summed.

2 The action and field equations

For a mechanical system with coordinates \(q_i(t)\), the action is

\[ S[q_i]=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}L(q_i,\dot q_i,t)\,dt. \]

In field theory, the dynamical variables are fields \(\phi_a(x)\) defined at every point of space-time. The action becomes

\[ S[\phi]=\int d^4x\,\mathcal L(\phi_a,\partial_\mu\phi_a,x), \tag{1}\]

where \(\mathcal L\) is the Lagrangian density. Consider a small variation \(\phi_a\to\phi_a+\delta\phi_a\) which vanishes on the boundary. At first order,

\[ \delta S=\int d^4x\left[ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi_a}\delta\phi_a +\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)} \partial_\mu\delta\phi_a \right]. \]

Integrating the second term by parts gives

\[ \delta S=\int d^4x\left[ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi_a} -\partial_\mu\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)} \right]\delta\phi_a, \]

because the surface term vanishes. The variations are arbitrary inside the integration region, so a physical field satisfies

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi_a} -\partial_\mu\left[ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)} \right]=0.} \tag{2}\]

These are the Euler–Lagrange equations for fields.

For a real scalar field,

\[ \mathcal L=\frac12\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi -\frac12m^2\phi^2. \tag{3}\]

We have

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi}=-m^2\phi, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}=\partial^\mu\phi. \]

Equation Equation 2 therefore gives

\[ (\Box+m^2)\phi=0, \qquad \Box=\partial_\mu\partial^\mu. \tag{4}\]

The action, rather than the precise form of \(\mathcal L\), is the important object. Adding a total divergence,

\[ \mathcal L' = \mathcal L+\partial_\mu F^\mu, \]

changes the action only by a boundary term. Under the usual boundary conditions, \(\mathcal L\) and \(\mathcal L'\) give the same field equations.

3 Noether’s theorem

3.1 Internal transformations

Consider a continuous transformation which does not move the space-time point:

\[ \phi_a(x)\longrightarrow \phi'_a(x) =\phi_a(x)+\varepsilon R_a(\phi,x)+O(\varepsilon^2), \tag{5}\]

where \(\varepsilon\) is a small constant. The variation of the Lagrangian density can always be rearranged as

\[ \delta\mathcal L =\left[ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi_a} -\partial_\mu\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)} \right]\delta\phi_a +\partial_\mu\left[ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)}\delta\phi_a \right]. \tag{6}\]

On a solution of the field equations, the first term vanishes. If the transformation is a symmetry, the remaining variation is at most a total divergence,

\[ \delta\mathcal L=\varepsilon\,\partial_\mu K^\mu. \]

Using \(\delta\phi_a=\varepsilon R_a\) in Equation 6 gives

\[ \boxed{ j^\mu= \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)}R_a-K^\mu, \qquad \partial_\mu j^\mu=0.} \tag{7}\]

This is the first Noether theorem: every continuous global symmetry of the action produces a conserved current.

Writing the conservation equation as

\[ \partial_tj^0+\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=0 \]

and integrating over space gives

\[ \frac{dQ}{dt} =-\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf j\cdot d\mathbf S, \qquad Q=\int_V d^3\mathbf x\,j^0. \]

If the flux through the boundary vanishes, \(Q\) is conserved.

NoteEmmy Noether

In 1918, Emmy Noether showed that continuous symmetries of the action lead to conservation laws and that local symmetries lead to identities between field equations. Her result gave a common origin to conservation laws which had previously been treated separately.

3.2 Example: a complex scalar field

Let

\[ \mathcal L=\partial_\mu\phi^*\partial^\mu\phi-m^2\phi^*\phi. \tag{8}\]

It is invariant under the global phase transformation

\[ \phi\to e^{-iq\alpha}\phi, \qquad \phi^*\to e^{iq\alpha}\phi^*, \tag{9}\]

where \(\alpha\) is constant. At first order,

\[ \delta\phi=-iq\alpha\phi, \qquad \delta\phi^*=iq\alpha\phi^*. \]

Treating \(\phi\) and \(\phi^*\) as independent fields, Equation 7 gives

\[ \boxed{ j^\mu=iq\left( \phi^*\partial^\mu\phi-\phi\partial^\mu\phi^* \right).} \tag{10}\]

Its divergence is

\[ \partial_\mu j^\mu =iq(\phi^*\Box\phi-\phi\Box\phi^*). \]

The field equations \(\Box\phi=-m^2\phi\) and \(\Box\phi^*=-m^2\phi^*\) make this expression zero. After quantisation, the associated charge counts particles and antiparticles with opposite signs.

3.3 Translations and energy-momentum

Space-time transformations also produce Noether currents. Under a translation

\[ x^\mu\to x'^\mu=x^\mu+\varepsilon^\mu, \]

a scalar field varies at a fixed point as

\[ \delta\phi=-\varepsilon^\nu\partial_\nu\phi. \]

If \(\mathcal L\) has no explicit dependence on \(x\), the associated current contains the canonical energy-momentum tensor

\[ \boxed{ T^\mu{}_{\nu} =\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)} \partial_\nu\phi_a -\delta^\mu{}_{\nu}\mathcal L, \qquad \partial_\mu T^\mu{}_{\nu}=0.} \tag{11}\]

The conserved four-momentum is

\[ P_\nu=\int d^3\mathbf x\,T^0{}_{\nu}. \]

Time translations give energy conservation and spatial translations give momentum conservation. For the real scalar field in Equation 3,

\[ T^{00}=\frac12\dot\phi^2 +\frac12(\boldsymbol\nabla\phi)^2 +\frac12m^2\phi^2. \]

This expression is the energy density stored in the field.

An infinitesimal Lorentz transformation has the form

\[ \delta x^\mu=\omega^\mu{}_{\nu}x^\nu, \qquad \omega_{\mu\nu}=-\omega_{\nu\mu}. \]

The antisymmetric tensor \(\omega_{\mu\nu}\) contains three rotations and three boosts. The corresponding current is

\[ J^{\lambda\mu\nu} =x^\mu T^{\lambda\nu}-x^\nu T^{\lambda\mu} +S^{\lambda\mu\nu}. \tag{12}\]

The first two terms describe orbital angular momentum. The last term depends on how the field components transform and gives the intrinsic spin part. It vanishes for a scalar field.

3.4 Global and local symmetries

In a global symmetry, the transformation parameter is constant. The corresponding Noether charge generally acts on physical states. In a local symmetry, the parameter may depend on space-time. Such a transformation often expresses a redundancy in the description.

For electromagnetism,

\[ A_\mu\to A_\mu+\partial_\mu\alpha(x) \]

does not change

\[ F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\mu. \]

The local symmetry implies an identity between the equations of motion. If

\[ \mathcal E_A^\mu=\frac{\delta S}{\delta A_\mu}, \]

then gauge invariance gives

\[ 0=\delta S =\int d^4x\,\mathcal E_A^\mu\partial_\mu\alpha =-\int d^4x\,\alpha\,\partial_\mu\mathcal E_A^\mu. \]

Since \(\alpha(x)\) is arbitrary,

\[ \partial_\mu\mathcal E_A^\mu\equiv0. \tag{13}\]

This is a simple form of Noether’s second theorem. It does not provide one independent physical charge at every point; instead, it shows that some field equations are not independent.

4 Groups and Lie algebras

A set \(G\) is a group if it has an associative product, an identity, an inverse for every element and closure under the product. A symmetry transformation must be reversible, and two successive symmetry transformations must still be a symmetry. This is why groups appear naturally.

A Lie group is also a smooth manifold. Its elements near the identity can be written

\[ U(\boldsymbol\alpha) =\exp(-i\alpha^aT^a), \tag{14}\]

where the Hermitian operators \(T^a\) are generators. For small parameters,

\[ U(\boldsymbol\alpha)=1-i\alpha^aT^a+O(\alpha^2). \]

The generators satisfy commutation relations

\[ \boxed{[T^a,T^b]=if^{ab}{}_cT^c,} \tag{15}\]

where \(f^{ab}{}_c\) are the structure constants. They describe the Lie algebra of the group.

For an abelian group, all generators commute. For a non-abelian group, the order of two transformations matters. The Jacobi identity,

\[ [T^a,[T^b,T^c]]+[T^b,[T^c,T^a]]+[T^c,[T^a,T^b]]=0, \]

imposes a consistency condition on the structure constants.

NoteSophus Lie

Sophus Lie introduced continuous transformation groups in the nineteenth century while studying differential equations. The passage from a group to its generators turns a nonlinear composition law into the linear algebra of commutators.

4.1 Representations

An abstract group becomes useful in physics through a representation. A representation assigns a matrix or an operator \(D(g)\) to each group element such that

\[ D(g_1g_2)=D(g_1)D(g_2). \]

Different objects may transform in different representations of the same group. Under rotations, a scalar has one component, a vector has three components and a spinor has two complex components. The group is the same, but its representations are different.

A representation is reducible if its vector space contains a proper invariant subspace. Otherwise it is irreducible. Irreducible representations are the basic blocks used to classify states.

An operator \(C\) which commutes with every generator,

\[ [C,T^a]=0, \]

is called a Casimir operator. By Schur’s lemma, a Casimir is proportional to the identity inside an irreducible representation. Its eigenvalue can therefore label that representation.

For \(SU(2)\), the quadratic Casimir is

\[ \mathbf T^2=T_1^2+T_2^2+T_3^2, \]

with eigenvalue \(j(j+1)\) in the spin-\(j\) representation.

4.2 Tensor products

If one state transforms in \(R_1\) and another in \(R_2\), their combined state transforms in the tensor product \(R_1\otimes R_2\). This product is usually reducible. For two \(SU(2)\) doublets,

\[ \mathbf2\otimes\mathbf2=\mathbf3\oplus\mathbf1. \tag{16}\]

The singlet is invariant under the group. In quantum mechanics it is the antisymmetric combination

\[ |0,0\rangle =\frac{1}{\sqrt2} (|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle). \]

The three symmetric states form the triplet.

5 The Poincaré group

The Poincaré group contains translations and Lorentz transformations. Its generators are the four-momentum \(P^\mu\) and the antisymmetric Lorentz generators \(M^{\mu\nu}=-M^{\nu\mu}\). Their algebra is

\[ [P^\mu,P^\nu]=0, \]

\[ [M^{\mu\nu},P^\rho] =i(\eta^{\mu\rho}P^\nu-\eta^{\nu\rho}P^\mu), \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} [M^{\mu\nu},M^{\rho\sigma}] =i(&\eta^{\mu\rho}M^{\nu\sigma} -\eta^{\mu\sigma}M^{\nu\rho}\\ &-\eta^{\nu\rho}M^{\mu\sigma} +\eta^{\nu\sigma}M^{\mu\rho}). \end{aligned} \tag{17}\]

The first Casimir is

\[ P^2=P_\mu P^\mu. \]

For a one-particle state, its eigenvalue is \(m^2\). Mass is therefore not just a parameter in an equation: it labels an irreducible representation of the space-time symmetry group.

The second Casimir is built from the Pauli–Lubanski vector

\[ W^\mu=\frac12\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} P_\nu M_{\rho\sigma}. \tag{18}\]

For a massive particle, we may work in its rest frame, \(P^\mu=(m,\mathbf0)\). Then

\[ W^0=0, \qquad \mathbf W=m\mathbf J, \]

and

\[ W^2=-m^2s(s+1). \tag{19}\]

The states are labelled by mass \(m\) and spin \(s\), with \(2s+1\) spin projections.

For a massless particle there is no rest frame. Physical states are instead labelled by helicity,

\[ h=\frac{\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf p}{|\mathbf p|}. \]

A photon has two physical helicities, \(h=+1\) and \(h=-1\). This does not mean that a four-vector field has only two components. Field components, constraints, gauge freedom and physical particle states are different things.

NoteWigner’s classification

Eugene Wigner classified elementary particles through the unitary irreducible representations of the Poincaré group. In this view, mass and spin are the invariant labels of a particle, not properties added after the theory has been built.

6 Internal symmetries

Internal symmetries act on field components without moving the space-time point. They organise particles into multiplets and determine which interactions are allowed.

6.1 The group \(U(1)\)

\(U(1)\) contains complex phases

\[ U(\alpha)=e^{-iq\alpha}. \]

It is one-dimensional and abelian. A field of charge \(q\) transforms as in Equation 9. Products with zero total charge are invariant. For example, \(\phi^*\phi\) is invariant, while \(\phi^2\) is not invariant unless \(q=0\).

If the phase becomes local, \(\alpha\to\alpha(x)\), an ordinary derivative produces an extra term:

\[ \partial_\mu\phi \to e^{-iq\alpha} (\partial_\mu\phi-iq\,\partial_\mu\alpha\,\phi). \]

Introduce a gauge field \(A_\mu\) and the covariant derivative

\[ D_\mu=\partial_\mu+iqA_\mu. \]

If

\[ A_\mu\to A_\mu+\partial_\mu\alpha, \]

then

\[ D_\mu\phi\to e^{-iq\alpha}D_\mu\phi. \]

The kinetic term \(|D_\mu\phi|^2\) is locally invariant. The interaction with the gauge field is therefore required by local phase invariance.

6.2 The group \(SU(2)\)

\(SU(2)\) is the group of unitary \(2\times2\) matrices with determinant one. In its fundamental representation, the generators are

\[ T^a=\frac{\sigma^a}{2}, \]

where \(\sigma^a\) are the Pauli matrices. They satisfy

\[ [T^a,T^b]=i\varepsilon^{abc}T^c. \tag{20}\]

A doublet

\[ \Psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_1\\\psi_2\end{pmatrix} \]

transforms as

\[ \Psi\to U\Psi, \qquad U=\exp\left(-i\alpha^a\frac{\sigma^a}{2}\right). \]

Because \(U^\dagger U=1\), the quantity \(\Psi^\dagger\Psi\) is invariant. The irreducible representations are labelled by \(j=0,\frac12,1,\ldots\) and have dimension \(2j+1\).

Historically, the proton and neutron were treated as an approximate isospin doublet,

\[ N=\begin{pmatrix}p\\n\end{pmatrix}. \]

The strong interaction is nearly invariant under rotations in this internal space. Electromagnetic effects and the difference between the up- and down-quark masses break the symmetry, which is why the proton and neutron masses are close but not equal.

6.3 The group \(SU(3)\)

\(SU(3)\) is the group of unitary \(3\times3\) matrices with determinant one. It has eight generators,

\[ T^a=\frac{\lambda^a}{2}, \qquad a=1,\ldots,8, \]

where \(\lambda^a\) are the Gell-Mann matrices. Their commutators are

\[ [T^a,T^b]=if^{abc}T^c. \]

Quark colours form the fundamental triplet,

\[ q=\begin{pmatrix}q_r\\q_g\\q_b\end{pmatrix}. \]

The eight gluons transform in the adjoint representation, which also has dimension eight. The tensor products

\[ \mathbf3\otimes\overline{\mathbf3} =\mathbf1\oplus\mathbf8, \]

and

\[ \mathbf3\otimes\mathbf3\otimes\mathbf3 =\mathbf1\oplus\mathbf8\oplus\mathbf8\oplus\mathbf{10} \]

show how colour singlets can be formed from a quark-antiquark pair or from three quarks. Observed hadrons are colour singlets.

For a local non-abelian symmetry, the covariant derivative is

\[ D_\mu=\partial_\mu+igA_\mu^aT^a. \]

The field strength is

\[ F_{\mu\nu}^a =\partial_\mu A_\nu^a-\partial_\nu A_\mu^a -gf^{abc}A_\mu^bA_\nu^c. \tag{21}\]

The last term is absent in electromagnetism. It produces direct interactions between gauge bosons. This difference follows from the non-commuting generators, not from an extra choice made later.

7 Exact, broken and anomalous symmetries

An exact symmetry leaves the action invariant and is also respected by the physical state under consideration. Its charge is conserved and states can be arranged into exact multiplets.

An explicit breaking occurs when a term in the Lagrangian is not invariant. The current then satisfies

\[ \partial_\mu j^\mu=\Delta, \]

where \(\Delta\) measures the breaking. If \(\Delta\) is small, the symmetry remains a useful approximation.

In spontaneous breaking, the action is symmetric but the lowest-energy state is not. Consider

\[ V(\phi)=-\mu^2|\phi|^2+\lambda|\phi|^4, \qquad \mu^2,\lambda>0. \]

The minima satisfy

\[ |\phi|=\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}. \]

All phases have the same energy, but a particular vacuum chooses one phase. For a global continuous symmetry this produces a massless Goldstone mode. For a gauge symmetry, the corresponding degree of freedom becomes part of a massive gauge field through the Higgs mechanism.

Finally, an anomaly is a symmetry of the classical action which is not preserved after quantisation. Anomalies of global symmetries can have physical effects. An anomaly in a gauge symmetry would make the theory inconsistent, so the particle content must make gauge anomalies cancel.

These cases should not be confused: explicit breaking changes the action, spontaneous breaking changes the vacuum, and an anomaly appears during quantisation.

8 Exercises

8.1 Exercise 1 — Klein–Gordon equation

Starting from Equation 3, derive the Euler–Lagrange equation. Then write it explicitly in terms of time and spatial derivatives.

NoteSolution

The derivatives of the Lagrangian density are

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi}=-m^2\phi, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}=\partial^\mu\phi. \]

Therefore

\[ (\Box+m^2)\phi=0. \]

With the chosen metric, \(\Box=\partial_t^2-\nabla^2\), so

\[ \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial t^2}-\nabla^2\phi+m^2\phi=0. \]

8.2 Exercise 2 — A shift symmetry

Consider a real massless scalar field,

\[ \mathcal L=\frac12\partial_\mu\phi\partial^\mu\phi. \]

Show that it is invariant under \(\phi\to\phi+\varepsilon\), find the Noether current and explain what changes when a mass term is added.

NoteSolution

The Lagrangian contains only derivatives, so a constant shift does not change it. Here \(R=1\) and \(K^\mu=0\). Thus

\[ j^\mu=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)} =\partial^\mu\phi. \]

Its conservation, \(\partial_\mu j^\mu=\Box\phi=0\), is the field equation. A mass term \(-m^2\phi^2/2\) changes under the shift and explicitly breaks the symmetry.

8.3 Exercise 3 — Current of a complex field

Derive Equation 10 from the global transformation Equation 9. Verify its conservation directly with the field equations.

NoteSolution

The infinitesimal variations are

\[ \frac{\delta\phi}{\alpha}=-iq\phi, \qquad \frac{\delta\phi^*}{\alpha}=iq\phi^*. \]

Noether’s formula gives

\[ j^\mu =\partial^\mu\phi^*(-iq\phi) +\partial^\mu\phi(iq\phi^*) =iq(\phi^*\partial^\mu\phi-\phi\partial^\mu\phi^*). \]

Taking the divergence gives

\[ \partial_\mu j^\mu=iq(\phi^*\Box\phi-\phi\Box\phi^*)=0, \]

because both fields satisfy the Klein–Gordon equation.

8.4 Exercise 4 — Energy of a scalar field

Use Equation 11 to calculate \(T^{00}\) for the real scalar field. Why is each contribution to the total energy non-negative?

NoteSolution

For this field,

\[ T^{\mu\nu}=\partial^\mu\phi\partial^\nu\phi -\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathcal L. \]

Since

\[ \mathcal L=\frac12\dot\phi^2 -\frac12(\boldsymbol\nabla\phi)^2-\frac12m^2\phi^2, \]

we obtain

\[ T^{00}=\frac12\dot\phi^2 +\frac12(\boldsymbol\nabla\phi)^2+\frac12m^2\phi^2. \]

For real \(\phi\) and \(m^2\geq0\), every term is a square multiplied by a non-negative coefficient.

8.5 Exercise 5 — The Lie algebra of \(SU(2)\)

Using the Pauli matrices, verify

\[ \left[\frac{\sigma_i}{2},\frac{\sigma_j}{2}\right] =i\varepsilon_{ijk}\frac{\sigma_k}{2}. \]

Then calculate the quadratic Casimir in the fundamental representation.

NoteSolution

The Pauli matrices obey

\[ [\sigma_i,\sigma_j]=2i\varepsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k. \]

Dividing by four gives the required relation. Since \(\sigma_i^2=I\),

\[ \mathbf T^2=\sum_{i=1}^3\frac{\sigma_i^2}{4} =\frac34I. \]

This is \(j(j+1)\) with \(j=1/2\).

8.6 Exercise 6 — Two spin-\(1/2\) states

Show that the singlet state in Equation 16 is annihilated by the total generators

\[ T_i^{\mathrm{tot}}=T_i\otimes I+I\otimes T_i. \]

Why does this prove that the state is invariant under \(SU(2)\)?

NoteSolution

For \(T_3^{\mathrm{tot}}\), the two terms in the singlet each have total magnetic number zero. For the raising operator,

\[ T_+^{\mathrm{tot}}|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle =|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle, \qquad T_+^{\mathrm{tot}}|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle =|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle, \]

so the difference is annihilated. The same holds for \(T_-^{\mathrm{tot}}\). All three generators therefore vanish on the singlet. Exponentiating them leaves the state unchanged.

8.7 Exercise 7 — Abelian and non-abelian transformations

Take two infinitesimal transformations

\[ U_a=1-i\alpha T^a, \qquad U_b=1-i\beta T^b. \]

Calculate \(U_aU_b-U_bU_a\) to the first non-zero order. Interpret the result for \(U(1)\) and \(SU(2)\).

NoteSolution

Terms linear in the parameters cancel, leaving

\[ U_aU_b-U_bU_a=-\alpha\beta[T^a,T^b]+O(\alpha^2,\beta^2). \]

\(U(1)\) has one generator, so the commutator vanishes. In \(SU(2)\), different generators do not commute; successive rotations around different internal axes depend on their order.

8.8 Exercise 8 — Mass and spin from Poincaré symmetry

For a massive particle in its rest frame, use Equation 18 to show that \(W^0=0\) and \(\mathbf W=m\mathbf J\). Deduce Equation 19.

NoteSolution

With \(P^\mu=(m,\mathbf0)\), the time component contains only spatial components of \(P\) and is zero. The spatial components reduce to

\[ W^i=\frac m2\varepsilon^{i0jk}M_{jk}=mJ^i. \]

Therefore

\[ W^2=-\mathbf W^2=-m^2\mathbf J^2. \]

In the spin-\(s\) representation, \(\mathbf J^2=s(s+1)\), hence

\[ W^2=-m^2s(s+1). \]

8.9 Exercise 9 — Selection rule

Let \(Q\) be a conserved charge with \([Q,S]=0\), where \(S\) is the scattering operator. If

\[ Q|i\rangle=q_i|i\rangle, \qquad Q|f\rangle=q_f|f\rangle, \]

show that \((q_f-q_i)\langle f|S|i\rangle=0\).

NoteSolution

Insert the commutator between the two states:

\[ 0=\langle f|[Q,S]|i\rangle =(q_f-q_i)\langle f|S|i\rangle. \]

If \(q_f\neq q_i\), the transition amplitude must vanish. Conservation laws therefore appear as selection rules.

8.10 Exercise 10 — Building \(U(1)\) invariants

A complex field transforms as \(\phi\to e^{-iq\alpha}\phi\). Decide which expressions are invariant: \(\phi\), \(\phi^2\), \(\phi^*\phi\), \((\phi^*\phi)^2\) and \(\phi^*\partial_\mu\phi\).

NoteSolution

\(\phi\) has charge \(q\) and \(\phi^2\) has charge \(2q\), so neither is invariant for \(q\neq0\). The products \(\phi^*\phi\) and \((\phi^*\phi)^2\) have total charge zero and are invariant. For a global transformation, \(\phi^*\partial_\mu\phi\) is also invariant because the phase is constant. Under a local transformation, it gains an extra term and must be replaced by \(\phi^*D_\mu\phi\).

8.11 Exercise 11 — Colour singlets

Under \(SU(3)\), a quark transforms as \(q_i\to U_i{}^j q_j\) and an antiquark as \(\bar q^i\to\bar q^j(U^\dagger)_j{}^i\). Show that \(\bar q^iq_i\) is a singlet. Then explain why \(\varepsilon^{ijk}q_iq_jq_k\) is also invariant.

NoteSolution

For the quark-antiquark combination,

\[ \bar q^i q_i \to \bar q^j(U^\dagger)_j{}^iU_i{}^kq_k =\bar q^jq_j. \]

For three quarks,

\[ \varepsilon^{ijk}U_i{}^aU_j{}^bU_k{}^c =(\det U)\varepsilon^{abc}. \]

Since \(\det U=1\) in \(SU(3)\), the antisymmetric three-quark combination is invariant.

8.12 Exercise 12 — Explicit or spontaneous breaking?

For a complex scalar field, compare the two potentials

\[ V_1=m^2|\phi|^2+\lambda|\phi|^4+\epsilon(\phi+\phi^*) \]

and

\[ V_2=-\mu^2|\phi|^2+\lambda|\phi|^4, \]

with positive \(\lambda\), \(\mu^2\) and non-zero \(\epsilon\). Identify the type of symmetry breaking in each case.

NoteSolution

The term \(\epsilon(\phi+\phi^*)\) changes under a phase rotation, so \(V_1\) explicitly breaks \(U(1)\). The potential \(V_2\) is invariant, but its minima have non-zero magnitude and arbitrary phase. Choosing one minimum breaks the symmetry spontaneously.

9 Problems

9.1 Problem 1 — Noether theorem in an internal plane

Consider two real scalar fields with

\[ \mathcal L =\frac12\partial_\mu\phi_1\partial^\mu\phi_1 +\frac12\partial_\mu\phi_2\partial^\mu\phi_2 -\frac12m^2(\phi_1^2+\phi_2^2). \]

  1. Show that the action is invariant under rotations of \((\phi_1,\phi_2)\).
  2. Find the infinitesimal variations.
  3. Derive the conserved current.
  4. Define \(\Phi=(\phi_1+i\phi_2)/\sqrt2\) and relate the result to the \(U(1)\) current.
NoteSolution

The finite transformation is

\[ \begin{pmatrix}\phi_1'\\\phi_2'\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix} \cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\ \sin\alpha&\cos\alpha \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\phi_1\\\phi_2\end{pmatrix}. \]

Both \(\phi_1^2+\phi_2^2\) and the corresponding kinetic term are invariant. At first order,

\[ \delta\phi_1=-\alpha\phi_2, \qquad \delta\phi_2=\alpha\phi_1. \]

Dividing out \(\alpha\), Noether’s formula gives

\[ j^\mu =-\partial^\mu\phi_1\,\phi_2 +\partial^\mu\phi_2\,\phi_1. \]

With \(\Phi=(\phi_1+i\phi_2)/\sqrt2\), the rotation becomes \(\Phi\to e^{i\alpha}\Phi\). Apart from the sign convention chosen for the charge, the current is

\[ j^\mu=i(\Phi^*\partial^\mu\Phi-\Phi\partial^\mu\Phi^*). \]

9.2 Problem 2 — From a global phase to an interaction

Let a complex scalar field transform locally as

\[ \phi(x)\to e^{-iq\alpha(x)}\phi(x). \]

  1. Show why \(\partial_\mu\phi\) does not transform like \(\phi\).
  2. Find the transformation of \(A_\mu\) that makes \(D_\mu\phi=(\partial_\mu+iqA_\mu)\phi\) covariant.
  3. Expand \((D_\mu\phi)^*D^\mu\phi\) and identify the interaction terms.
  4. Explain why the electromagnetic field strength is invariant.
NoteSolution

Differentiating the transformed field gives

\[ \partial_\mu\phi' =e^{-iq\alpha} (\partial_\mu\phi-iq\,\partial_\mu\alpha\,\phi). \]

The extra term is cancelled if

\[ A_\mu' = A_\mu+\partial_\mu\alpha. \]

Then \(D_\mu'\phi'=e^{-iq\alpha}D_\mu\phi\). Expanding the kinetic term gives

\[ \begin{aligned} (D_\mu\phi)^*D^\mu\phi ={}&\partial_\mu\phi^*\partial^\mu\phi +iqA^\mu(\phi\partial_\mu\phi^*-\phi^*\partial_\mu\phi)\\ &+q^2A_\mu A^\mu\phi^*\phi. \end{aligned} \]

The last two terms describe interactions. Finally,

\[ F_{\mu\nu}' =\partial_\mu A_\nu'-\partial_\nu A_\mu' =F_{\mu\nu}, \]

because mixed derivatives commute.

9.3 Problem 3 — Why non-abelian gauge bosons interact

Take

\[ D_\mu=\partial_\mu+igA_\mu, \qquad A_\mu=A_\mu^aT^a. \]

  1. Calculate \([D_\mu,D_\nu]\).
  2. Show that it can be written as \(igF_{\mu\nu}\).
  3. Recover Equation 21.
  4. Explain the physical difference with \(U(1)\).
NoteSolution

Acting on a field,

\[ [D_\mu,D_\nu] =ig(\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\mu) -g^2[A_\mu,A_\nu]. \]

Using

\[ [A_\mu,A_\nu] =A_\mu^aA_\nu^b[T^a,T^b] =if^{abc}A_\mu^aA_\nu^bT^c, \]

we find

\[ [D_\mu,D_\nu]=igF_{\mu\nu}^aT^a, \]

with

\[ F_{\mu\nu}^a =\partial_\mu A_\nu^a-\partial_\nu A_\mu^a -gf^{abc}A_\mu^bA_\nu^c, \]

which agrees with Equation 21. For \(U(1)\), the generators commute and the last term vanishes. In a non-abelian theory it remains, so the gauge field itself carries the group charge and interacts with other gauge fields.
