Riemannian geometry

Metrics, connections, geodesics and curvature

Author

Mohammed Reguig

1 Manifolds and tangent spaces

A smooth manifold of dimension \(n\) is a space which locally looks like \(\mathbb R^n\). A coordinate chart is a map

\[ x=(x^1,\ldots,x^n):U\subset M\longrightarrow x(U)\subset\mathbb R^n \]

whose transition maps are smooth. Coordinates are only local labels. A coordinate singularity does not necessarily mean that the geometry itself is singular.

At a point \(p\), the tangent space \(T_pM\) is an \(n\)-dimensional vector space. In a chart its natural basis is

\[ \partial_i=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}. \]

The dual basis of the cotangent space \(T_p^*M\) is denoted by \(dx^i\) and satisfies

\[ dx^i(\partial_j)=\delta^i{}_j. \]

A vector and a one-form are written

\[ X=X^i\partial_i, \qquad \alpha=\alpha_i\,dx^i. \]

Their pairing, \(\alpha(X)=\alpha_iX^i\), is independent of the chosen coordinates.

2 The Riemannian metric

A Riemannian metric assigns a scalar product to every tangent space. In coordinates,

\[ g=g_{ij}\,dx^i\otimes dx^j, \qquad g_{ij}=g_{ji}, \]

and the matrix \((g_{ij})\) is positive definite. Its inverse satisfies

\[ g^{ik}g_{kj}=\delta^i{}_j. \]

The line element is

\[ ds^2=g_{ij}\,dx^i dx^j. \]

For a curve \(\gamma(t)\), its length and energy are

\[ L(\gamma)=\int_a^b \sqrt{g_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j}\,dt, \]

\[ E(\gamma)=\frac12\int_a^b g_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j\,dt. \]

The Riemannian volume form on an oriented manifold is

\[ dV_g=\sqrt{|g|}\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n, \qquad |g|=\det(g_{ij}). \tag{1}\]

For example, the Euclidean plane in polar coordinates has

\[ ds^2=dr^2+r^2d\theta^2. \]

The sphere of radius \(R\) has

\[ ds^2=R^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2), \]

and the Poincaré half-plane has

\[ ds^2=\frac{dx^2+dy^2}{y^2}, \qquad y>0. \]

2.1 Gradient, divergence and Laplacian

The gradient of a function \(f\) is defined by

\[ g(\operatorname{grad}f,X)=df(X). \]

Therefore

\[ \operatorname{grad}f =g^{ij}(\partial_jf)\partial_i. \]

The divergence of a vector field and the Laplace–Beltrami operator are

\[ \operatorname{div}X =\frac1{\sqrt{|g|}} \partial_i\left(\sqrt{|g|}X^i\right), \tag{2}\]

\[ \boxed{ \Delta_gf =\frac1{\sqrt{|g|}} \partial_i\left(\sqrt{|g|}g^{ij}\partial_jf\right).} \tag{3}\]

With this sign convention, \(\Delta=\sum_i\partial_i^2\) in Euclidean space.

3 The Levi–Civita connection

Vectors based at different points belong to different vector spaces. A connection gives a rule for differentiating a vector field in the direction of another vector field. A Riemannian metric determines one connection which is torsion-free and compatible with the metric. It is called the Levi–Civita connection.

In coordinates,

\[ \nabla_{\partial_i}\partial_j =\Gamma^k{}_{ij}\partial_k, \]

where

\[ \boxed{ \Gamma^k{}_{ij} =\frac12g^{k\ell} \left( \partial_i g_{j\ell} +\partial_j g_{i\ell} -\partial_\ell g_{ij} \right).} \tag{4}\]

Christoffel symbols depend on the coordinates and are not tensor components. They can be non-zero in a flat space when a curved coordinate system is used.

Along a curve, a vector field \(V=V^k\partial_k\) has covariant derivative

\[ \frac{DV^k}{dt} =\dot V^k+\Gamma^k{}_{ij}\dot x^iV^j. \tag{5}\]

\(V\) is parallel when \(DV/dt=0\). Metric compatibility then implies that scalar products between parallel fields remain constant.

4 Geodesics

A geodesic has a velocity vector which is parallel along the curve:

\[ \frac{D\dot\gamma}{dt}=0. \]

In coordinates,

\[ \boxed{ \ddot x^k+\Gamma^k{}_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j=0.} \tag{6}\]

An affine parameter gives constant speed. Geodesics are stationary points of the energy functional and locally minimize length over sufficiently short intervals.

The exponential map is defined by

\[ \exp_p(v)=\gamma_v(1), \]

where \(\gamma_v(0)=p\) and \(\dot\gamma_v(0)=v\). A connected manifold is geodesically complete if every geodesic can be extended for all real parameter values. The Hopf–Rinow theorem relates this property to completeness of the metric distance.

5 Curvature

Our sign convention for the Riemann tensor is

\[ R(X,Y)Z =\nabla_X\nabla_YZ-\nabla_Y\nabla_XZ-\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z. \]

Its coordinate components are

\[ R^\ell{}_{kij} =\partial_i\Gamma^\ell{}_{jk} -\partial_j\Gamma^\ell{}_{ik} +\Gamma^m{}_{jk}\Gamma^\ell{}_{im} -\Gamma^m{}_{ik}\Gamma^\ell{}_{jm}. \tag{7}\]

For two independent vectors \(X,Y\), the sectional curvature is

\[ K(X,Y) =\frac{g(R(X,Y)Y,X)} {g(X,X)g(Y,Y)-g(X,Y)^2}. \]

The Ricci tensor and scalar curvature are contractions of the Riemann tensor:

\[ \operatorname{Ric}_{ij}=R^k{}_{ikj}, \qquad S=g^{ij}\operatorname{Ric}_{ij}. \]

In dimension two, the Riemann tensor is completely determined by the Gaussian curvature \(K\), and

\[ S=2K. \]

Euclidean space has \(K=0\), a sphere of radius \(R\) has \(K=1/R^2\), and hyperbolic space of radius \(R\) has \(K=-1/R^2\).

NoteRiemann’s idea of intrinsic geometry

In 1854, Bernhard Riemann proposed that geometry could be defined from measurements made inside a space, without referring to an external Euclidean space. The metric and its curvature make this idea precise: inhabitants of a surface can detect its curvature using distances, angles and geodesics alone.

6 Coordinate exercises

The stars indicate the difficulty. Each solution is folded so that the question can be attempted first.

6.1 Exercise 1 — Polar coordinates

Let

\[ F(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta), \qquad r>0. \]

Find the Jacobian, the natural basis and the Euclidean metric. Explain why polar coordinates fail at \(r=0\) and why the angular interval must be restricted.

NoteSolution

The Jacobian matrix and its determinant are

\[ DF=\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&r\cos\theta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det DF=r. \]

The basis vectors are

\[ e_r=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta), \qquad e_\theta=(-r\sin\theta,r\cos\theta). \]

They are orthogonal, with norms \(1\) and \(r\), so

\[ ds^2=dr^2+r^2d\theta^2. \]

At \(r=0\), the Jacobian vanishes and every angle represents the same point. For \(r>0\), \(\theta\) and \(\theta+2\pi\) also represent the same point, so one chooses an open angular interval and removes a ray if needed.

6.2 Exercise 2 — Cylindrical coordinates

In \(\mathbb R^3\), let

\[ F(\rho,\varphi,z)=(\rho\cos\varphi,\rho\sin\varphi,z). \]

Describe the coordinate surfaces and calculate the natural basis, metric and volume element.

NoteSolution

\(\rho=\rho_0\) gives a cylinder, \(\varphi=\varphi_0\) a half-plane and \(z=z_0\) a horizontal plane. The basis is

\[ e_\rho=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi,0), \quad e_\varphi=(-\rho\sin\varphi,\rho\cos\varphi,0), \quad e_z=(0,0,1). \]

Thus

\[ ds^2=d\rho^2+\rho^2d\varphi^2+dz^2, \qquad dV=\rho\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,dz. \]

6.3 Exercise 3 — Parabolic coordinates

Consider

\[ x=\frac12(u^2-v^2), \qquad y=uv. \]

Show that the coordinate curves are parabolas. Find the Jacobian and metric, then express \(u^2\) and \(v^2\) in terms of \(x,y\).

NoteSolution

For fixed \(u=u_0\),

\[ x=\frac{u_0^2}{2}-\frac{y^2}{2u_0^2}, \]

and a similar calculation for fixed \(v\) gives the second family of parabolas. The basis vectors are

\[ e_u=(u,v), \qquad e_v=(-v,u). \]

Their determinant is \(u^2+v^2\), and they are orthogonal. Therefore

\[ ds^2=(u^2+v^2)(du^2+dv^2). \]

If \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), then

\[ u^2=r+x, \qquad v^2=r-x. \]

The signs must still be chosen so that \(uv=y\); the inverse is only local.

6.4 Exercise 4 — A helix

The curve

\[ \gamma(t)=(\cos t,\sin t,t), \qquad 0\leq t\leq2\pi, \]

is given in Cartesian coordinates. Express it in cylindrical and spherical coordinates, then calculate its length.

NoteSolution

In cylindrical coordinates,

\[ \rho=1, \qquad \varphi=t, \qquad z=t. \]

In spherical coordinates with colatitude \(\theta\),

\[ r=\sqrt{1+t^2}, \qquad \varphi=t, \qquad \theta=\arccos\frac{t}{\sqrt{1+t^2}}. \]

Since \(\dot\gamma=(-\sin t,\cos t,1)\), its speed is \(\sqrt2\) and

\[ L=2\pi\sqrt2. \]

6.5 Exercise 5 — Stereographic projection

Project the unit sphere without its north pole onto the plane \(z=0\). Derive the direct and inverse maps, then find the spherical metric in the projected coordinates \((X,Y)\).

NoteSolution

The line through the north pole and \((x,y,z)\) gives

\[ X=\frac{x}{1-z}, \qquad Y=\frac{y}{1-z}. \]

With \(R^2=X^2+Y^2\), the inverse is

\[ x=\frac{2X}{1+R^2}, \quad y=\frac{2Y}{1+R^2}, \quad z=\frac{R^2-1}{1+R^2}. \]

Differentiation gives

\[ g=\frac{4}{(1+X^2+Y^2)^2}(dX^2+dY^2). \]

The projection is conformal: it preserves angles but not lengths. Two such charts, from opposite poles, cover the sphere.

7 Metric and measure exercises

7.1 Exercise 6 — A metric with a cross term

For

\[ ds^2=2(du^2+du\,dv+dv^2), \]

find \((g_{ij})\) and its inverse. Use \(x=(u+v)/\sqrt2\), \(y=(u-v)/\sqrt2\) to diagonalize the metric.

NoteSolution

Because the cross term is \(2g_{uv}du\,dv\),

\[ (g_{ij})=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad (g^{ij})=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}. \]

The coordinates are not orthogonal. Substitution gives

\[ ds^2=3dx^2+dy^2. \]

With \(X=\sqrt3x\) and \(Y=y\), this becomes \(ds^2=dX^2+dY^2\).

7.2 Exercise 7 — Metric and area of a sphere

For

\[ F(\theta,\varphi) =R(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\cos\theta), \]

calculate the induced metric, area element and total area.

NoteSolution

The tangent vectors are orthogonal and have norms \(R\) and \(R\sin\theta\). Hence

\[ ds^2=R^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2), \]

\[ dA=R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi. \]

Integrating over \(0\leq\theta\leq\pi\) and \(0\leq\varphi<2\pi\) gives

\[ \operatorname{Area}(S_R^2)=4\pi R^2. \]

7.3 Exercise 8 — Geometry of a torus

For \(R>a>0\), consider

\[ F(\theta,\varphi) =((R+a\cos\theta)\cos\varphi, (R+a\cos\theta)\sin\varphi,a\sin\theta). \]

Find the induced metric, surface area and enclosed volume.

NoteSolution

The tangent vectors are orthogonal, with norms \(a\) and \(R+a\cos\theta\). Thus

\[ ds^2=a^2d\theta^2+(R+a\cos\theta)^2d\varphi^2, \]

\[ dA=a(R+a\cos\theta)\,d\theta\,d\varphi, \qquad A=4\pi^2Ra. \]

Replacing \(a\) by \(0\leq\rho\leq a\) gives

\[ dV=\rho(R+\rho\cos\theta)\,d\rho\,d\theta\,d\varphi, \]

and therefore

\[ V=2\pi^2Ra^2. \]

7.4 Exercise 9 — Conformal change of metric

Let

\[ g=e^{2\sigma(x,y)}(dx^2+dy^2). \]

Find the inverse metric and area element. Show that angles are unchanged and determine the local scale factors for lengths and areas.

NoteSolution

We have

\[ g_{ij}=e^{2\sigma}\delta_{ij}, \qquad g^{ij}=e^{-2\sigma}\delta^{ij}, \qquad dA_g=e^{2\sigma}dx\,dy. \]

The factor \(e^{2\sigma}\) cancels in the cosine formula for an angle. Infinitesimal lengths are multiplied by \(e^\sigma\) and areas by \(e^{2\sigma}\).

7.5 Exercise 10 — Radial metrics

For

\[ g=dr^2+f(r)^2d\theta^2, \]

find the circumference of \(r=r_0\) and the area of \(0\leq r\leq r_0\). Apply the result to \(f(r)=r\), \(R\sin(r/R)\) and \(R\sinh(r/R)\).

NoteSolution

Directly from the metric,

\[ L(r_0)=2\pi f(r_0), \qquad A(r_0)=2\pi\int_0^{r_0}f(r)\,dr. \]

For the plane,

\[ L=2\pi r_0, \qquad A=\pi r_0^2. \]

For the sphere,

\[ L=2\pi R\sin(r_0/R), \quad A=2\pi R^2[1-\cos(r_0/R)]. \]

For the hyperbolic plane,

\[ L=2\pi R\sinh(r_0/R), \quad A=2\pi R^2[\cosh(r_0/R)-1]. \]

7.6 Exercise 11 — Mercator projection

On a sphere, use latitude \(\lambda\) and longitude \(\varphi\):

\[ g=R^2(d\lambda^2+\cos^2\lambda\,d\varphi^2). \]

Set \(x=\varphi\) and find \(y(\lambda)\) such that the metric is conformal to \(dx^2+dy^2\).

NoteSolution

Equal coefficients require

\[ \frac{dy}{d\lambda}=\frac1{\cos\lambda}. \]

Hence one may choose

\[ y=\log\tan\left(\frac\pi4+\frac\lambda2\right). \]

Since \(\cos\lambda=\operatorname{sech}y\),

\[ g=R^2\operatorname{sech}^2y\,(dx^2+dy^2). \]

The map preserves angles, but distances and areas are distorted, and the poles are sent to \(y=\pm\infty\).

8 Differential-operator exercises

8.1 Exercise 12 — Gradient in polar coordinates

For the Euclidean polar metric and

\[ f(r,\theta)=r^2\cos(2\theta), \]

find \(\operatorname{grad}f\) and its squared norm. Check the answer in Cartesian coordinates.

NoteSolution

Using \(g^{ij}=\operatorname{diag}(1,r^{-2})\),

\[ \operatorname{grad}f =2r\cos(2\theta)\partial_r -2\sin(2\theta)\partial_\theta. \]

Since \(f=x^2-y^2\), the Cartesian result is \(2x\partial_x-2y\partial_y\). Finally,

\[ \|\operatorname{grad}f\|^2=4r^2. \]

8.2 Exercise 13 — Divergence and flux

On the punctured plane, let \(X=r\partial_r\). Find its divergence and verify the divergence theorem on the disk \(D_R\). Generalize to \(X_p=r^p\partial_r\).

NoteSolution

Since \(\sqrt{|g|}=r\),

\[ \operatorname{div}X=\frac1r\partial_r(r^2)=2. \]

Thus

\[ \int_{D_R}\operatorname{div}X\,dA=2\pi R^2. \]

On the boundary, \(n=\partial_r\) and \(ds=R\,d\theta\), so the flux is also \(2\pi R^2\). More generally,

\[ \operatorname{div}(r^p\partial_r)=(p+1)r^{p-1}. \]

8.3 Exercise 14 — Laplacian on the sphere

Derive the Laplace–Beltrami operator on \(S_R^2\) and apply it to \(f=\cos\theta\).

NoteSolution

Using Equation 3,

\[ \Delta f =\frac1{R^2\sin\theta} \partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta f) +\frac1{R^2\sin^2\theta}\partial_\varphi^2f. \]

For \(f=\cos\theta\),

\[ \Delta f=-\frac2{R^2}f. \]

Since \(f=z/R\), it is the restriction of a Cartesian coordinate and is a spherical harmonic of degree one.

8.4 Exercise 15 — Radial harmonic functions

For \(u(x)=U(r)\) in \(\mathbb R^n\setminus\{0\}\), show that

\[ \Delta u=U''+\frac{n-1}{r}U'. \]

Solve \(\Delta u=0\) for \(n=2\) and \(n\geq3\).

NoteSolution

The radial part of the Euclidean volume contains \(r^{n-1}\), hence

\[ \Delta u=\frac1{r^{n-1}}\frac d{dr} \left(r^{n-1}U'\right). \]

Therefore \(r^{n-1}U'=C\). The solutions are

\[ U=A+B\log r \quad(n=2), \]

and

\[ U=A+Br^{2-n} \quad(n\geq3). \]

The non-constant solutions are singular at the origin and are not smooth harmonic functions on all of \(\mathbb R^n\).

8.5 Exercise 16 — Green’s identity

Let \((M,g)\) be compact, connected and without boundary. Prove

\[ \int_Mf\Delta f\,dV_g =-\int_M\|\operatorname{grad}f\|^2dV_g, \]

and deduce that every harmonic function on \(M\) is constant.

NoteSolution

The product rule gives

\[ \operatorname{div}(f\operatorname{grad}f) =\|\operatorname{grad}f\|^2+f\Delta f. \]

Its integral is zero because \(M\) has no boundary. This proves the identity. If \(\Delta f=0\), then the integral of \(\|\operatorname{grad}f\|^2\) vanishes, so \(\operatorname{grad}f=0\). Connectedness makes \(f\) constant.

With a boundary, one adds

\[ \int_{\partial M}f\,\partial_nf\,dA_g. \]

9 Connection exercises

9.1 Exercise 17 — Christoffel symbols in polar coordinates

Find all non-zero Christoffel symbols for

\[ g=dr^2+r^2d\theta^2. \]

Write the covariant derivatives of the coordinate basis and explain why the plane can still be flat.

NoteSolution

The only non-zero metric derivative is \(\partial_rg_{\theta\theta}=2r\). Thus

\[ \Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}=-r, \qquad \Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta} =\Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta r}=\frac1r. \]

Therefore

\[ \nabla_{\partial_r}\partial_\theta =\nabla_{\partial_\theta}\partial_r =\frac1r\partial_\theta, \qquad \nabla_{\partial_\theta}\partial_\theta=-r\partial_r. \]

These coefficients describe the variation of the polar basis. Curvature is tensorial and remains zero.

9.2 Exercise 18 — Christoffel symbols on the sphere

For the spherical metric, find the non-zero Christoffel symbols. Determine which regular curves with constant \(\theta\) are geodesics.

NoteSolution

The non-zero symbols are

\[ \Gamma^\theta{}_{\varphi\varphi} =-\sin\theta\cos\theta, \]

\[ \Gamma^\varphi{}_{\theta\varphi} =\Gamma^\varphi{}_{\varphi\theta}=\cot\theta. \]

For constant \(\theta=\theta_0\), the first geodesic equation requires

\[ \sin\theta_0\cos\theta_0=0. \]

The polar cases are degenerate, so the only regular parallel which is a geodesic is the equator, \(\theta_0=\pi/2\).

9.3 Exercise 19 — A conformal metric

For \(g_{ij}=e^{2\sigma}\delta_{ij}\), show that

\[ \Gamma^k{}_{ij} =\delta^k{}_j\partial_i\sigma +\delta^k{}_i\partial_j\sigma -\delta_{ij}\delta^{k\ell}\partial_\ell\sigma. \]

Apply this formula to the Poincaré half-plane.

NoteSolution

Insert \(g^{ij}=e^{-2\sigma}\delta^{ij}\) and

\[ \partial_i g_{j\ell} =2e^{2\sigma}(\partial_i\sigma)\delta_{j\ell} \]

into Equation 4. For \(\sigma=-\log y\), the non-zero symbols are

\[ \Gamma^x{}_{xy}=\Gamma^x{}_{yx}=-\frac1y, \]

\[ \Gamma^y{}_{xx}=\frac1y, \qquad \Gamma^y{}_{yy}=-\frac1y. \]

9.4 Exercise 20 — Uniqueness of the Levi–Civita connection

Let \(\nabla\) and \(\widetilde\nabla\) be torsion-free and compatible with the same metric. Set

\[ A(X,Y)=\nabla_XY-\widetilde\nabla_XY, \qquad B(X,Y,Z)=g(A(X,Y),Z). \]

Prove that the two connections are equal.

NoteSolution

The difference of two connections is tensorial. The torsion-free condition gives

\[ B(X,Y,Z)=B(Y,X,Z), \]

while metric compatibility gives

\[ B(X,Y,Z)=-B(X,Z,Y). \]

Applying these two symmetries repeatedly yields

\[ B(X,Y,Z)=-B(X,Y,Z), \]

so \(B=0\). Since the metric is non-degenerate, \(A=0\) and

\[ \nabla=\widetilde\nabla. \]

9.5 Exercise 21 — Christoffel symbols are not a tensor

Compare the Euclidean connection in Cartesian and polar coordinates. Use the transformation law to identify the non-tensorial term.

NoteSolution

The Cartesian Christoffel symbols vanish, while in polar coordinates

\[ \Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}=-r, \qquad \Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}=\frac1r. \]

A tensor which vanishes in one chart vanishes in every chart. The connection law contains the additional term

\[ \frac{\partial y^a}{\partial x^k} \frac{\partial^2x^k}{\partial y^b\partial y^c}, \]

which prevents tensoriality. This term cancels in the difference between two connections.

9.6 Exercise 22 — Contracted Christoffel symbol

Show that

\[ \Gamma^i{}_{ik}=\partial_k\log\sqrt{|g|}, \]

and deduce the coordinate formula for the divergence.

NoteSolution

Contracting Equation 4 and exchanging dummy indices gives

\[ \Gamma^i{}_{ik}=\frac12g^{i\ell}\partial_k g_{i\ell}. \]

Jacobi’s determinant formula gives

\[ \partial_k\log|g|=g^{i\ell}\partial_k g_{i\ell}. \]

Hence

\[ \Gamma^i{}_{ik}=\partial_k\log\sqrt{|g|}. \]

Using \(\nabla_iX^i=\partial_iX^i+\Gamma^i{}_{ik}X^k\) gives Equation 2.

10 Geodesic exercises

10.1 Exercise 23 — Plane geodesics in polar coordinates

Derive the polar geodesic equations, show that \(r^2\dot\theta\) is constant, and decide whether radial lines and circles are geodesics.

NoteSolution

Using Exercise 17 in Equation 6 gives

\[ \ddot r-r\dot\theta^2=0, \qquad \ddot\theta+\frac2r\dot r\dot\theta=0. \]

The second equation is equivalent to

\[ \frac d{dt}(r^2\dot\theta)=0. \]

If \(\theta\) is constant, \(\ddot r=0\), so radial lines at constant speed are geodesics. If \(r=r_0>0\), the first equation forces \(\dot\theta=0\); non-trivial circles are not geodesics.

10.2 Exercise 24 — Geodesics of the sphere

Let \(\gamma(t)\in S_R^2\) be a constant-speed geodesic. Show that

\[ \ddot\gamma=-\frac{v^2}{R^2}\gamma \]

and deduce that non-constant geodesics are great circles.

NoteSolution

The tangential part of \(\ddot\gamma\) vanishes, so \(\ddot\gamma\) is parallel to the normal vector \(\gamma\). Differentiating \(\gamma\cdot\gamma=R^2\) twice gives

\[ v^2+\gamma\cdot\ddot\gamma=0. \]

Thus the stated equation follows. If \(p=\gamma(0)\) and \(w=\dot\gamma(0)\),

\[ \gamma(t)= \cos\left(\frac{vt}{R}\right)p +\frac Rv\sin\left(\frac{vt}{R}\right)w. \]

The curve stays in the plane spanned by \(p\) and \(w\), whose intersection with the sphere is a great circle.

10.3 Exercise 25 — Hyperbolic geodesics

For

\[ g=\frac{dx^2+dy^2}{y^2}, \]

find the unit-speed geodesics and show that the boundary \(y=0\) is infinitely far away.

NoteSolution

The geodesic Lagrangian is

\[ L=\frac{\dot x^2+\dot y^2}{2y^2}. \]

Since \(x\) is cyclic,

\[ \frac{\dot x}{y^2}=c. \]

Unit speed gives \(\dot x^2+\dot y^2=y^2\). If \(c=0\), the geodesics are vertical lines. Otherwise integration gives

\[ (x-a)^2+y^2=\frac1{c^2}. \]

They are semicircles orthogonal to the boundary. Along a vertical line,

\[ \int_\varepsilon^{y_0}\frac{dy}{y} =\log\frac{y_0}{\varepsilon}\to\infty, \]

so the boundary lies at infinite distance.

10.4 Exercise 26 — Energy and length

For a regular curve \(\gamma:[a,b]\to M\), prove

\[ L(\gamma)^2\leq2(b-a)E(\gamma), \]

and determine the equality case.

NoteSolution

Let \(v(t)=\|\dot\gamma(t)\|\). Cauchy–Schwarz gives

\[ \left(\int_a^bv\,dt\right)^2 \leq(b-a)\int_a^bv^2dt =2(b-a)E. \]

Equality holds exactly when the speed is constant. Length is unchanged by an increasing reparametrization, while energy usually changes because it contains the square of the speed.

10.5 Exercise 27 — Clairaut’s relation

A surface of revolution has metric

\[ g=du^2+f(u)^2dv^2. \]

For a unit-speed geodesic, show that

\[ f(u)\sin\alpha=\text{constant}, \]

where \(\alpha\) is the angle with a meridian.

NoteSolution

The coordinate \(v\) is cyclic in

\[ L=\frac12(\dot u^2+f(u)^2\dot v^2), \]

so \(f(u)^2\dot v=C\). In the orthonormal basis

\[ e_u=\partial_u, \qquad e_v=\frac1{f(u)}\partial_v, \]

the azimuthal component of the unit velocity is \(f(u)\dot v=\sin\alpha\). Hence \(f(u)\sin\alpha=C\).

11 Curvature exercises

11.1 Exercise 28 — The polar plane is flat

Use the polar Christoffel symbols to calculate \(R^r{}_{\theta r\theta}\) and deduce the Gaussian curvature.

NoteSolution

From Equation 7,

\[ R^r{}_{\theta r\theta} =\partial_r(-r) -\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta} =-1-\frac1r(-r)=0. \]

In dimension two, the Riemann tensor is determined by \(K\), so

\[ K=0. \]

The non-zero Christoffel symbols came only from the polar coordinates.

11.2 Exercise 29 — Curvature of conformal metrics

For

\[ g=e^{2\sigma}(dx^2+dy^2), \]

use

\[ K=-e^{-2\sigma}(\partial_x^2\sigma+\partial_y^2\sigma) \]

to find the curvature of the stereographic unit-sphere metric and the Poincaré half-plane.

NoteSolution

For the sphere,

\[ \sigma=\log2-\log(1+r^2), \qquad \Delta\sigma=-\frac4{(1+r^2)^2}. \]

Since \(e^{-2\sigma}=(1+r^2)^2/4\), we obtain \(K=1\).

For the half-plane,

\[ \sigma=-\log y, \qquad \Delta\sigma=\frac1{y^2}, \qquad e^{-2\sigma}=y^2, \]

so \(K=-1\).

11.3 Exercise 30 — Constant-curvature spaces

Suppose an \(n\)-dimensional manifold satisfies

\[ R(X,Y)Z =K[g(Y,Z)X-g(X,Z)Y]. \]

Find the Ricci tensor and scalar curvature, then apply the result to \(S_R^n\).

NoteSolution

In an orthonormal basis \((e_a)\),

\[ \operatorname{Ric}(Y,Z) =\sum_a g(R(e_a,Y)Z,e_a). \]

Substitution gives \(nKg(Y,Z)-Kg(Y,Z)\), hence

\[ \operatorname{Ric}=(n-1)Kg, \qquad S=n(n-1)K. \]

For \(S_R^n\), \(K=1/R^2\), so

\[ \operatorname{Ric}=\frac{n-1}{R^2}g, \qquad S=\frac{n(n-1)}{R^2}. \]

11.4 Exercise 31 — Gauss–Bonnet theorem

For a compact oriented surface without boundary,

\[ \int_MK\,dA=2\pi\chi(M). \]

Check the theorem on a sphere, find the integral for a torus and discuss the possible sign of curvature for a surface of genus \(q\).

NoteSolution

On \(S_R^2\),

\[ \int K\,dA=\frac1{R^2}(4\pi R^2)=4\pi =2\pi\chi(S^2). \]

For the torus, \(\chi=0\), so \(\int K\,dA=0\). For an orientable surface of genus \(q\),

\[ \int_MK\,dA=4\pi(1-q). \]

Everywhere positive curvature is possible only when \(q=0\). Everywhere negative curvature requires \(q\geq2\). A torus cannot have curvature of one strict sign everywhere.

11.5 Exercise 32 — Jacobi fields

Along a unit-speed geodesic in a constant-curvature space, let \(E\) be parallel, unit and normal to \(\dot\gamma\). Set \(J=fE\) and solve

\[ \frac{D^2J}{dt^2}+R(J,\dot\gamma)\dot\gamma=0 \]

with \(f(0)=0\), \(f'(0)=1\) for \(K=1/R^2\), \(0\) and \(-1/R^2\).

NoteSolution

Since \(E\) is parallel and normal to \(\dot\gamma\),

\[ f''+Kf=0. \]

The three solutions are

\[ f(t)=R\sin(t/R) \quad(K=1/R^2), \]

\[ f(t)=t \quad(K=0), \]

and

\[ f(t)=R\sinh(t/R) \quad(K=-1/R^2). \]

Positive curvature can refocus geodesics, flat geometry gives linear separation, and negative curvature makes nearby geodesics diverge rapidly.
